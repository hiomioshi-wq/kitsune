PART ONE

In the mountain towns, the first snow closes the high passes, and the post goes on anyway.

That is the rule. Nobody remembers who made it. The postmaster at Hollow Pine says it is because people need letters most when they cannot reach each other any other way. The old women in the bathhouse say it is because winter words are heavier, and only a courier's satchel is strong enough to carry them. Kitsu, who carries the letters, has never had an opinion about it. She was born in the dark, and she prefers it, and she likes the snow, because it makes the lantern light go further.

She came back over the ridge into Hollow Pine on the first night of winter, with snow on her ears and the parcel from the lighthouse in her satchel, under the flap that says ALL LETTERS ARRIVE.

The parcel was wrapped in oilcloth. On the front, in large, careful letters, a lighthouse keeper named Oyuki had written a single word.

BROTHER.

Kitsu had carried it for three weeks, west from the sea, up through the foothills, into the first cold. She had not looked at it much. Couriers do not. But every night, when she held it to the lantern to check the way, the ink of the address had gone a little fainter.

She knew what that meant. She had hoped she was wrong.



PART TWO

The stationer's shop at Hollow Pine sat at the bottom of the hill by the frozen millpond, a narrow building with a green door and a window full of paper: spring paper, pale as cherry blossom, that the shop was famous for, and winter paper, thick and grey, for letters that needed to keep out the cold.

The light was on. Kitsu knocked.

The door was opened by a young badger in an apron covered in ink, with spectacles pushed up on his head and a pen behind each ear. He looked at Kitsu, at her red scarf that her grandmother had knitted too long, at the satchel, and his whiskers went still.

"You're the night courier," he said.

"I have a parcel for the stationer."

"I'm the stationer now." He stepped aside to let her in out of the snow. "Tobin. I was his apprentice. Mr. Haru died. In the autumn. The week after the cherry leaves came down."

Kitsu stood in the warm little shop, among the shelves of paper and the jars of ink and the smell of glue and woodsmoke, and she took out the parcel.

BROTHER.

The ink was almost gone now. You had to tilt it to the lantern to read it at all.

"It's from his sister," she said. "At the lighthouse by the sea. She got his letter. The one he sent in spring. She sent this back."

Tobin sat down very suddenly on a stool.

"He wrote to her?" he said. "He did it? He actually sent it?" He laughed, a little wet laugh. "He talked about writing that letter for six years. Every spring he'd take out a sheet of the good paper and sit there with the pen over it and then put it back. I didn't think he ever—"

He stopped. He looked at the parcel in Kitsu's paws.

"He never knew," he said. "Did he. He never knew if she got it."

"The post is stubborn," Kitsu said. "But it is not fast."



PART THREE

There is a rule about letters to the dead. Every courier learns it in her first winter.

You do not burn them. You do not return them. You do not leave them on a grave, because the rain gets at them, and the words wash out, and that is worse than never sending them at all.

You hold them up to the lantern, and you watch where the ink goes dark.

Because a person who has died is not at a place anymore. But they are still in people. And a letter to someone who has died is delivered to whoever is carrying them now.

Kitsu lifted her lantern. She had made it herself, long ago, from the paper of a letter that had come back marked UNKNOWN, and when you held an envelope up to its light, the ink of the address went faint where the person used to be, and dark where the person was now.

She held up the parcel.

BROTHER went faint. Fainter. Almost gone.

And then, under it, very slowly, as if someone were writing it with a warm finger on a cold window, another word came up in the oilcloth. Dark and clear.

APPRENTICE.

Tobin stared at it.

"That's me," he said.

"Then it's yours," said Kitsu, and put it in his paws.



PART FOUR

Inside the parcel was a stack of paper.

Not spring paper. Not winter paper. A third kind, that neither of them had ever seen. It was blue-white, and when you held it to the light it showed a watermark of a lighthouse, leaning a little to the left.

And on top of it, a note, in the same large, careful hand.

Dear Brother,

I have been making paper. Did you know a lighthouse keeper has a great deal of time in the winter? I found the old press in the cellar that Father used for the logbooks. I have been practicing since your letter came. This is my best batch.

I would like to trade. Ten sheets of mine for ten sheets of your spring paper. Lighthouse for cherry blossom. And then you write to me on mine, and I will write to you on yours, and we will each have a little piece of the other one's place in our hands every time we have something to say.

I have forty years of things to say. I hope you have the paper.

Your sister, who is asking,
Oyuki

Tobin read it three times. Then he took his spectacles off and wiped them on his apron for a long time, longer than spectacles need to be wiped.

"He would have loved this," he said. "He would have stood here and held it up to the window and told me all about the fibers. He'd have been insufferable about it."

"Write to her," said Kitsu.

"She doesn't know me."

"She knows him," said Kitsu. "You know him. That's most of a letter already."

Tobin looked at her. Then he went to the shelf, and took down ten sheets of the good spring paper, pale as cherry blossom, and wrapped them in oilcloth, and sat down at his master's desk with a pen from behind his ear.

He wrote until the lantern burned low. Kitsu did not read it. Couriers do not. But she saw that it was long, and that he laughed sometimes while he wrote it, and cried once, and that at the end he signed it carefully, the way you sign something you mean.

On the front he wrote: KEEPER. THE LIGHTHOUSE AT THE END OF THE SPIT.

"Is that enough of an address?" he asked.

Kitsu held it to the lantern. The ink went dark and sure.

"It's plenty," she said.



PART FIVE

The snow got worse that week. The postmaster closed the high passes with a chain across the road and a sign that said NO POST BEYOND THIS POINT UNTIL THAW, and the other couriers went home to their dens and their fires and their families, and only Kitsu stayed out, because the lighthouse letter was in her satchel, and the post goes on anyway.

She was sorting her satchel in the back room of the post office on the fourth night, by lantern light, when she found an envelope she had not put there.

It was small. Grey winter paper. No stamp. Tucked down at the very bottom of the satchel, under the flap, as if it had been there a long time, waiting for her to notice.

On the front, in a hand she knew better than her own, it said:

KITSU.

She sat very still.

Couriers do not get letters. That is not a rule. It is just how it is. The post goes through them, not to them. Kitsu had carried thousands of letters in her life, from every town in the mountains to every town beyond, and not one, not ever, had her name on it.

She held it up to the lantern, out of habit.

The ink did not go faint. It went dark. Darker. So dark it almost glowed.

Whoever wrote it was alive. And they were thinking about her right now.



PART SIX

She opened it. She had never opened a letter before. Her paws were clumsy at it.

Little one,

The snow is in. The pass will close tomorrow. I know you will be out in it, because you were born in the dark and you prefer it, and because the post goes on anyway, and you have always been your mother's daughter and my granddaughter both, which means stubborn twice over.

I have been knitting.

You remember that the scarf was too long. You told me when you were small that it dragged on the ground and got burrs in it, and I told you to grow into it, and you did. But I never told you why it was too long.

It was supposed to be two scarves. One for you, and one for me, from the same ball of red wool, so that when you were out on the night roads and I was up here at the top of the pass by the fire, we would each have a little piece of the other one around our necks.

I ran out of time before I could cut it. So I gave you all of it. I thought: she will be colder than me.

I have found more of the same wool. I have been knitting the other half. It is finished.

Come home for the winter post, little one. Just one night. I am getting old. I would like to see you wear it, and I would like you to see me wear mine.

The pass is closed. I know. But you have never cared about that.

Your grandmother,
who has always been waiting at the top of the road

There was no address on the back. There didn't need to be. Kitsu knew exactly where the top of the road was.



PART SEVEN

She went that night.

She took the lighthouse letter first. That was the job. She walked it down to the low road and gave it to the old badger who drives the winter sledge to the sea, and watched him go, and only then did she turn uphill, toward the chain across the pass, and the sign, and the dark.

She stepped over the chain.

The snow was up to her chest by the first bend. By the second, the wind was so strong she had to walk with her shoulder into it, and her lantern swung on its pole so hard that the light went everywhere at once, up into the falling snow, down into the drifts, out into the black where the mountain dropped away.

She held the envelope up to it every hundred steps. KITSU. Dark and sure. Still waiting.

At the third bend she met a snow hare, white on white, almost invisible, sitting very still in the lee of a rock.

"The pass is closed," said the hare.

"I know," said Kitsu.

"There's nothing up there but one house."

"I know," said Kitsu.

The hare looked at her red scarf, which was still too long, and dragging in the snow behind her, collecting little balls of ice.

"Ah," said the hare. "You're the granddaughter. She's been talking about you to the birds all autumn."

And it hopped up and ran ahead of her, white on white, showing her where the drifts were shallow, all the way to the top.



PART EIGHT

The house at the top of the road is very small and very old, with a roof weighed down with stones so the wind can't take it, and one window, and in the window, every night of Kitsu's life, a light.

It was on.

She didn't knock. You don't knock at your grandmother's.

Inside it was warm, and smelled of pine smoke and barley tea and wool, and by the fire in her chair sat a very old fox, grey all over, grey to the tips of her ears, with spectacles on her nose and a scarf around her neck.

A red scarf. Exactly the same red. Not too long. Just right.

"There you are," said her grandmother, as if Kitsu had stepped out for a minute and not been gone a year. "You're letting the snow in. Shut the door. Come here. Let me see."

Kitsu shut the door. She came and knelt by the chair. Her grandmother reached out with two old paws and took the end of Kitsu's scarf, the long end, the end that had dragged on the ground for years and gathered burrs and ice and the dust of every road in the mountains, and she held it next to the end of her own.

The same red. The same wool. The same stitch, the little slanting one that was hers alone.

"There," said her grandmother. "Two halves."

Kitsu found that she could not say anything at all. It was the first time in her life she had nothing to deliver.

So she just put her head down in her grandmother's lap, the way she had when she was very small, and her grandmother put one old grey paw on her ears, and they sat like that by the fire while the snow came down outside and closed the pass completely.



PART NINE

She stayed the whole winter, in the end. The pass doesn't open until the thaw, and nobody can make it, and anyway, for once, there was nowhere else she needed to be.

In the mornings she split wood. In the afternoons her grandmother taught her the little slanting stitch, and she was bad at it, and they laughed. In the evenings they drank barley tea and listened to the wind.

And every night, out of habit, Kitsu lit her lantern and held it up to the window.

Not to see the way. To see the other one.

Far below, down in Hollow Pine, in a narrow shop by the frozen millpond, a light was on in the window, where a badger in an inky apron sat up late at a desk, writing on blue-white paper with a leaning lighthouse in it.

And far beyond that, past the foothills, past the long cold miles, on a black rock at the end of a spit of sand, a great lamp turned and turned over the sea, where an old crane in a wrong-buttoned cardigan sat in her kitchen, writing on paper pale as cherry blossom.

And between them, on the low road, a winter sledge was carrying a letter through the snow.

Lights in windows. All the way down the mountain. All the way to the sea.

In the spring, when the pass opened, Kitsu went back to the night roads with her satchel and her lantern and her red scarf, which was not too long anymore. It was the right length now. It just felt different, knowing where the other half was.

The flap of her satchel still says ALL LETTERS ARRIVE.

She has decided that it's true. Even the ones that take forty years. Even the ones addressed to people who aren't there. Even the ones nobody ever expected to get.

Especially those.

THE END
