THE LIBRARY UNDER THE LAKE

A Kitsune Original. Text released under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0.


PART ONE

When they flooded the valley to make the reservoir, they moved the school, the noodle shop, the shrine and most of the graves. They did not move the library, because the library had been closed for eleven years, and because the man from the water company said that books were cheaper to replace than to carry.

So the library went under with the rest of the old village: the long wooden reading room, the card catalogue with its brass handles, the stove, the clock, and nine thousand, four hundred and twelve books, give or take the ones that had never been returned.

Mina had one of those.

It was a book about knots. She had borrowed it when she was nine, the summer before the library closed, and she had meant to bring it back, and then her family moved to the city, and the book came with them in a box marked KITCHEN, and for twenty years it sat on one shelf or another, its due date stamped on a card in the back pocket in faded purple ink: 14 AUGUST.

She found it again the week her grandmother died, packing up the city apartment. She sat on the floor among the boxes and read the whole thing, every knot, the clove hitch and the bowline and the one called the thief knot, which looks like a reef knot but comes undone if you are not the one who tied it. And then she did something she could not afterwards explain. She took the train back to the valley, and walked up to the edge of the reservoir, and stood looking at the flat grey water under which the library lay.

"I'm sorry," she said. "It's very overdue."


PART TWO

The water rippled, although there was no wind.

Then, about ten metres out, something rose to the surface: a small wooden box with a slot in the top, bobbing gently, as patient as a duck. On the side, in white paint, were the words BOOK RETURNS.

Mina looked around. The path along the dam was empty. A heron stood in the shallows on one leg and did not seem surprised.

She took off her shoes. She waded in up to her knees, then her thighs, gasping at the cold, holding the book above her head. When she reached the box, she saw that the slot was exactly the right size. She pushed the book through. It made a soft, satisfied thump, the sound she remembered from being nine.

The box sank.

Mina stood in the reservoir with her skirt soaked and felt, for the first time in a month, light. She waded back to shore and put on her shoes, and was halfway down the path before she heard a voice behind her, thin and old and slightly echoing, as though it came from inside a large wooden room.

"There is," the voice said, "the matter of the fine."


PART THREE

The librarian was a carp.

He rose up out of the water at the reservoir's edge, a great old orange-and-white fish wearing a pair of half-moon spectacles that should not, by any reasonable account, have stayed on his face. He held a ledger in his fins.

"Twenty years, two months and nine days," the librarian said. "At the old rate of one yen per day, that would be seven thousand, three hundred and seventy yen. However."

"However?" said Mina.

"However, the library has been closed. Therefore no fines have accrued since the closing date. Therefore you owe only for the days between your due date and the closing." He consulted the ledger. "Sixteen days. Sixteen yen."

Mina found sixteen yen in her coat pocket, mostly in one-yen coins. She held them out. The carp shook his head.

"We don't take money any more," he said. "Where would we spend it? No. The library accepts payment in kind."

"What kind?"

The carp looked at her over his spectacles for a long moment. "You read the book," he said. "Before you returned it. I could tell. The pages smell read."

"I read it," Mina admitted. "All of it. Last night."

"Then you know a knot," said the carp. "Tie one for me."


PART FOUR

That was how Mina became the library's first new borrower in thirty-one years.

The knot she tied was a bowline, in a bit of string from her coat. The carp accepted it gravely and took it down below, and when he came back up he was carrying, in his mouth, very carefully, a second book. It was dry. It was a book about the stars.

"Due in three weeks," the carp said, as clearly as a fish can with a book in its mouth. "Bring it back, and you may take another. If you are late, you will owe us a constellation."

She brought it back on time. The next book was about bread. The one after that was a collection of ghost stories from the old valley, written by a schoolteacher who had died before the flood, and in the margins, in pencil, someone had corrected the names of all the places.

Over the years, she learned that she was not the only one. A retired engineer from the water company came on Thursdays, and paid his late fees in apologies, which the carp said were worth more than knots but harder to get. A boy from the new school borrowed a book about volcanoes every single time and had to be talked out of it. An old badger who sold umbrellas brought back a dictionary he had borrowed in his youth and paid with one umbrella, which the carp hung over the circulation desk, deep in the dark water, where it did not seem to do any good at all.

"Libraries are not about the books," the carp told Mina once, when she asked why he stayed down there alone with nine thousand, four hundred and twelve volumes and a slowly rusting clock. "They are about the coming back. A book on a shelf is a promise that somebody will return. I am simply waiting to see the promises kept."


PART FIVE

When Mina grew old herself, she moved back to the valley, to a small house above the reservoir, and on clear days she could see, very faintly, the roof of the reading room down in the green water.

On the morning she found the knot book again, in a box her own granddaughter was packing, she laughed until she cried.

It had a new card in the back pocket. The due date was stamped in faded purple ink, and it was today.
