Incarnate: Sponsor
A spinoff of the Corruption Saga. Set during Relapse.

Trigger warning: This story contains addiction, enabling, war crimes, the breeding of monsters, the death of a young man, a cover-up, adult language, and a narrator who has decided that some people are tools.
Read at your own risk.



Chapter One: Hi, I'm Marguerite

Hi, I'm Marguerite, and I've been clean for two years, four months and eleven days.

That one's true. I want to start with something true, because there's not going to be a lot of it.

I run the meeting at the laundromat on Fifth. Tuesday and Friday nights. Folding chairs in a circle in front of the dead machines. Styrofoam cups. Coffee I make out of chicory and burnt barley because there's no real coffee left in the restored world, or if there is, nobody's selling it to people like us.

People like us. The Reformed. That's what the pamphlets the Council hands out call us. People who used to be corrupted and aren't anymore. Who did terrible things with red eyes and now have to live with brown ones. We have scars where the corruption came out. Mine go up both arms like somebody poured fire on me and it decided to stay. We're not allowed to live in the nice parts of the new cities. We're not allowed to hold certain jobs. We're allowed to sit in a laundromat on Fifth and drink chicory and say our names out loud.

So that's what I do. Twice a week. I sit in the circle and I say the opening words and I nod at people when they share and I say "Thank you for sharing" and I never, ever share myself.

"When I'm ready," I tell them.

They think it's because it's too painful.

It's because if they knew, they'd kill me in my folding chair. And honestly, they'd be right.



Chapter Two: Kennel Master

I was a captain under Tombstone. They know that much. Everybody knows that much. It's in my file.

What's not in my file is what I was captain of.

The hounds.

I bred them. I designed them. Before the corruption, before everything, I was a dog trainer. That's the joke. I had a little business out of my house, obedience classes, agility, a sign in the yard with a cartoon beagle on it. I could get anything to sit. I could get a three-hundred-pound mastiff to roll over by looking at it. When the corruption came for me, it found that. It liked that. It made that into something.

Tombstone gave me the residue from the corrupted that fell in the first battles. Black glass. He said: make me something that hunts it. Something that can smell where the corruption's been, and where it's going, and get there first.

So I did. I made the hounds out of the glass. Too many joints. Mouths that open sideways. Coats like oil. They smell the corruption the way a shark smells blood, from miles away, and they go to it, and they eat whatever's carrying it.

They were beautiful. I'm sorry. They were. Every one of them came out of my hands. I named the first litter. Bishop. Clover. Tess. I'd sit with them in the kennels at night and they'd put their huge glassy heads in my lap.

When Tombstone fell, I didn't. I ran. And they kept going without me, the way he did, nobody to call them home.

And they kept breeding. I made them able to. That was my best work. That's the thing nobody knows. Every pack out there in the ruins, every one that comes through a window, is my grandkids.



Chapter Three: Ena

I knew who she was the first night she came in.

Everybody knows. Shining blonde hair, even cut short and dirty. The prophecy girl. The one who ended the first world and most of the second. The anti-god of destruction, they called her, back when she was Corrupt. She sat down in the farthest folding chair with her arms crossed and her hood up and she said, "Hi. I'm Ena. I don't know how many days."

And I looked at her and thought: there it is.

Not "there's a girl who needs help." I've been doing this two years. I know what that looks like. It looks like Dov, nineteen, skinny, picking at the scars on his wrists, desperate for somebody to tell him he's not garbage.

Ena didn't look like that. Ena looked like a loaded gun somebody had left on a table in a room full of children.

And I've been watching the packs grow.

That's the other thing nobody knows. I keep a map. In my apartment. On the wall. Every hound sighting, every attack, every pin. Two years ago there were maybe forty of my dogs left in the ruins. Last month I counted three hundred. They're getting closer to the settlements. They're getting bolder. The Council doesn't care, because the Council lives in the nice parts of the new cities with walls and guards, and the hounds don't go there, because there's no residue there. The hounds come here. To the ruins. To the Reformed. To people like us, who still carry a little of it in our scars.

They're coming for us. And I made them. And I can't stop them.

But she could.



Chapter Four: The Count

Here's how I did it. Slow.

First month, I just let her come. I nodded. I said, "Thank you for sharing." I gave her a chip when she hit thirty days. A real chip, a poker chip I painted gold. She held it in her hand like it was a bomb.

Second month, I started asking her to stay after. Help me stack the chairs. Just talk. She told me about her brother. About the knife. About the grave on the hill and the stone she carved with rebar. About the cat. About Eri, who climbs seven flights every Tuesday. She told me about the hum. What it felt like. How it made everything quiet.

I listened. That's the job. That's what a sponsor does.

I learned where she lived. I learned which roof. I learned about the water tank. I watched her once, from the building across the street, go behind the tank and come out ten minutes later with her eyes red at the edges.

Day thirty-two. She'd used. She came to the meeting that Friday and said "Thirty-five days" and I said "Thank you for sharing" and gave her a little nod.

I knew. Of course I knew. I've been doing this for two years. And before that, I spent ten years under Tombstone watching corrupted people lie about how much they'd taken. I can see the red at the edges from across a room.

I didn't say anything.

I wanted to know what she'd do with it.



Chapter Five: Dov

I liked Dov. I need to say that.

He came in a year ago. He'd been one of Tombstone's runners, a kid really, fourteen when the corruption took him, carrying messages between camps. He'd never killed anybody. He'd just watched a lot of it happen. He felt like he'd killed everybody.

He looked at Ena the way the young ones look at the stars of the program. The ones who are making it. He'd sit next to her if she'd let him. He'd bring her the good chair. He told the group about the vein of shards in the rail yard and how he walked away because he thought about her, and he was so proud, and I was proud of him too, really proud, the real kind, the kind I haven't felt since my agility dogs.

And I'm the one who told the packs where the meeting was.

Not on purpose. Not exactly. I'd been leaving residue. Little pinches of black glass in the alley behind the laundromat. Old habit. Training. You leave a scent trail, you see who follows. I wanted to see how close the packs would come. I wanted to see if they'd come for Ena. I wanted to see, honestly, if I was right about her.

I didn't think six would come at once.

I didn't think they'd come on a Friday.

The first one through the window hit me before I could get my gun up. I went down behind the counter. I hit my head on the edge of a dryer. Things went gray.

I wasn't out the whole time. I want you to know that. I was lying behind the dryer with blood in my eyes, and I could see. Not everything. Enough.



Chapter Six: What I Saw

I saw Petrosyan go down. I saw the sister go down. I saw the hounds walk around Ena like she was their mother.

I saw Ena run.

I thought she'd left. I lay there and I thought: well, Marguerite, you were wrong. You fed six of your grandkids a room full of your people and the gun you were counting on walked out the door.

And then she came back.

I've seen a lot of corrupted. I was one. I served under the worst of them. I've never seen anything like what came back through that laundromat door. She was glowing. Not red at the edges. Red all the way through. Red coming off her skin like heat. Her left hand was black and steaming. She was laughing, and the laugh wasn't hers.

And my dogs looked up at her. All six. Like they'd heard their name.

She didn't move. She just looked at them. And they came apart. Like sugar in water. Bishop's grandkids. Clover's. Tess's. Into black glass on the tile.

I should've felt something. I made those dogs. I felt something. It wasn't grief.

It was relief. Because I was right. She can do it. She's the only thing in this world that can call them home.

And then Dov stood up from behind the dryers and said, "Ena?"

And then he said, "You're using."

And I saw her hand move.



Chapter Seven: Where's The Kid

I could have stopped it. I need to be honest about that, because I'm not going to be honest about it anywhere else.

I had the gun. It was empty, but she didn't know that. I could have shouted. I could have said her name. I could have said, "Ena, no." I've said "no" to things a lot bigger than her. Three-hundred-pound mastiffs. A corrupted Tombstone captain. Myself, every day for two years, four months and eleven days.

I didn't.

Because in the half second before her hand moved, I thought: if he tells, she's done. If he tells, she gets reported to the Council, and they take her away, to wherever they take the ones who relapse hard, and she's gone, and I'm sitting here with three hundred of my dogs and nobody to call them home.

So I lay there behind the dryer and I let a nineteen-year-old kid who brought her the good chair turn into glass on the floor of my laundromat.

Then I waited thirty seconds. Counted them. And I pushed myself up, and I wiped the blood out of my eyes, and I came around the counter like I'd just woken up.

"Where's the kid?" I said.

And she lied. Of course she did. "They got him. I was too late."

And I looked at her for a long time. Long enough that she'd know I knew. And then I said what I'd planned, the thirty seconds I spent behind the dryer, planning it.

"You used. We'll reset your count. That's okay. We'll reset it together. That's what it's for."

And I hugged her.

She let me. She was shaking. She'll be grateful to me for that hug for the rest of her life.

That's a sponsor.



Chapter Eight: The Blue Tin

We had the funeral. I picked a handful of shards off the floor for the jar. At random. I didn't look which ones. That's the only mercy I gave anybody that week, and it was for me.

Then I went back to the laundromat at three in the morning. I knew she'd come. They always come back for the leftovers. I've watched enough of them.

I got there first.

I picked up the rest. Every shard. Dogs and Dov, all mixed together, I couldn't tell which, nobody could. I put them in a little blue tin I'd had for years. It used to hold buttons. My mother's buttons. I'd kept it through the corruption and the war and Tombstone and everything after.

I walked two blocks toward Ena's building. I found a pile of rubble on the corner that she passes every morning on the way to the grave. I put the blue tin on top of it, where the light would catch it. Where she'd see it.

Then I went home and sat in front of my map with all its pins and I waited.

She found it. I watched from across the street, from my spot in the empty building. Saw her stop. Saw her pick it up. Saw her look around, guilty, and put it in her jacket, and keep walking. And that night, after she went back to the laundromat on her hands and knees for the shards that weren't there anymore, she found them already gone, and she didn't know who took them, and she didn't care, because she already had the tin.

Twenty-two shards. Enough for months, if she's careful. She won't be careful. That's fine. I know where more are.

On Tuesday, Eri came by the laundromat. The demon with one eye. Ena's friend. She sat in a folding chair across from me after the meeting and she said, "How's she really doing? Tell me the truth. She tells me day one, day two, but I know her. I'm a mind demon, Marguerite. I know when somebody's got something behind a door."

I could have told her. Eri would have stopped it. Eri would have climbed seven flights and kicked over the water tank and found the brick and the tin and dragged Ena out by her hair to someplace safe.

"She's doing great," I said. "She's really trying. Day five. I'm so proud of her."

Eri looked at me with her one eye for a long time. I don't know what she saw. I've spent a long time building doors. I'm good at it.

"Okay," Eri said. "Thanks, Marguerite. Thanks for being there for her."

"That's what I'm for," I said.



Chapter Nine: When I'm Ready

Friday's meeting. Seven people now. The circle's smaller. I took two chairs out so it wouldn't look so empty.

Ena came in last. Hood up. Eyes a little red at the edges. Nobody but me noticed.

"Hi, I'm Ena," she said. "Day six."

"Thank you for sharing," I said.

After, I asked her to stay and stack chairs. She did. We stacked them against the dead machines, quiet, the way we always do.

"Hey," I said. "There's a pack. Out past the rail yard. Big one, maybe forty dogs. They took out a settlement on the east side last week. Families. Kids."

She stopped stacking.

"Somebody's got to do something," I said. "The Council won't. The Council's never going to come out here." I put my hand on her shoulder, the way I did that night. "I'm not asking you to do anything. I'd never ask you to put your recovery at risk. I'm just telling you. Somebody's got to."

She was quiet for a long time.

"Somebody has to," she said.

"I'm proud of you," I said. "Whatever you decide. I'm proud of you."

I watched her walk out. I watched her turn left, toward her building, toward the roof, toward the tank, toward the brick.

Then I went home and I took one pin out of the map. The rail yard pin. I already know how it ends. She'll go out there with my mother's button tin in her pocket and she'll come back with forty fewer dogs and twenty fewer days and a laugh that isn't hers. And she'll come to the meeting and say "day one" and I'll say thank you for sharing.

And then I'll find her the next pack.

Three hundred dogs. Maybe more by now. It's going to take her a long time. Years, maybe. She'll use the whole time. She'll be Corrupt again by the end, probably. All the way. Red all the way through.

I did the math. Three hundred dogs that will eat every Reformed in the ruins, against one girl who already ended the world once. It's a good trade. I'd make it again. I'm making it every Friday.

Somebody in the circle asked me last week, again, when I'm going to share. Two years and they still ask.

"When I'm ready," I said.

They nodded. They think it's grief. They think I'm carrying something heavy.

I'm not. I'm carrying a leash. It's just very long, and the dog on the end of it has shining blonde hair, and she thinks I'm holding her hand.
