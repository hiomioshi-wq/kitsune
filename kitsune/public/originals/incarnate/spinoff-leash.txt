Chapter One: Do You Agree?

Ivy Calder never raised her voice.

She did not need to. She had a soft, warm, reasonable voice, the voice of a favorite teacher or a very good doctor, and when she used it, people leaned in to listen. She would tilt her head. She would smile, just slightly, with her eyes more than her mouth. And then she would ask a small, reasonable question.

"You'd like the window open, wouldn't you? It's so warm in here."

"Yes," the person would say. "Yes, I would."

And that was all it took.

Ivy was a lesser anti-god of order. Her power was so quiet that for most of her life she had not known she had one. It was this: anyone who agreed with her, about anything, however small, was bound to her. A thin invisible thread, from her hand to their heart. And once the thread was there, she could pull it. Gently. Never hard. Just a little tug, here and there, to keep them going the way she knew was best.

She called it the Leash. Never out loud.

By the second year after the end of everything, Ivy Calder held eleven thousand leashes.

She did not keep a pink palace, like that silly girl Dolly who had made such a spectacle of herself and then given it all away. Ivy kept an office. A plain white office on the top floor of the Hall of Restoration, the building where the new government of the restored world met. Her title was Undersecretary for Public Calm. Nobody quite knew what an Undersecretary for Public Calm did. Everybody agreed that the restored world was much calmer since she had been appointed.

The trains ran on time. The streets were clean. The markets were orderly. Nobody shouted in the town squares anymore about who should be on the monument, or whether Corrupt had been a monster or a victim. Somebody always happened to be standing nearby, a pleasant, reasonable person, who would ask a pleasant, reasonable question:

"Don't you think it's better not to dwell on it?"

And the shouter would say, "Yes. Yes, I suppose it is."

And go home. And not dwell on it. And never quite know why.

Ivy sat in her white office on the top floor and felt the eleven thousand threads in her hands, the way a spider feels its web, and was content.

Almost.

There was one thread she held tighter than all the others. A thread she had held since she was six years old. A thread she checked a hundred times a day, the way you check your pocket for your keys.

Juno.


Chapter Two: Juno

Juno Pell had been Ivy's best friend since the first day of school, when Ivy, aged six, had walked up to the smallest, shyest, most frightened-looking girl in the room and said:

"You want to sit with me, don't you?"

And Juno, aged six, holding a lunchbox with a frog on it, had said, "Yes."

It was the first leash Ivy ever made. She had not even known she was making it. She only knew that from that day on, Juno was hers. Juno sat with her. Juno played the games Ivy wanted to play. Juno wore her hair the way Ivy said it looked best, and liked the books Ivy said were good, and stopped being friends with the loud girl in the next class when Ivy said the loud girl was a bad influence.

It was good for Juno. Ivy was absolutely sure of that. Juno was soft. Juno was frightened of everything. Juno cried when people raised their voices and froze when she had to make a decision and would, Ivy was certain, have been eaten alive by the world if Ivy had not been there to steer her through it.

Now they were twenty-two. Juno lived in the apartment across the hall from Ivy, in the plain white building near the Hall of Restoration. She worked as Ivy's secretary, in a plain white office next to Ivy's own. Every morning she brought Ivy coffee, exactly the way Ivy liked it, and every evening they ate dinner together at Ivy's table, and every night Ivy lay in bed and checked the thread.

Still there. Still tight. Still hers.

And then, one Tuesday in early spring, Juno came into the office with the coffee and set it down on Ivy's desk and said:

"I went to the grey towns this weekend."

Ivy looked up.

"You didn't tell me you were going."

"I know," said Juno. She was not quite looking at Ivy. She was looking at the coffee. "I wanted to see. Everyone's talking about it. The girl with the Horseman in her. Pia. And Dolly Adore, the pink girl, who let everyone go. She works in a bakery now. Did you know that? She gave away the whole palace and she works in a bakery." Juno's hands twisted together. "I talked to her. Dolly. She was so nice. She didn't do the eye thing. She just talked to me. About what it felt like. Having everyone love her and knowing none of it was real."

The thread in Ivy's hand was very still.

"And?" said Ivy softly.

Juno looked up. Her eyes were wet.

"And I wondered," she said, "if I'd know. If it was me. If somebody had me on a string. I wondered if I'd even be able to tell."

Ivy smiled her best smile. The warm, reasonable one.

"Juno," she said. "Sweetheart. You're tired. You've been traveling. You'd like to take the afternoon off, wouldn't you?"

Juno opened her mouth.

And for the first time in sixteen years, there was a pause before she answered.

It was only half a second. It was barely anything. But Ivy felt it. Felt the thread go slack, just for that half a second, like a fishing line when the fish stops pulling.

"Yes," said Juno at last. "Yes. I would."

The thread tightened again. Ivy let out a breath she had not known she was holding.

"Good," she said. "Go home. Rest. I'll see you at dinner."

Juno went. And Ivy sat alone in the white office for a long, long time, with her hand pressed flat against the desk, feeling for the thread.


Chapter Three: The Problem With the World

That night, Ivy went to see Famine.

She did not tell anyone. She took the night train out to the red canyon at the center of the restored world, and walked down the path in the dark, and stood at the edge of the black glass hollow where the Horseman of Famine stood with her arms open, holding up the world.

Famine opened her black eyes.

"Undersecretary," she said. "You're a long way from your office."

"I wanted to see you," said Ivy pleasantly. "The nexus. The point that holds everything together. You must find it very tiring."

"Not so much, lately," said Famine. "A fire anti-god and her sister came and fed me. I'm good for a hundred years." Her black eyes studied Ivy. "What do you want?"

Ivy smiled.

"I want to help," she said. "That's all I've ever wanted. Look at this world, Famine. Two years since the war, and it's still so fragile. People still shout in the squares. They still argue about the monument, and the anti-gods, and whose fault it all was. And every time they argue, every time someone loses their temper, every time a crowd gets angry, I can feel it. The world shakes. Just a little. And you have to hold it."

"That's my job."

"It doesn't have to be," said Ivy. "If everyone were calm. If everyone agreed. If nobody ever argued again, about anything, then you'd never have to strain. The world would never shake. There would never be another apocalypse. Never another Galactix, never another Tombstone, never another girl crying at a tower because her brother died saving everyone." She stepped closer, to the very edge of the black glass. "I could do that. I'm already doing it. Eleven thousand people. But I could do it for everyone. If I were the nexus."

Famine was very still.

"You want my place," she said.

"I want to give you a rest," said Ivy. "You've held it for two years. You'd like to rest, wouldn't you?"

It was the question. The soft, reasonable question. Ivy felt the thread form in her hand, felt it reach out toward the Horseman of Famine, toward her heart.

Famine looked at her with her black, bottomless eyes.

"No," she said.

The thread snapped like a dry twig.

Ivy blinked.

"I'm hungry," said Famine. "I'm always hungry. That's what I am. And do you know what I'm hungriest for, Undersecretary? Not food. Not power." The black eyes did not blink. "Argument. Noise. People shouting in town squares about whether my sister Horseman should be on a monument. People disagreeing. That's what a world eats. That's what keeps it alive. A world where nobody argues is a world that's already dead. It just hasn't stopped moving yet." She smiled, very slightly. "You'd starve it. And then you'd wonder why it got so quiet."

Ivy stood at the edge of the black glass with the snapped thread hanging from her hand.

"You'll change your mind," she said pleasantly.

"No," said Famine. "But you might."


Chapter Four: Eleven Thousand and One

She did not go to Famine again. She had a better idea.

If Famine would not agree, then Famine did not need to agree. The nexus was a place, not just a person. A point where dimensions met. And Ivy had read, in the old records in the Hall of Restoration, that the nexus could be shared. That once, long ago, during the great war, it had been held by two beings at once.

All she needed was enough threads.

She spent a month pulling. Gently. Never hard. Here and there. The Council of Restoration voted to place a guard around the canyon, for Famine's safety. The guards were all people Ivy held. The engineers voted to build a platform at the edge of the black glass hollow, for the comfort of visitors. Ivy chose the engineers. The doctors who checked on Famine every week began to recommend that the Horseman be given a companion at the nexus, to share the burden. Ivy had spoken to every one of the doctors.

"You agree, don't you?" she would say. "It's only kind."

And they would say yes.

Eleven thousand became twelve. Twelve became fifteen. By the end of the month, Ivy Calder held twenty thousand leashes, and every one of them was pulling, gently, toward the red canyon at the center of the world.

And through all of it, every morning, Juno brought her coffee.

Juno was quieter now. Ivy had noticed. She did not chatter about the grey towns anymore. She did not talk about Dolly Adore. She set the coffee down on the desk, exactly the way Ivy liked it, and went back to her own white office, and typed, and filed, and said very little.

The thread was still there. Ivy checked it a hundred times a day. Still there. Still tight.

But every time Ivy asked her a question now, there was a pause.

Half a second.

Then a whole second.

Then two.

"You'll come to dinner tonight, won't you?"

Pause. "Yes."

"You'd like the window open, wouldn't you?"

Pause. "Yes."

"You agree that I'm doing the right thing. Don't you, Juno?"

A long, long pause.

"Yes," said Juno. And then, so quietly that Ivy almost didn't hear it: "I think so."

Ivy put down her pen.

I think so. Juno had never said I think so in her life. Juno said yes. Juno always said yes. That was the point of Juno. That was what Ivy had built, sixteen years ago, with one question on the first day of school.

"Juno," said Ivy, in her softest, warmest, most reasonable voice. "Look at me."

Juno looked.

"You've been talking to someone," said Ivy. "Haven't you?"

And Juno, who had never in sixteen years kept a single thing from Ivy, looked her best friend in the eye and said:

"Yes. Dolly. And Pia. And a Horseman named War." She took a breath. "And they told me what a leash feels like."


Chapter Five: What a Leash Feels Like

"It feels like being loved," said Juno.

They were sitting at Ivy's table, at dinner, with the window open because it was a warm spring night and Ivy liked it open. Neither of them was eating.

"That's what Dolly said," Juno went on. "She said the Crush felt like being loved. To the people she did it to. Warm and pink and safe. And Pia said War told her a leash would feel different. War said a leash feels like being right. Like every choice you make is the right one, and you never have to worry, because someone's already worried for you." She looked down at her untouched plate. "That's what you feel like, Ivy. You always have. You feel like being right."

"Because I am right," said Ivy softly. "About you. I've always been right about you. You were so scared, Juno. On the first day of school. You were the most frightened little girl I'd ever seen. You'd have been eaten alive."

"Maybe," said Juno.

"I protected you."

"Maybe," said Juno again. She lifted her eyes. "But I'll never know, will I? Because you never let me try."

Ivy said nothing.

"Do you know what Dolly asked me?" said Juno. "She asked me what my favorite book was. And I started to say the one you always said was good. And then I stopped. Because I didn't know if I liked it, or if I liked it because you said so. And I stood there in that grey bakery for five minutes, Ivy, trying to remember a single thing I liked that you didn't choose for me." Her voice cracked. "And I couldn't. I couldn't think of one thing."

Ivy reached across the table and took Juno's hand. Felt the thread there, under the skin, warm and tight.

"You're upset," she said gently. "You've had a long day. You'd like to go to bed, wouldn't you? And in the morning this will all seem—"

"No," said Juno.

The thread snapped.

It did not snap like Famine's thread, like a dry twig. It snapped like a guitar string, sharp and singing, and the recoil of it went up Ivy's arm and into her chest and she gasped out loud and let go of Juno's hand.

Juno was standing up. Her chair had fallen over behind her. Her face was white and wet and terrified and absolutely, completely her own.

"No," she said again. Softer. Shaking. "No. I don't want to go to bed. I want to go home. My home. Not across the hall from you. I don't even know where I'd go. I don't know anything. But I want to find out." She backed toward the door. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I love you. I think I do. I think it's me that loves you, not the leash. But I don't know. And I need to know. So I have to go."

"Juno," said Ivy.

"Please don't follow me."

And Juno Pell, who had never once in sixteen years made a decision on her own, walked out of Ivy Calder's apartment, and down the plain white stairs, and out into the warm spring night, and did not look back.

Ivy sat at the table with the window open.

Twenty thousand threads in her hands. Twenty thousand people who agreed with her about everything.

And the only one she had ever cared about was gone.


Chapter Six: The Canyon

She went to the nexus the next morning.

She did not plan it. She simply found herself on the night train, and then on the path down into the red canyon, with twenty thousand threads pulling behind her, and the guards she held stepping aside to let her pass, and the platform her engineers had built standing at the edge of the black glass hollow like an open door.

Famine opened her eyes.

"You came back," said the Horseman.

"I came to help," said Ivy. Her voice was not quite as warm as usual. Not quite as steady. "You'll let me share the burden. You'll let me help hold the world. Everyone agrees it's for the best. Everyone." She gestured behind her, at the guards, the engineers, the doctors, the long line of people filing silently down the canyon path behind her, pulled by their threads. "Twenty thousand of them. They all agree."

"I don't," said Famine.

"You don't have to," said Ivy. "The nexus can be shared. I read it. I just have to step onto the glass, and once I'm part of it, I'll hold every thread in the world. Not twenty thousand. Everyone. Everyone in every dimension that touches this one. And I'll make it calm. I'll make it safe. Nobody will ever be frightened again. Nobody will ever have to leave—"

She stopped.

Famine looked at her for a long moment with her black, bottomless eyes.

"Nobody will ever have to leave you," said the Horseman quietly. "That's what you were going to say."

"No."

"Yes," said Famine. "You can't say no to me, Undersecretary. I'm hunger. I can smell what you're starving for. And it isn't calm. It isn't order. It's one person. It's always been one person. And she walked out of your apartment last night." The black eyes softened, very slightly. "And you think if you hold everyone, she'll come back. Because she'll have to."

Ivy's hands were shaking. All twenty thousand threads trembled with them.

"Step back from the glass," said a voice behind her.

Ivy turned.

At the top of the canyon path, standing in a line in front of the silent crowd, were four figures. A tall silver-haired woman with eyes that flickered red: Ena. Corrupt. A girl in grey boots with flour on her hands and eyes the color of strawberry ice cream, held carefully down: Dolly. A thin pale girl with one brown eye and one dark red one: Pia, and War.

And between them, white-faced and shaking and absolutely, completely herself, Juno.


Chapter Seven: The Line

"You came," said Ivy. Her voice broke on it. "Juno. You came back."

"I came to stop you," said Juno. "I'm sorry. I came to stop you."

"You can't," said Ivy. "None of you can. Look." She lifted her hands, and behind her, filling the canyon path, twenty thousand people lifted their heads at the same time, their eyes calm and empty and agreeing. "They'll do anything I ask. If I ask them to stand between you and the glass, they'll stand there. If I ask them to carry you away, they'll carry you. Gently. I never hurt anyone. I'd never hurt anyone. But you can't stop me."

"We know," said Ena.

She came down the path. Slowly. Her silver hair blowing. The red light flickering around her fingers. She stopped a few feet from Ivy, at the edge of the black glass.

"I knew a man once," she said, "who wanted to hold everything. His name was Tombstone. He was the nexus of my old world. He thought if he held enough of it, he'd be safe. He corrupted me. Made me his lieutenant. I killed half the world for him." Her red eyes did not blink. "And at the end, when my brother killed him, do you know what he was? He was alone. Holding everything, and alone."

Ivy said nothing.

"I'm not here to fight you," said Ena. "War wanted to fight you. Pia said no. Dolly said no. I said no. We're not here to fight." She glanced back at Juno. "We're here to hold the line. So she can say what she came to say."

And Ena stepped aside.

Juno came down the canyon path. Her hands were shaking. Her face was wet. She walked past Ena, past the edge of the platform, until she stood in front of Ivy at the edge of the black glass, close enough to touch.

"You asked me a question," said Juno, "on the first day of school. You said, you want to sit with me, don't you. And I said yes. And I've been saying yes for sixteen years." She took a breath. "I'm going to ask you a question now. And I want you to answer it without the leash. Without asking me anything back. Without tilting your head. Just you. Can you do that?"

Ivy stared at her.

"Yes," she whispered.

"Why did you pick me?" said Juno. "That first day. There were thirty kids in that room. Why me? The smallest, scaredest one. Why did you walk up to me?"

The canyon was silent. Twenty thousand people stood motionless on the path. Famine watched with her black eyes. Somewhere at the back, Pia's red eye flickered.

And Ivy Calder, who had never once in her life answered a question she had not asked herself, opened her mouth, and found that she did not have an answer ready. No reasonable reply. No warm, soft, persuasive voice.

Only the truth, which she had never said out loud. Not to anyone. Not even to herself.

"Because I was scared too," she said.

Juno went very still.

"I was scared," said Ivy. Her voice was coming apart. "I was six and my mother had just left and I didn't know anyone and I didn't know what I was, I didn't know about the leash, I just knew I was so scared I couldn't breathe. And I looked around that room, and I saw you, holding a lunchbox with a frog on it, and you looked even more scared than me. And I thought, if I take care of her, then I won't have to be scared. If I'm the one in charge, then nobody can leave me. Because I'll be holding on." She was crying now. She did not try to stop. "And then you said yes. And it worked. And I never let go. Because I was so scared that if I let go, you'd see how scared I was. And you'd leave."

The canyon was very quiet.

"I would never have left," said Juno softly. "If you'd asked me. Really asked. Without the leash. I would have said yes anyway." She reached out and took Ivy's shaking hand. "I think. I don't know. That's the thing, Ivy. We'll never know. Because you never gave me the chance."

Ivy looked down at their joined hands.

There was no thread there now. Not between them. Juno had snapped it. There was only a hand, warm and small and trembling, holding hers. By choice.

"Do you want to find out?" said Juno.


Chapter Eight: Letting Go

It was the hardest thing Ivy had ever done.

Twenty thousand threads. She had spent years gathering them. Every single one was a person who agreed with her, who would never argue, never shout, never leave. Every single one was a little piece of the safety she had been building since she was six years old.

She stood at the edge of the nexus with Juno's hand in hers, and she let go.

She did it one at a time. Not all at once, the way Dolly had. Ivy was not built for all at once. She had never done anything all at once in her life. She let go of the guards first, and the guards blinked and looked around at the red canyon and said, "What are we doing here?" She let go of the engineers, and they stared at the platform they had built and scratched their heads. She let go of the doctors, the clerks, the councillors, the shopkeepers, the train drivers, the people who had stood in town squares and been asked a soft reasonable question and gone home without arguing.

And as she let go, she could hear it. All across the restored world. Faint at first, and then louder, and then louder still.

Noise.

People arguing in town squares about the monument. People shouting about whether Corrupt had been a monster or a victim. People disagreeing about the trains, and the markets, and the weather, and each other. Twenty thousand people waking up and finding, all at once, that they had opinions again. Loud ones. Messy ones. Their own.

In the black glass hollow, the Horseman of Famine threw back her head and breathed it in, and laughed, a deep rough hungry laugh.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, that's good. That's so good. Listen to them. Listen to all that noise." Her black eyes shone. "That's the best meal I've had since the sisters fed me fire."

Ivy let go of the last thread.

She stood on the canyon floor, empty-handed, for the first time in sixteen years, and felt the silence where the threads had been. It was enormous. It was terrifying. It felt like standing on the edge of a cliff with nothing to hold onto.

Juno's hand tightened on hers.

"I'm still here," said Juno.

"Because you want to be?" whispered Ivy.

Juno thought about it. Really thought about it, for a long time, with her face screwed up, the way Ivy had never once in sixteen years seen her think.

"I don't know yet," said Juno honestly. "But I'm here right now. And I chose it. That's something."

"That's something," said Ivy, and cried.


Chapter Nine: Questions

Ivy Calder resigned as Undersecretary for Public Calm the next week. Nobody quite knew what an Undersecretary for Public Calm had done, so nobody quite knew what they would be missing. The restored world got louder. The trains ran late. The markets got messy. People shouted in the town squares about the monument again, and somebody went in the night with a chisel and added another line, and somebody else went in the next night and argued with it in a different handwriting, and it went on like that for months, the tower slowly covered in a long, loud, unfinished conversation in stone.

Famine said it was the healthiest the world had ever been.

Juno moved out of the apartment across the hall. She went to the grey towns for a while. She worked at the bakery with Dolly, and found out she hated baking, and loved the grey jam, and liked books with dragons in them, which Ivy had always said were childish. She cut her hair short, and then decided she hated it short, and grew it back. She cried a lot, at first. Then less.

She wrote to Ivy every week. Short letters. Things she had found out she liked. Things she had found out she didn't.

Ivy wrote back. It was hard. Every letter, her pen hovered over the page, and she felt the old pull, the old soft reasonable voice, rising up in her: You'd like to come home now, wouldn't you? You'd agree it's time?

She never wrote it. She wrote other things instead. Real things. That she was scared. That she missed her. That she had started going to the town square in the evenings and standing at the edge of the crowd and listening to people argue, and that it made her stomach hurt, and that she was trying to get used to it.

She did not ask Juno a single question. Not in a whole year of letters. She did not trust herself to.

And then, at the end of the year, Juno wrote:

I'm coming back to the city next week. I'd like to have dinner. With you. If you want.

And underneath, in smaller writing:

You can ask me something. One thing. I'll tell you if I mean it.

Ivy read the letter nine times.

On the night of the dinner, they sat at a table in a small loud restaurant near the Hall of Restoration, with the window open because it was a warm spring night and Juno liked it open. Juno had her hair long again. She had a book with a dragon on the cover sticking out of her bag. She looked nervous and happy and completely, entirely herself.

Ivy sat across from her with her hands in her lap, because she did not trust them on the table.

"You can ask," said Juno. "One thing."

Ivy took a breath.

She thought of every soft reasonable question she had ever asked. You'd like the window open, wouldn't you. You want to sit with me, don't you. You agree, don't you. Questions that were not really questions. Questions that only had one answer.

She did not ask any of those.

"Are you happy?" she said instead. "Really. Without me deciding. Are you happy?"

Juno looked at her for a long time. Then she smiled. It was not the old smile, the shy, nervous, waiting-to-be-told smile. It was a new one. Ivy had never seen it before. It was crooked and bright and a little bit fierce.

"Mostly," said Juno. "Not all the time. Sometimes I'm really sad. Sometimes I don't know what I'm doing at all. But mostly?" She nodded. "Yeah. Mostly I am."

"Good," said Ivy, and found that she meant it. That it did not hurt. That it was, in fact, the best thing anyone had ever said to her.

"Now I get to ask you one," said Juno.

Ivy braced herself.

"What's your favorite book?" said Juno. "Not the one you always told people was good. Your actual favorite. The one you like."

Ivy opened her mouth to say the title she had said a thousand times. The serious one. The respectable one. The one that made her look calm and reasonable and in control.

And then she stopped.

And she thought.

And for the first time in her whole life, Ivy Calder did not know the answer to a question.

"I don't know," she said slowly. "I don't think I've ever let myself have one."

Juno grinned and pulled the book with the dragon on it out of her bag and slid it across the table.

"Then start with this," she said. "You'll hate it. Tell me if you hate it. I want to argue about it."

Ivy looked down at the dragon. Then up at her oldest friend, sitting across the table by choice, in a loud restaurant, in a loud and messy and living world, waiting to argue with her.

"Okay," she said.

It was not a yes. It was not agreement.

It was the beginning of a conversation.
