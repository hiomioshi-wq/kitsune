Incarnate: Only Child
A spinoff of the Corruption Saga. During Main Character, Uncut.

Trigger warning: This story contains grief, the death of a child, memory loss, a family that remembers what you don't, a choice to stay comfortable, and a narrator who decides she'd rather not know.
Read at your own risk.



Chapter One: The Hum

I'm making tea when the hum starts.

Not in the kettle. In me. It comes up from under my ribs like a song you heard in a store once and can't remember the name of. And it's everywhere. I can hear it coming out of the radiator. Out of the window, from the street. Out of my phone on the counter, even though the phone is off.

I'm humming it too. I don't know when I started.

My name is Noor Haddad. I'm nineteen. I live in a basement apartment in Squirrel Hill with a radiator that clanks and a window at sidewalk level where you can watch people's feet. I work at a coffee shop on Murray Avenue now. I'm good at it. I'm good at everything lately. I'm happy. I have a boyfriend named Theo who makes latte art that looks like cats.

I'm an only child.

I want to say that up front because it's important, and because recently there's been some confusion about it.



Chapter Two: The Roommate

There was a girl who lived here for two weeks. Calypso. She had a crown. I don't remember much about her except that she was so beautiful, and that I was sad before she came, and I wasn't sad after.

I don't remember what I was sad about. That's the weird part. I remember being sad. I remember lying on the mattress for months, staring at the ceiling. I remember buying bus tickets at three in the morning and not using them. I remember my mom calling and calling and me not picking up.

But I don't remember why.

It's like finding a scar and not knowing where it came from. You know something happened. You just can't find the story.

She left a bus ticket on the kitchen table when she went. Folded. I kept it. I don't know why. It's in the drawer with the takeout menus.

And now everybody in the world is humming the same song, and the news says it's coming from everywhere at once, and scientists are on CNN saying "mass auditory phenomenon," and preachers are on Fox saying the end times, and on TikTok everybody's just saying her name.

Calypso. Calypso. Calypso.

I know her name. I lived with her.

I don't tell anybody that.



Chapter Three: Mom's House

My mom calls on Sunday. I pick up now. I pick up every time. She says it's like I'm a different person.

"Come for dinner, habibti. Your father's making maqluba."

So I take the bus to their house in Brookline. A little brick house with a porch and a lemon tree in a pot that never grows any lemons. And Mom hugs me too long at the door, the way she does now, like she's checking I'm real, and Dad's in the kitchen flipping the pot over onto a platter, and everybody cheers when it comes out clean, and it's nice. It's so nice.

And then I go upstairs to use the bathroom, and I pass a door.

It's closed. It's always been closed, I think. I don't remember it ever being open. It's at the end of the hall, past my old room, and it's got stickers on it. Old stickers, half peeled. A Pittsburgh Penguins logo. A dinosaur. A sticker that says KEEP OUT in red letters, and under it, in marker, in a kid's handwriting: THIS MEANS NOOR.

I stand there for a long time.

THIS MEANS NOOR.



Chapter Four: The Room

I open it.

It's a kid's room. A boy's room. Bunk bed with only the top bunk made. Penguins posters. A desk covered in Lego. A shelf of books, Percy Jackson, Wings of Fire, a math workbook for sixth grade with the answers filled in, wrong, in pencil, and corrected, in a different pencil, in my handwriting.

My handwriting.

There are photos on the corkboard over the desk. A boy. Dark curly hair. My nose. My mom's eyes. Grinning with a missing tooth. At the beach with my dad. At a Penguins game with my mom. On a swing. On a bike.

With me.

Me at seventeen with my arm around him. Me at fifteen holding him up to see a giraffe at the zoo. Me at eighteen sitting on the porch steps with him, both of us with popsicles, both of us laughing at something off the edge of the picture.

I don't know him.

I sit down on the bottom bunk. It creaks. It smells like boy. Like sweat and pencil shavings and that cheap shampoo that smells like fake green apples.

I don't know him at all.

The hum's coming up from my ribs, louder now. It sounds like it's trying to tell me something. Or trying to make me not hear something.

I take one photo off the corkboard. Me and the boy on the porch with popsicles. I turn it over. On the back, in my mom's handwriting:

Noor and Sami. Summer before.

Before what?



Chapter Five: Dinner

I go back downstairs with the photo in my pocket. Dad's pouring tea. Mom's cutting baklava.

"Mom," I say. "Who's Sami?"

Everything stops.

My dad's hand stops. Tea keeps pouring, over the rim of the glass, onto the tablecloth, and he doesn't notice. My mom puts the knife down very slowly.

"What did you say?"

"Sami. There's a room upstairs. With pictures. Of me and a boy. It says Sami on the back."

My mom puts her hand over her mouth. Her eyes go wet.

"Noor," she says. "Noor. Habibti. Your brother."

"I don't have a brother."

My dad sits down. Hard. Like his knees stopped working.

"Eight months ago," my mom says. Very carefully. Like she's handing me something made of glass. "Nine now. The crosswalk on Forbes. You were supposed to walk him home. You were late. A car."

She stops. She can't finish.

"You don't remember," my dad says. Not a question. He's looking at me like he's never seen me before. "Since that girl. Since you came back happy. We thought... we thought you were doing better. We thought you were healing. We didn't want to say his name, we thought it would set you back. But you don't remember him at all."

I look down at the photo. The boy with the popsicle. Laughing.

The hum gets louder.

And I feel something. Way down. At the bottom of a well I didn't know I had. A sound. Like someone very small calling my name from very far away, annoyed.

"Nooooor."



Chapter Six: Juniper

She's waiting on my steps when I get home. A girl. Twenties. Thrift-store cardigan, paint-spattered Doc Martens, a messenger bag stuffed with papers and a box of black candles sticking out the top. Bags under her eyes like she hasn't slept in a month. She's smoking a clove cigarette and her hands are shaking.

"Noor Haddad?"

"Who are you?"

"Juniper. I'm the one who keeps burning her name." She holds up a phone. On it, paused, is a livestream. Grainy. A girl in a crown in a Motel 6. "I saw you. In the background. Week four. She was streaming from your apartment. Your face is behind her for like eleven seconds. I've watched it two hundred times."

She stands up. She looks at me like I'm a door.

"She ate something out of you. Didn't she. Something big."

I don't say anything. The photo's in my pocket. I can feel the edge of it through my jeans.

"I can give it back," Juniper says.



Chapter Seven: What She Said

We sit on the floor of my apartment. She's got her papers spread everywhere. Newspaper clippings. Printouts. A drawing from 1891 of girls in gray dresses, one of them circled in red. A photo of a town in Illinois where everybody's standing in the street smiling. A map of the whole country with red string going from pin to pin, east, east, east, ending at the ocean.

"When she eats a want," Juniper says, "it doesn't disappear. It goes into her. It's in there. Everything she ever ate. Every sailor. Every girl in Switzerland. Every kid in that gym. Your brother. All of it, inside her, like stones in a stomach."

"Okay."

"And right now, she's everywhere. That's what the hum is. She went into something huge and she's singing through it. To everything. Every god, every world. She's wide open. For the first time in forty thousand years, she's not hiding."

"Okay."

"So there's a door," Juniper says. "If you've been eaten by her, there's still a thread between you and what she took. Like a receipt. And if I light the right candle, and say the right words, and you hold that thread, I can pull. I can pull it back out of her. Your brother. All of him. Every memory. You'd remember him again."

The radiator clanks. Outside the window, feet go by. Everyone's humming.

"And it would hurt her," Juniper says, quieter. "It would hurt her a lot. It would make the song stutter. Maybe enough to give somebody, somewhere, a chance to stop it."

I look at the photo. The boy. The popsicle.

"What would it feel like?" I ask. "For me?"

Juniper doesn't answer right away. That's how I know.

"Like it just happened," she says. "Like the crosswalk was this morning. All nine months of grief, all at once. Everything she ate out of you, back in you. All of it."



Chapter Eight: Theo

Theo comes over that night. He brings pad thai. He makes me tea with a cat drawn in the foam with a toothpick. He's so sweet. He doesn't know about any of it. He met me after. He's only ever known me happy.

We sit on the mattress and watch a show and he falls asleep with his head on my shoulder, and I sit there in the blue light of the TV, and I take out the photo again.

I try to remember. I try so hard. I squeeze my eyes shut and I think: Sami. Sami. Popsicles. Porch. The zoo. The giraffe. A Penguins game.

Nothing.

Just the hum.

And the little voice, down at the bottom, so far away.

"Nooooor."

And I think about what Juniper said. Like the crosswalk was this morning.

I think about the mattress. Months of it. Staring at the ceiling. Not eating. Bus tickets at three a.m. My mom calling and calling. I don't remember why, but I remember how. I remember what it was like to be that girl. It was like being buried alive. It was like being at the bottom of a well with the lid on.

And I look at Theo, asleep on my shoulder. And the cat in the foam of my tea, dissolving. And my apartment, which I keep clean now. And my job, which I'm good at. And my mom, who hugs me too long at the door because she's so glad I'm back.

And I think: I'd lose all of this. I'd go back to the mattress. For a boy I don't know.



Chapter Nine: No

Juniper comes back in the morning. She's got the candles out of her bag already. Black. Seven of them. She's got chalk. She's got a knife, a little one, for the blood.

"Ready?"

"No," I say.

She stops.

"I'm not doing it."

"Noor—"

"I don't know him." My voice is steady. That surprises me. "I'm sorry. I know that's terrible. I know he was my brother. I know my mom keeps his room and my dad can't say his name. I know I was supposed to walk him home. But I don't know him. And I'm happy. For the first time in my life, I think. And you want me to give that back, and go back down the well, to maybe make a song stutter. For gods I've never heard of. In worlds I've never been to."

"It's not just gods." Juniper's hands are shaking worse. "She's not going to stop at gods. You know that. She never stops. She's going to eat everything, Noor. Every person on Earth. Your mom. Theo. Everyone. Everyone is going to end up like you."

"Like me," I say. "Happy?"

Juniper stares at me.

I go to the kitchen drawer. I take out the bus ticket Calypso left. Folded. I hand it to Juniper.

"Here. Use this. She touched it. Maybe it's a thread."

"It's not enough."

"Then I'm sorry."

And I am. I really am. I'm sorry the way you're sorry about a car accident on the news. It's sad. It's far away. It's not mine.

"Please leave," I say.

She looks at me for a long time. Then she picks up her candles and her chalk and her little knife and her messenger bag, and she goes up the steps, past the window, and I watch her feet walk away down the sidewalk.

I put the photo back in the drawer with the takeout menus.



Chapter Ten: Only Child

I go to work. I make lattes. Theo draws cats in the foam. Everybody hums.

My mom calls on Wednesday. "Come for dinner, habibti."

I go. I don't go upstairs. I use the downstairs bathroom.

My dad doesn't look at me during dinner. My mom keeps starting sentences and stopping them. Finally, over the baklava, she says, very carefully, "Noor. The girl. The one who was with you. If there's a way. Any way. To get it back. To remember him. Would you want—"

"No, Mom."

She puts her fork down.

"I'm sorry," I say. "I'm happy. Isn't that what you wanted? You wanted me to be happy."

My mom looks at me with her eyes, which are Sami's eyes, I know that now from the pictures, and she doesn't say anything. She just nods. And she gets up and goes upstairs, and I hear the door at the end of the hall open, the one with the stickers, and close.

My dad finishes his tea. He looks at the lemon tree in the pot by the window.

"He used to water that," he says. "Every day. He said it would grow lemons if you believed in it hard enough."

I look at the lemon tree. No lemons. Never any lemons.

"That's sweet," I say.

And I mean it. The way you mean it about a stranger.



Chapter Eleven: The Hum Gets Louder

It's been three weeks. The hum's louder every day.

The news has stopped pretending to explain it. There are people in some cities who've stopped going to work. They just stand in the streets, humming, smiling. They look so peaceful. There was a story about a whole hospital in Cleveland where nobody's sad anymore. Not the patients, not the families, not the doctors. Everybody's just happy. Even the ones who are dying.

Theo hums in his sleep now.

My mom hums when she waters the lemon tree.

I hum all the time. It's nice. It fills in the space where something should be.

Sometimes, late at night, when the radiator clanks and the street is quiet and Theo is asleep, I hear the other thing. Way down. At the bottom. Getting fainter every time.

"Nooooor."

And I hum over it. Louder. Until it stops.

I saw on the news this morning that a girl in Ohio was arrested trying to break into a hospital in Cleveland with seven black candles and a knife. They showed her mug shot. Cardigan. Bags under her eyes. She was screaming something at the cameras. They muted it. You could read her lips, though, if you watched it a couple times.

I watched it a couple times.

She was screaming my name.

Then the news cut to a story about a lemon tree in Pittsburgh that bloomed overnight. First time in nine months. A little brick house in Brookline. My parents' house. My mom on the porch, holding a lemon, crying, while a reporter asked her how it felt.

"It's a miracle," the reporter said.

My mom said something I couldn't hear. The hum was too loud.

I changed the channel. I'm an only child. I always have been.

It's easier that way.
