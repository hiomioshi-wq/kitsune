Incarnate: Statue
A spinoff of the Corruption Saga, told by Tim. Book One, from the other side of the knife.

Trigger warning: This story contains the stabbing of a sister, a prophecy believed, hell, purgatory, being turned to stone, murder, grief, adult language, and a narrator who has had a very long time to think about whether he meant it, and has an answer.
Read at your own risk.



Chapter One: Plinth

There's a city in Purgatory made of statues.

You've been there, if you read Book One. Your crimes will be scattered over the city as statues, each showing how you hurt people. That's the rule of the place. Everything you ever did that you can't take back gets carved and set up on a street corner, life-size, in grey stone, so anybody who walks past can see.

Most of the statues are small. A man kicking a dog. A woman reading her sister's diary. A kid with a lighter by a shed.

Mine is on the main square. On a plinth. Big.

It's a boy in a kitchen, holding a knife, putting it into his sister.

That's me. I'm the statue. I don't mean I'm in it, or that it looks like me. I mean when they carved it, they carved me into it, and now I'm stone, and I'm on a plinth, and I've been standing in the middle of that square with my arm out and my sister on the end of it for longer than I was ever alive.

My name was Tim. Some people called me Modify, later. I was the anti-god of creation, later. None of that is carved. The statue doesn't care what you were later.

It only cares about the kitchen.



Chapter Two: Bullshit

Here's what I want you to know first, before the kitchen. So you have it.

I loved my sister.

Everyone says that. Every statue in this city would say it if it could talk. The guy kicking the dog loved the dog. But I need you to have it anyway.

When she was seven people started whispering at her. In the grocery store. In the car line at school. When the end begins, corruption will fall, and there shall be one with shining blonde hair, the beloved sister. They'd look at her hair. She had the brightest hair in our town. It was like a lamp.

And kids wrote it on her locker in marker. She will end everything. All of it.

So I got a Sharpie. A fat black one, the kind that bleeds through paper. And every morning before first bell I went to her locker and I crossed it out, every word, and underneath I wrote BULLSHIT, in capitals, and drew a little smiley face so she'd laugh.

She always laughed.

I did that for five years. I went through eleven Sharpies. I kept the dead ones in a shoebox under my bed. I don't know why. I guess I wanted proof.

I want that carved somewhere. It isn't. There's no statue in this city of a boy with a Sharpie. They only carve the bad ones.



Chapter Three: Sunday

Our parents died on a Sunday.

You know this. Pancakes. Dad burning them. Mom singing about a river. A woman in a white coat climbing in through the kitchen window, calm as a nurse, and our parents just stopping, like somebody turned them off, right there between the stove and the table.

I didn't see her face. I saw her back as she went out the window again. I saw her hand on the sill. That's all.

Ena saw her face.

Ena was fifteen. She stood at the stove with the spatula in her hand and pancake batter dripping off it onto the floor, and she looked at the window, and she didn't scream.

She smiled.

Not a big one. Not a happy one. Just the corners. Just for a second.

I've thought about that smile every day for longer than you've been alive. I think it was shock. People smile in shock. It's a thing, I looked it up later.

I think it was shock.



Chapter Four: The Glitches

After Sunday she started to go wrong.

Not all at once. Little things. She'd be doing homework at the kitchen table and her hand would flicker, like a bad video, there and then not there and then there again. She'd laugh at nothing. She'd stop in the middle of a sentence and her eyes would go red, just at the edges, and she'd say something in a voice that wasn't hers, low, like a drain.

Baby. Everybody's gonna watch.

And then she'd blink and be Ena again and say, "What? What'd I say?"

She had nightmares. She'd scream in her sleep. I'd go in and she'd be sitting up in bed shaking with her hands over her face saying, I saw myself killing people, Tim, I saw myself, I had a knife and I was killing everybody.

I'd hold her. I'd say it was a dream. I'd say it was bullshit.

I had stopped believing it was bullshit.

That's the thing I've never said. That's the thing the statue knows. Somewhere between the Sunday and the knife, I stopped crossing it out in my head.



Chapter Five: What People Said to Me

You don't know what it's like to be the brother.

Everybody looks at the sister. The prophecy's about the sister. But somebody has to live in the house with her. Somebody has to sleep down the hall.

People came to me. Grown-ups. Teachers. A priest, once, at the gas station. A woman from the town council who brought a casserole after the funeral and stood in the doorway and said, very quietly, holding my wrist so hard it left marks:

"You know what she is. Everybody knows. You're the only one close enough. If it starts, you're the only one who can stop it."

I said she was my sister.

The woman said, "That's what the prophecy says. The beloved sister. Who do you think it means beloved by?"

She left the casserole. It was tuna. Ena ate most of it. She said it was the best thing she'd had in months.

I couldn't eat any. I kept looking at her hands.



Chapter Six: The Kitchen

It was a Tuesday. I want that on the record. Not a Sunday. A Tuesday. Nothing special.

She was standing at the counter making a sandwich. Peanut butter. She had her back to me. Her hair was up in a clip.

And her whole body glitched.

Not her hand. All of her. She went out like a TV and came back, and when she came back she was turned around, facing me, and she was holding the butter knife, and her eyes were red all the way across, and she was smiling the corners smile. The Sunday smile.

And she said, in the drain voice:

"Hi, Timmy. You've been waiting for this."

I want to tell you I didn't think. That it was a reflex. That's what I told myself for a long time. That I saw red eyes and a knife and my body did it without me.

That's not true.

I thought. I had about one second, and I thought a whole sentence in it, very clearly, the way you think the answer on a test.

The sentence was: Finally.

Not, oh no. Not, Ena. Not, what's happening.

Finally. Here it is. I knew it. They were right. I was right to stop believing.

I was relieved.

There was a real knife in the block by the stove. The big one, for bread. I took it out. It came out of the block so easily. Like it had been waiting too.

She didn't fight. That's what I didn't tell anybody. She was holding a butter knife and she didn't even lift it. She just looked at me. And right as I got to her, right at the very end, the red went out of her eyes, all at once, like a light switching off, and it was just Ena. Just my sister. Blue eyes. Confused. Peanut butter on her thumb.

"Tim?" she said.

I'd already started. You can't stop a knife halfway. It doesn't work like that. I've had a long time to check.



Chapter Seven: In

That's the statue.

That exact second. The knife going in, and her eyes blue, and her mouth open on my name.

They carved it perfectly. They got the clip in her hair. They got the peanut butter on her thumb. They got my face, which is the part I can't stand, because my face in the statue is not scared, and it's not sad.

It's sure.

People stop in front of it. Purgatory has a lot of foot traffic. Damned souls on their way to wherever. They stop and look up at the boy and the girl on the plinth and they read the little plaque. It doesn't say my name. It says what the statue's for.

FULFILLED A PROPHECY HE ONCE CALLED BULLSHIT.

Some of them laugh.



Chapter Eight: Afterward

You know what happened after. Book One goes on for a long time.

She died. And then she didn't stay dead, because prophecies don't let you do that. She came back as the thing they'd all been whispering about. Corrupt. She ended the world. Just like they said. The grocery store people and the locker people and the casserole woman. All of them, gone, in the end she was supposed to bring.

I got to be right.

It was the worst thing that ever happened to me.

I became something too. The anti-god of creation. I made a helmet out of silver with an H on it, for hellbound. I fought her. I fought for her. She made me kill people. I made myself kill people. I joined a resistance. I lost a resistance. Famine was my friend for a while, which is a sentence I never thought I'd say.

And every time I saw Ena, every time, red or blue, monster or sister, I saw a butter knife she didn't lift.

She forgave me. Once. In hell, at the end of everything, she said I'd been through enough. She said, I hurt you too, a while back, when I was still a monster.

I let her say it. I nodded. I didn't tell her about the one second. About finally.

I've never told anybody.

I'm telling you because you're not anybody. You're a page.



Chapter Nine: How I Ended

Eri killed me. Not in a dramatic way. A portal. I was following her through one, and it closed, and I was on both sides of it, and then I wasn't on either.

There was nothing left of me to bury. That's true. Ena made a grave anyway. On the hill above what used to be our town. She carved the stone with a piece of rebar. The letters lean.

TIM.

BROTHER.

I'M SORRY.

ME TOO.

I can see it from here. Purgatory has a great view of everything you lost. She goes on Thursdays and sits with her back against it and tells it lies. Day forty-one. Day nine. She tells my stone she's clean. She tells my stone she's sorry.

She brings a cat. She named it after me. It's orange and it's a jerk and it sits on my stone like it owns it.

I'M SORRY. She carved that because she thinks she owes me an apology. For Corrupt. For hell. For the world.

ME TOO. She carved that because she thinks I'd say it back. That I'd be sorry about the kitchen. That if I could, I'd say, me too, Ena, I'm sorry, I was scared, it was a reflex, I never meant it.

She carved my half of the apology for me. With rebar. Over three days.

It's the kindest thing anyone has ever done for me, and it's a lie.



Chapter Ten: The Answer

So here's the answer. You've been waiting. I have too. I've had a very long time standing on this plinth to work it out, and I'm going to give it to you straight, because you came all this way.

Did I mean it?

Yes.

For one second, in a kitchen, on a Tuesday, I meant it completely. I believed every word they'd ever whispered. I picked the town over my sister, and the prophecy over the Sharpie, and the casserole woman over eleven dead markers in a shoebox. I looked at the brightest hair in our town and I thought, finally.

And the second after that, when her eyes went blue, I was sorry. I've been sorry ever since. Both of those are true. One doesn't cancel the other. That's what nobody tells you. You can be sorry forever and still have meant it.

The statue only got the first second.

That's fair. That's the one that counted.



Chapter Eleven: Day One

Something's happening.

The square is shaking. All the statues on all the corners are shaking. The man and the dog. The woman and the diary. The kid with the lighter. Little cracks running up their legs.

I can feel it all the way down here. A song. Red at the edges. Coming from somewhere far off, on an old highway, out past the ruins. And two words, in a voice I'd know anywhere.

Day one.

It's her. Of course it's her. She's finishing it. The sentence. All of it.

The plinth splits. My stone arm, the one with the knife, cracks at the elbow and drops off and smashes on the square, and for the first time in forever there's no knife in her. Just the stone girl, standing there on the plinth across from me, with her mouth open on my name and peanut butter on her thumb.

Everything's going out. Statues crumbling to sand. The city of Purgatory falling down like a sandcastle when the tide comes in.

And I've got maybe one second left. Same as the kitchen.

So I use it. I think a whole sentence in it, very clearly, the way you think the answer on a test. Not to anybody. To a stone girl with a clip in her hair. To a grave on a hill. To a cat.

Bullshit.

All of it. The prophecy. The whispers. The casserole. The knife. Me.

I'm crossing it out.

I don't have a Sharpie. I don't have hands. But I'm crossing it out anyway, every word, the way I did every morning before first bell.

And underneath, I'm drawing a little smiley face.

So she'll laugh.
