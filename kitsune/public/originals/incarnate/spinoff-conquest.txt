Chapter One: The First Horseman

Of the Four Horsemen of the Apocalypse, Conquest always rode first.

That was the order. It had been the order since the very first world ended, in the very first dimension, so long ago that nobody but the Horsemen remembered it. First Conquest, on a white horse, with a crown. Then War, on a red one, with a sword. Then Famine, on a black one, with a set of scales. Then Death, last of all, on a pale one, with nothing in her hands but an umbrella.

Conquest came first because conquest is how every ending begins. Somebody decides that something belongs to them. Somebody reaches out and takes it. And after that the fighting comes, and then the hunger, and then the stopping. But the taking comes first.

She was very good at it. She was the best there had ever been. She could ride into a city with a golden megaphone in one hand and the reins in the other, and say one word, and every person in that city would kneel.

SUBMIT.

That was her word. She had said it ten thousand times, in ten thousand cities, at the ends of ten thousand worlds. It never failed.

She was blonde, with a crown of thorns on her head that she had made herself, out of the first briar she ever conquered, and she was beautiful in the way a cold morning is beautiful, sharp and bright and impossible to look at for very long. And she was, though nobody knew it, though she would have taken the throat of anyone who said it out loud, the loneliest of the four.

Because the thing about conquering is that nobody ever chooses you.

They kneel. They obey. They give you their cities and their thrones and their crowns. But they do it because you said SUBMIT, and they could not do anything else. Not one of them, in ten thousand worlds, had ever looked at Conquest and decided, freely, of their own will: yes. You. I'll follow you.

She had never let herself think about that. Not once. Not in ten thousand years.

And then the world ended without her.


Chapter Two: The Apocalypse She Missed

She had been so ready.

She remembered that. Standing in the empty ruins of a dimension called Terra with her sisters, her megaphone in her hand, her white horse stamping, ready to ride. The apocalypse was coming. She could smell it. A girl with shining blonde hair. A prophecy. Corruption. Everything she was made for.

"I'm ready," she had said. "Let's go."

And Famine had said, quietly, "I'm not sure we should."

And War had said nothing, because War had already gone down into hell to fight something, and had not come back.

And Death had only stood there with her umbrella dripping, and looked at the empty ruins, and said:

"There's nobody here."

There wasn't. That was the thing. The apocalypse had already happened. A man named Tombstone had used a girl named Ena to corrupt the whole world, every person on it, before the Horsemen ever arrived. By the time the four of them rode in, there was nothing left to conquer. No cities. No thrones. No one to kneel.

Conquest had ridden through the empty streets of Terra with her megaphone raised, and said SUBMIT, and her voice had echoed back at her off a thousand empty buildings, and nobody had knelt.

There was nobody to kneel.

After that, she did not know what to do. The others found things. War found a quarry, eventually, and a girl. Famine found a boy in silver armor with an H on his chest, and helped him, and became the nexus, and stood holding up the world. Death found what Death always found, which was everyone, eventually.

Conquest found nothing.

She wandered. Through the broken world, through the restored one, through a dozen small dimensions at the edge of everything. Riding her white horse. Carrying her megaphone. Looking for something to take.

And everywhere she went, people already had what they needed. The restored world was full of people who had lost everything and were building it back, by hand, together. Nobody needed to be conquered. Nobody wanted to be. And for the first time in ten thousand years, the first Horseman of the Apocalypse began to wonder what she was for.


Chapter Three: The Town That Belonged to Nobody

The town was called Holloway, and it was the smallest town Conquest had ever seen.

It sat in a fold of brown hills at the edge of the restored world, where the land had been badly broken in the war and was only just beginning to heal. Twelve houses. A well. A schoolhouse with no roof. A field of thin, struggling wheat. Maybe sixty people, most of them refugees from cities that no longer existed, who had come here because nobody else wanted the land.

There was no mayor. There was no council. There was no lord, no queen, no Undersecretary for Public Calm. Nobody had ever claimed Holloway. Nobody had thought it was worth claiming.

Conquest rode into it on a grey afternoon, on her white horse, and stopped in the middle of the dirt road that ran between the twelve houses, and looked around.

Here, she thought. Here, at least. Something nobody else wants. I'll take it. I'll make it mine. And then I'll be what I'm for again.

She raised the golden megaphone.

The people of Holloway were coming out of their houses to look at her. A tall blonde woman on a white horse, with a crown of thorns, in the middle of their road. They did not look frightened. They looked mostly curious, and a little tired, the way everyone in the restored world looked a little tired.

"People of Holloway," said Conquest, and her voice through the megaphone rolled out over the brown hills like thunder. "I am Conquest. The first Horseman of the Apocalypse. This town is mine."

Nobody said anything.

"SUBMIT," said Conquest.

And sixty people in the dusty road of Holloway knelt.

They went down all at once, the way they always did. Knees in the dirt. Heads bowed. An old man with a cane. A woman with a baby on her hip. Three teenage boys who had been fixing a fence. The schoolteacher, still holding a piece of chalk. All of them kneeling, all of them silent, all of them hers.

Conquest lowered the megaphone. She waited for the feeling. The one she always felt, when a city knelt. The warm rush of it. The rightness. The sense of being exactly what she was meant to be.

It came.

It was smaller than she remembered.

And then a small voice, at the back of the crowd, said:

"Why?"


Chapter Four: The Girl Who Didn't Kneel

She was about nine.

She had a round face, and a gap between her front teeth, and dirt on her knees, and she was standing up. The only person in the whole town still standing. She was at the back of the crowd, by the roofless schoolhouse, holding a crown.

Not a real crown. A paper one. Yellow paper, cut into points, with stars drawn on it in purple crayon. The kind of crown a child makes on a rainy afternoon.

"Why?" she said again. "Why do we have to kneel?"

Conquest stared at her.

Nobody had ever not knelt. Not in ten thousand worlds. Not a king, not a general, not a god. The word SUBMIT went into you and bent your knees whether you wanted it to or not. That was what it was.

"Because I said so," said Conquest.

"But why?" said the girl. "You didn't say why. You just said this town is yours. It's not yours. You just got here. You haven't even been inside the schoolhouse." She frowned. "And there's no roof, so if you want to own it you have to fix the roof. That's what owning things is."

The whole town was kneeling around her in silence, heads bowed, unable to move. The girl stood in the middle of them like a tree in a field of wheat.

Conquest raised the megaphone again.

"SUBMIT," she said.

The girl did not kneel.

"No, thank you," she said politely.

Conquest lowered the megaphone very slowly.

She looked at the child. A small, round-faced, gap-toothed girl, with dirt on her knees and a paper crown in her hands. There was nothing special about her. No power. No glowing eyes. No Horseman inside her. She was just a girl.

"How are you doing that?" said Conquest.

"Doing what?"

"Not kneeling."

The girl thought about it.

"I don't know," she said. "My legs just don't want to. I didn't choose you." She held up the paper crown. "I'm making this for somebody I chose. So I can't kneel for somebody else. That wouldn't be fair."

Conquest looked at the paper crown.

"Who is it for?" she said, before she could stop herself.

The girl smiled with the gap in her teeth.

"I haven't decided yet," she said. "That's the best part. I get to pick."


Chapter Five: Owning Things

Conquest let the town up.

She did not know why. She simply lowered the megaphone, and the word went out of the sixty people in the road, and they stood up slowly, brushing the dirt off their knees, looking at each other and at her with bewildered faces.

"What was that?" said the old man with the cane.

"I'm Conquest," said Conquest. "I conquered you."

"Oh," said the old man. He considered this. "Well. Are you going to do anything with us?"

Conquest opened her mouth and found she did not know.

What did you do with a conquered town? In ten thousand worlds, she had never stayed long enough to find out. She conquered, and War came after her and fought, and Famine came after War and starved, and Death came last of all and stopped everything. That was the order. Conquest never had to do anything with what she took. By the time she had taken it, it was already ending.

But nothing was ending in Holloway. It had already ended, a long time ago, and these sixty people had walked out of the ruins and started again.

"The schoolhouse has no roof," said the girl with the paper crown, coming up beside the white horse. "You could start with that."

The schoolteacher, a thin young man with chalk on his fingers, said nervously, "Nell, don't bother the Horseman—"

"She said she owns us," said Nell. "If you own something, you have to take care of it. That's the rule." She looked up at Conquest. "Right?"

Conquest looked down at her.

Ten thousand worlds. She had never once taken care of anything.

"I don't know how to fix a roof," she said.

"I'll show you," said Nell. "It's not hard. Mr. Ambry showed me. You need wood and nails and you need to not fall off."

And that was how the first Horseman of the Apocalypse ended up on top of a roofless schoolhouse in a town at the edge of the restored world, with a hammer in one hand and a mouthful of nails, while a nine-year-old girl with a paper crown stood on the ground below and shouted instructions.

"No! The other way! You're putting it in upside down!"

"There is no upside down to a board."

"There is if you're doing it wrong!"


Chapter Six: The Order of Things

She stayed.

It was not a decision. She simply did not leave. Every morning she woke up in the empty room behind the schoolhouse, where the teacher let her sleep, and every morning there was something that needed doing. The roof. Then the well, which had a crack in its wall. Then the fence around the wheat field, which the three teenage boys had been trying to fix for a month without enough wood.

Conquest found wood. It turned out that if you rode a white horse into the next valley and said SUBMIT to a forest, the trees did not kneel, but the woodcutter who lived there was very willing to trade lumber for a ride on the horse.

She did not use the megaphone in Holloway again. She did not decide not to. She just never needed it. When she wanted the teenage boys to help with the fence, she asked them. When she wanted the old man with the cane to stop trying to lift heavy things, she asked him. When she wanted Nell to stop following her around shouting instructions, she asked her, and Nell said no, and kept following her.

It was the strangest month of her very long life.

At night, she lay awake in the room behind the schoolhouse and thought about the order of things. First Conquest, then War, then Famine, then Death. The taking, then the fighting, then the hunger, then the stopping. It had always been that way. It was what she was made of.

But here, in Holloway, there was no fighting after her. There was no hunger. There was no stopping. She had taken the town, and then she had fixed its roof, and then the town had kept going.

"What comes after Conquest," she said to the dark one night, "if nothing ends?"

And a voice from the doorway, low and rough and very old, said:

"Building."

Conquest sat up.

A thin pale girl was standing in the doorway of the little room. Grey boots. A mended coat. One brown eye and one dark red one.

"War," said Conquest.

"Sister," said War, through the girl's mouth. "You've been gone a long time."


Chapter Seven: Sisters

"How did you find me?" said Conquest.

"Famine felt you," said War. "She feels everyone. She said one of the Horsemen was in a town at the edge of the world doing something she'd never felt a Horseman do before. She sent me to check." The red eye looked around the small room. At the hammer on the floor. The sawdust on the blanket. The yellow paper crown, which Nell had left on the windowsill that afternoon and forgotten. "What are you doing, sister?"

"Fixing a well," said Conquest.

There was a long silence.

Then the girl in the doorway, who was not War, who was only the girl War lived in, a quiet seventeen-year-old named Pia, laughed out loud. She clapped her hand over her mouth immediately, but it was too late.

"Sorry," said Pia. "Sorry. It's just. You're the first Horseman. You're Conquest. And you're fixing a well."

"I'm aware," said Conquest stiffly.

"Why?" said War.

Conquest did not answer for a long time.

"Because a girl asked me why," she said at last. "And I didn't have an answer. Ten thousand worlds, and I never once had an answer. I said SUBMIT, and people knelt, and I thought that meant they were mine. But they weren't mine. They were just—stuck. Like flies on a ribbon. Not one of them ever chose me." She looked at the paper crown on the windowsill. "And then a nine-year-old stood up in the middle of a road, and said she couldn't kneel, because she was making a crown for somebody she'd chosen. And I realized I'd never been chosen. Not once. By anybody. And I wanted to know what that felt like."

War was quiet for a long time.

"I know what it feels like," she said finally. Her voice was very low. "Pia chose me. Not at first. At first I made a deal with her when she was dying. But later. After. She could have made me leave, and she didn't. She chose to keep me." The red eye flickered. "It's the best thing that's ever happened to me, sister. In ten thousand years of wars. Being chosen."

Conquest looked at her sister. At the girl her sister lived in.

"How do you get chosen?" she asked. It came out very small. "I don't know how. I only know how to take."

War thought about it.

"You stop asking them to kneel," she said. "And you start asking them what they want. And then you help them get it. Even if it's a roof. Even if it's a well." She paused. "And then you wait. And you don't take anything. And maybe one day somebody gives you something you didn't ask for."

Conquest's eyes went to the paper crown on the windowsill.

"And if they don't?" she said.

"Then you still fixed a well," said War. "That's not nothing."


Chapter Eight: The Raid

The raiders came at the end of summer.

They were not monsters. Conquest almost wished they were. They were men, twenty of them, on horses, from a burned-out city in the west. They had lost everything in the war, the way everyone had, and instead of building it back, they had decided to take it from people who had built theirs. They had been riding through the edge of the restored world for a year, taking. Wheat. Water. Horses. Whatever a town had, they took it.

Conquest saw them coming from the roof of the schoolhouse, where she was replacing a broken shingle. Twenty riders. Dust rising behind them. Riding fast.

She knew exactly what they were. She had been them, in ten thousand worlds. She knew the way they sat their horses. She knew the look in their eyes. They had decided that something belonged to them.

She climbed down off the roof.

The people of Holloway were coming out of their houses. The old man with the cane. The woman with the baby. The three teenage boys. The schoolteacher with chalk on his hands. Nell, with her paper crown, which she had finally finished, and which she now wore on her own head for safekeeping until she decided.

They looked at the dust on the horizon. Then they looked at Conquest.

"What do we do?" said the schoolteacher.

Conquest knew what she could do. She could ride out to meet the raiders with her megaphone, and say SUBMIT, and all twenty of them would kneel in the dust. And then they would be hers. And she could make them leave. And they would never come back.

It would be so easy. It was the easiest thing in the world. It was what she was.

And then they would be stuck. Like flies on a ribbon. Twenty men who had never chosen to leave, who would leave anyway, because a Horseman said so. And they would go and take from some other town. Because nothing would have changed in them. Nothing at all.

"What do you want to do?" said Conquest.

The schoolteacher stared at her.

"What?"

"I'm asking you," said Conquest. "All of you. It's your town. You built it. What do you want to do?"

There was a long, frightened silence in the dusty road.

Then the old man with the cane said, slowly, "I don't want to give them our wheat. We worked so hard on that wheat."

"I don't want to fight them," said the woman with the baby. "Somebody'll get hurt."

"I want them to leave," said one of the teenage boys.

"I want them to stop," said Nell, from under her paper crown. "Taking things. I want them to stop for good. Not just from us. From everybody."

Conquest looked down at her.

"Okay," she said. "Then let's do that."


Chapter Nine: What Conquest Is For

She rode out to meet them alone, on her white horse, without the megaphone.

The raiders reined in on the hill above Holloway, twenty of them, dust settling around their horses. Their leader was a big man with a scarred face and a hard, tired look in his eyes. He looked at the blonde woman on the white horse with the crown of thorns, and his hand went to the sword on his hip.

"Who are you?" he said.

"Conquest," said Conquest. "The first Horseman of the Apocalypse."

The raiders murmured. The leader's hand tightened on his sword.

"You going to make us kneel?" he said. "We've heard about you. One word, they say, and everybody drops."

"I could," said Conquest.

"Then do it," said the leader, and there was something in his voice that was almost a dare and almost a plea. "Do it and get it over with. Make us kneel. Make us leave. We've been riding for a year. We're tired. Go on."

Conquest looked at him for a long time.

She knew that voice. She had heard it in her own head, in the empty ruins of Terra, when the apocalypse happened without her. The voice of somebody who had lost the thing they were made for, and kept taking because they did not know what else to do.

"No," she said.

The leader blinked.

"No?"

"I'm not going to make you kneel," said Conquest. "I've done that ten thousand times. It never changes anything. You'd leave this town, and you'd go take from the next one. Because nobody ever asked you what you actually wanted." She leaned forward on her horse. "So I'm asking. What do you want?"

The leader stared at her.

"What do I want?"

"You lost a city," said Conquest. "I can see it on you. You had a home, and the war took it, and now you take other people's homes, because if you can't have one, nobody should. I know. I was the first Horseman. I've been taking things since before your world was born. I know exactly what it looks like." She paused. "So what do you want? Not what do you want to take. What do you want?"

The silence stretched out across the brown hills.

Behind the leader, one of the raiders, a young one, barely older than the teenage boys in the town, said quietly:

"I want to go home."

"There's no home," said the leader roughly. "It burned."

"I know," said the young raider. "I just want one."

Conquest turned and looked down the hill at Holloway. Twelve houses. A well, no longer cracked. A schoolhouse with a new roof. A field of wheat, thin but growing. Sixty people standing in the dusty road, watching. And at the front of them, a nine-year-old girl in a yellow paper crown.

"That town has room," said Conquest. "There are empty houses at the north end. The roofs need fixing. The wheat needs more hands. It won't be the city you lost. But it could be a home. If you want it. If you build it."

The leader followed her gaze.

"They'd let us?" he said. "After we came here to take from them?"

"I don't know," said Conquest. "I'm not going to make them. You'd have to ask."


Chapter Ten: Chosen

They asked.

It took a long time. It was a long, hard, awkward conversation in the middle of the dusty road, between twenty tired raiders and sixty tired townspeople, with the first Horseman of the Apocalypse standing off to the side, saying nothing, holding her horse's reins. There was shouting. There was crying. The old man with the cane said a great many things about his wheat that could not be repeated in front of children. The woman with the baby said she didn't trust them, and she had every reason not to.

But at the end of it, five of the raiders stayed.

The young one, who wanted to go home. Two brothers who had been carpenters before the war. A woman who had been a midwife. And, to everyone's surprise, the leader, the big man with the scarred face, who stood in the road for a long time after the others had ridden away, looking at the empty houses at the north end of town, and then said, gruffly, to nobody in particular:

"Which one needs a roof worst?"

The rest rode on. Conquest did not stop them. She did not know whether they would keep taking. Maybe they would. Maybe one day they would find another town, and another Horseman, or another girl in a paper crown, and stop.

That night, she sat on the steps of the schoolhouse and watched the stars come out over Holloway. The new roof was solid over her head. The well was full. At the north end of the town, someone was hammering, late into the dark, fixing a roof for a man who had come here to take and had decided, instead, to stay.

Nell came and sat down beside her. She was still wearing the yellow paper crown.

"You didn't use your megaphone," said Nell.

"No."

"Why not?"

Conquest thought about it.

"Because you asked me why," she said. "On the first day. And I didn't have an answer. And I've been looking for one all summer." She looked up at the stars. "I think the answer is: there isn't a good reason. Not for making people kneel. There's only ever been one reason, and it's a bad one."

"What is it?"

"That I was scared," said Conquest quietly, "that nobody would ever choose me. So I made sure they never got the chance."

Nell was quiet for a while.

Then she reached up and took the yellow paper crown off her own head. The one she had been making all summer. The one with purple crayon stars. The one she had been saving for somebody she chose.

She held it out.

"Here," she said.

Conquest stared at it.

"That's yours," she said. "That's for somebody you pick."

"I picked," said Nell. "I decided today. When you rode up the hill without your megaphone." She pushed it into Conquest's hands. "You didn't take it. I'm giving it to you. That's different. That's the whole point."

Conquest sat on the steps of the schoolhouse in the dark, holding a paper crown.

Ten thousand worlds. Ten thousand cities. Ten thousand crowns, gold and silver and jeweled, taken from the heads of kings and queens who knelt in the dust because she said one word.

She had never once been given one.

Slowly, very slowly, she reached up and took off the crown of thorns. The one she had made herself, out of the first briar she ever conquered, ten thousand years ago. It came away from her hair with a small dry sound, like a dead branch snapping.

She set it down on the step beside her.

And then she put on the paper crown.

It did not fit very well. It was too small, and one of the points was bent, and the purple crayon stars were a little smudged. It was the most beautiful thing she had ever worn.

"How do I look?" she asked.

Nell looked at her, head tilted, very seriously.

"Like a Horseman," she said. "But a good one."


Chapter Eleven: The Order of Things, Again

Of the Four Horsemen of the Apocalypse, Conquest always rode first.

She still does. That has not changed. When a world begins to end, somewhere in the vast multiverse, Conquest is still the first to arrive, on her white horse, with her golden megaphone.

But she does not say SUBMIT anymore.

She rides into the dying city, and she gets down off her horse, and she finds the people who are still there, the frightened ones, the angry ones, the ones who have decided that something belongs to them and are about to reach out and take it. And she asks them what they want.

It does not always work. Sometimes the world ends anyway. Sometimes War comes after her and fights, and Famine comes after War and starves, and Death comes last of all with her umbrella and stops everything, the way it has always been.

But sometimes, now, the order changes.

First Conquest. Then nobody. Then building.

She wears a yellow paper crown with purple crayon stars, bent at one point and too small for her head. It has been mended many times, with tape, and glue, and once, after a very hard battle, with string. She will not take it off. If anyone asks her why the first Horseman of the Apocalypse wears a paper crown, she tells them:

"Because somebody gave it to me."

And if they ask why that matters, she tells them about a town called Holloway, at the edge of the restored world, with twelve houses and then seventeen, a well that does not crack, a schoolhouse with a good roof, and a girl with a gap in her teeth who stood up in the middle of a dusty road when everyone else was kneeling, and asked why.

The crown of thorns is still on the schoolhouse steps, where she left it. Nobody has moved it. The children of Holloway call it the Horseman's crown, and dare each other to touch it, and nobody ever does.

But every spring, Nell, who is grown now, and teaches in the schoolhouse with the good roof, goes out to the steps and pulls the weeds from around it, and leaves it where it is. A reminder. Of the thing that was taken, once, and put down.

And of the thing that was given instead.
