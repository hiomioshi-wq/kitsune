Incarnate: Chalk
A spinoff of the Corruption Saga

Trigger warning: This story contains occult horror, memory loss, a missing sister, a human sacrifice, adult language, and a narrator who decides that one person is an acceptable price.
Read at your own risk.



Chapter One: Notes To Myself

I found the first note on my bathroom mirror. In chalk. In my handwriting.

HIS NAME IS MARK. YOU KNOW HIM. HE TOOK IT FROM YOU.

I stood there with my toothbrush in my mouth for maybe five minutes. Then I spat and I went through my whole room.

There were nine more.

Under my mattress: THE BARN. TANNER BARN. YOU WERE THERE. YOU WON.

Inside my left boot: CALYPSO IS NOT A GIRL. YOU KNEW THAT BEFORE ANYONE.

Taped under my desk drawer, on an index card, chalk smudged: IF YOU ARE READING THIS HE MADE YOU FORGET. HE HAS THE SONG. HE KEPT IT. YOU KNEW HE WOULD. THAT'S WHY YOU WROTE THESE. DON'T PANIC. YOU'RE SMARTER THAN HIM. YOU'RE SMARTER THAN ALL OF THEM.

The last one was in my grandmother's Bible, in the back, on the page where she wrote down all the births and deaths in the family. Under my sister's name. Under Wynn.

IT'S HUNGRY AGAIN. YOU KNOW WHAT IT WANTS.

I didn't remember writing any of them.

I sat on my floor surrounded by little white notes in my own handwriting and I laughed, because what else are you supposed to do. And then I cried, because I didn't know who I was crying for. And then I stopped, because that's what we do in my family. We stop. We get up. We go to the barn.



Chapter Two: What My Family Is

My grandmother called it the Tenant.

It lives under the Tanner barn on Route 9. It was there before the barn. It was there before the town. It was there before the people who lived here before the town, who had their own name for it, which my grandmother wouldn't say out loud because she said it still answered.

It's a hunger. Like Calypso, sort of. But Calypso's a tourist. Calypso moves around. The Tenant stays. It's been in that dirt so long it's part of the dirt, and every hundred years or so, it gets loud.

My family keeps it quiet.

That's the deal. My great-great-grandmother made it, back when the barn was new and the Tanners were still alive to own it. We feed the Tenant a little. It gives us a little. We get the chalk and the words and the knowing. We get to see things like Calypso for what they are. We get to fight them.

And it gets a price.

My grandmother paid with her sight. Her left eye at twenty, her right eye at forty. She said she could see better without them.

My mother refused to pay. She moved us to the city when I was six, and she drank, and she wouldn't talk about it, and when I was nine she moved us back because the Tenant had started to whisper to her through the drains and she said she'd rather be close to it than chased by it.

My sister paid when she was fourteen.

Wynn. She was three years older than me. She taught me the first circles. She taught me the old word for no. She went out to the barn one night in October, because it was getting loud, and somebody had to, and Mom wouldn't. She left a note on the kitchen table in chalk.

SOMEBODY HAS TO. LOVE YOU. DON'T COME.

I went anyway. I was eleven. I found her chalk circle in the middle of the barn floor, perfect, finished. I found her shoes inside it. That's all.

The Tenant was quiet for six years after that.

Then Calypso came to town.



Chapter Three: The Gap

I know there was a barn fight. I know it because my body knows. There's a scar on my palm from a vial I don't remember breaking. My nose bled for three days. My ribs are bruised in the shape of a closet door.

I know there was a boy, because I wrote it down nine times.

I don't remember his face. When I try, there's just static. A gray buzz where a person should be. Like a TV between channels. I can feel the edges of it, the shape of a hole, the exact outline of someone who used to be in my head, and every time I reach for the middle, my hand goes through.

He did it well. I'll give him that. Clean. No splinters.

I went to school on Monday, and I walked down B Hall, and I looked at every boy's face, trying to find the one that hurt to look at. None of them did. That's the point. He took the hurt too.

Brad Okafor wasn't at school. People said he was sick. People said he'd stopped talking, except for one sentence. People said his mom had a priest over and the priest left fast.

Devon Krause was in Saint Agnes's psych ward, apologizing to the ceiling.

Mark Ellison was gone. Took his dad's car. His dad was telling everyone he was proud of him. Over and over. To the mailman. To the cops. To the church ladies who came over with a casserole. Love you, proud of you, love you, proud of you.

Mark Ellison. That's the name from the notes.

I said it out loud in the hallway. Mark. Mark Ellison. Nothing. Static.

But my chalk hand twitched.



Chapter Four: It's Loud

It was loud that night.

I could hear it from my bed. Two miles away. Under the barn. Under the dirt. A sound like a huge animal breathing through its nose, slow and wet, in and out. Mom was asleep on the couch with the TV on. The drains in the house were gurgling in rhythm with it. In and out.

The barn fight cracked something. That's my guess. All that song. All that Calypso, pouring into the barn, the walls going soft and crawling, everything underground coming up to listen. The Tenant heard it. The Tenant got a taste. My circles held it down, barely, but it's awake now, and it's awake hungry.

And somewhere out west, the boy who erased me is driving toward the girl who made him, and when they meet, I don't think it's going to be a reunion. I think it's going to be a feast. Two hungers in one place, feeding each other. Bigger every day.

I can't stop that with chalk. I barely stopped Calypso alone and I had help. I had help I can't remember.

I need more.

The Tenant has more.

I got up. Put on my boots. The one with the note was still in it. CALYPSO IS NOT A GIRL. I didn't take it out. I walked the two miles to Route 9 in the dark with my backpack on, and the whole way the drains in the street breathed with me.



Chapter Five: The Price

I sat in the middle of the barn in a fresh circle, in my grandmother's words, with my sister's shoes in my lap. I kept them. Don't judge me.

I said the old name. The one my grandmother wouldn't say.

The floor of the barn went warm.

The Tenant doesn't have a voice. It doesn't need one. It talks the way dirt talks, by pressing. It pressed. Up through the boards. Through my knees. Into my head. And what it pressed was a feeling, and the feeling was: yes. Finally. One of you came.

I said, "I need to be strong enough to stop them. Both of them."

It pressed: Yes.

"How much?"

And it showed me.

Not an eye. Not a voice. Not a sister's shoes. It showed me a person. It wanted a whole one. It had gotten Wynn, and it had liked that, the whole of somebody, and it wanted the whole of somebody again. That's what my family had been doing wrong, it said, in its pressing way. Little bits. Eyes and years. Stingy. Give it one whole person, and it would give me everything it had, and it had so, so much.

"Me?" I said.

It pressed: No. You're the door. We need the door.

"Then who?"

And it showed me a picture. A boy on his knees, in a white room, saying sorry to a ceiling.



Chapter Six: Saint Agnes

I want to tell you I said no. I want to tell you I walked out of the barn and went home and found another way.

I went to Saint Agnes's on Wednesday.

It's two towns over. The psych ward is on the fourth floor. Visiting hours are two to four. I told the front desk I was his cousin. I didn't even need chalk for that. Nobody checks. Nobody wants to visit a boy who says sorry to the ceiling.

Devon was in a room by himself. Big kid. Linebacker shoulders. He'd lost weight. They'd shaved his head for some reason. He was sitting on the bed in a hospital gown, staring at the corner where the wall met the ceiling, and his lips were moving.

"I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

His eyes were blue at the edges.

I sat in the plastic chair next to the bed. I watched him for a long time.

I knew who Devon Krause was. Everybody did. He'd held a kid's head in a toilet in sixth grade. He'd broken a girl's wrist in eighth grade because she laughed at him. He'd called me a devil-worshipping freak every single day of freshman year, and once he'd taken my backpack and dumped it in the creek, and all my chalk had melted, and I had to walk home through town knowing that if something came, I couldn't stop it.

And he was gone already. That's what I told myself. Look at him. Look at his eyes. There's nobody in there. The boy who erased me hollowed him out. Whatever made Devon Devon got sung out of him in a cafeteria two weeks ago. This is a body that apologizes. That's all.

It's not like he's using it.

That's the math. I'll say it out loud so somebody hears it. I did the math, and the math said: one hollow boy who used to be cruel, in exchange for stopping two monsters who will hollow thousands.

It's a good trade. Any general would make it. Any god would.

I took out a piece of chalk.

"Devon," I said.

He kept staring at the ceiling. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

"I know," I said. "Me too."

And then, I swear, for half a second, his eyes moved. Off the corner. Down. To me. And something came up behind them, way down, like a fish coming up to the surface of a dark pond. Something scared. Something that recognized me. Something that was still in there.

"Raven?" he said. In his own voice. Tiny.

I drew the circle around his bed anyway.



Chapter Seven: Delivery

I'm not going to describe all of it.

The circle in chalk on the linoleum around his bed. The words. The window that was supposed to be locked opening on its own. The cold air. The smell of a barn two towns away, dirt and old hay and something underneath that wasn't either.

Devon didn't fight. He couldn't. The song in him held him still. That made it easier. I'd thank Mark Ellison for that, if I could remember his face.

The floor under the bed went warm. Then soft. Then it opened, the linoleum, like a mouth, and it wasn't the floor of Saint Agnes's fourth floor down there anymore. It was dirt. It was the dark under the Tanner barn. It was breathing.

Devon went down slowly. Like sinking in a pool. He kept saying sorry the whole way.

"I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I'm sorry."

And then, right at the end, just as his face went under: "Raven, I'm sorry."

The floor closed. Linoleum again. Clean. Not even a chalk mark left. The bed was neatly made. Like nobody had ever been in it.

I sat in the plastic chair for the rest of visiting hours, until two to four was over. Then I went home.



Chapter Eight: Everything It Has

It came that night.

Not to the barn. To me. Into me. While I was asleep. The way it always does with my family, I think, through the drains, through the floor, up through the mattress and into my back.

I woke up and the world was different.

I could see everything. I mean everything. I could see the hunger in every person in town like a little flame, like pilot lights in a row of stoves. I could see where Calypso had been, a smear of blue across the map in my head going west. I could see where the boy had been, a thinner blue line chasing the thicker one. Mark Ellison. Still static, his face. But I could see his trail now. I could follow it to the end of the world.

I could see Wynn.

That was the part it didn't tell me about. It showed me Wynn, because Wynn was in it. Wynn was part of it now, the way Devon was part of it now, the way everyone it had ever been fed was still in there, pressed down in the dark under the dirt. I could feel her. Fourteen forever. Still saying the word for no.

And I could feel Devon, newer, louder, still saying sorry.

They were in me now too. Riding along. Everything it has, it said. You wanted everything.

I looked in the bathroom mirror. The note was still there. HIS NAME IS MARK. YOU KNOW HIM. HE TOOK IT FROM YOU.

My eyes were black. Not dark. Black. All the way across. Like a barn floor at night.

I wiped the note off with my hand and the chalk didn't smear. It soaked into my skin. Into me. Like it was going home.



Chapter Nine: Going West

I packed my backpack. Chalk. Grandma's Bible. Wynn's shoes.

Mom was on the couch. She woke up when I walked past. She looked at me. At my eyes.

She didn't scream. My family doesn't scream. She just put her hand over her mouth and said, behind it, "Oh, baby. What did you give it?"

"Nobody," I said. "Nobody who was using it."

She started crying. She didn't stop me. She never stopped anybody.

I walked out to Route 9. I didn't need a car. The ground went with me. Every step I took, the dirt under it knew me, and pushed, and the miles went by underneath like a conveyor belt, like the whole country was a floor and I was standing on the part that moved.

I'm going to find them. Calypso and the boy with the static face. I'm going to stop them. That's the plan. That's what I paid for. That's what Devon paid for.

But here's the thing I didn't think about, sitting in the plastic chair in Saint Agnes doing my math.

The Tenant isn't a weapon. It's a hunger. Same as them. It's just bigger and older and slower and it lives in the ground instead of a cheerleader. And I'm the door. That's what it said. You're the door. We need the door.

When I find them, I'm going to open.

And everything that comes through is going to be hungry, and it's not going to stop at two.

I can feel the pilot lights in every town I pass. Little flames. Hundreds. Thousands. The Tenant is watching them through my eyes. Pressing. Patient. It's been under one barn for ten thousand years. It's never been able to travel.

I know what my sister would say. She'd say the old word for no.

I don't say it. I keep walking. I tell myself it's because I have to stop them, and that's true, and also, underneath it, in the dark part where Devon is still apologizing, there's something else, something I'll only say once, here, to you.

It feels good. Being strong enough. Finally.

I think that's how it gets everybody.
