Chapter One: What the Nexus Feels

Famine could feel everyone in the world being hungry.

That was the part nobody told you about being the nexus. They told you it was an honor. They told you the whole restored world would hang from you like a coat from a hook, and that as long as you stood there in the black glass hollow at the bottom of the red canyon, nothing would fall apart. They told you it would be quiet. Restful, even. A long, still watch.

They did not tell you that the nexus was the place where everything in the world touched everything else. That standing at the center of it was like standing at the center of a spider's web, feeling every thread. That every heartbeat in the restored world ran through you. Every breath. Every want.

Every hunger.

And Famine was hunger. That was what she was made of. The second of the Four Horsemen to ride out at the end of every world, the one who came after War and before Death. The empty belly. The failed harvest. The cupboard with nothing in it.

She had been standing in the hollow for three years now, and she could feel all of it.

A baby crying for milk in a grey town in the east. Hungry. A soldier on a night watch on the northern wall, stomach growling. Hungry. A girl with strawberry-pink eyes, working the early shift at a bakery, so tired she forgot to eat breakfast. Hungry. An old man alone in a big house, who had not wanted to eat anything since his wife died. Hungry. A woman with silver hair, standing at the foot of a white tower, not for food, but for her brother. Hungry, hungry, hungry.

Famine stood in the black glass with her arms open and felt it all, every second, like rain on her skin.

She did not mind, most days. It was what she was for. She understood hunger better than anyone in any dimension, and she would rather feel it than not. It reminded her that everyone was alive.

But there was one hunger she felt every single night, at the same time, just after midnight, that she could not stand.

It was her own.


Chapter Two: Drake

His name was Drake.

He had been Tim's best friend. Before the corruption, before the anti-gods, before any of it. Two boys in a grey city on Terra, throwing rocks at the river, talking about nothing. Drake was the loud one. Drake was the one who laughed first and loudest at everything. Drake was the one who, when the whole city started whispering about Tim's little sister and the prophecy, walked up to the biggest whisperer in the school and punched him in the mouth.

Famine knew all this because she had read it in Tim's mind, later, when she was helping him. Tim thought about Drake almost as often as he thought about his sister.

She had met Drake in hell.

He had been corrupted by then. Ena, Corrupt, had taken him, the way she took everyone. Half his armor was black and glitching with red. His eyes were pupilless. He fought for Corrupt the way a puppet dances. But every so often, in the middle of a battle, something would flicker in his face, and for half a second he would look at Tim, and Famine would see the boy who threw rocks at the river, trapped behind the red, screaming.

Famine had been hungry, then. So hungry. She was a Horseman of the Apocalypse, and the apocalypse had happened without her, and she had come down into hell to find out why, and she had found a ruined world and an army of the corrupted and a boy in silver armor trying to save his sister. She had decided to help him. She had never decided to help anyone before. It made her feel strange.

And she had been so hungry.

When Corrupt sent Drake at them, in the burning rocks of hell, Famine had done the only thing a Horseman of Famine knows how to do in a fight. She had starved him. Reached into the corruption in him and pulled, hard, trying to eat it out of him, trying to leave nothing behind but the boy.

But she had been so hungry. Three thousand years of being a Horseman, and she had never once been fed. And when she started pulling, she could not stop.

She ate the corruption. Then she kept eating.

She ate Drake's head.

That was how Tim's mind remembered it, afterward, in plain words, the way minds sometimes remember the worst things in the plainest possible way. Famine eats Drake's head, killing Drake. Famine feels guilty. Three short lines. Like a report.

It had not felt like three lines. It had felt like the end of the world. Her world. The one she had not even known she had.

Tim forgave her. He said it was the corruption, that Drake would have died anyway, that she had been trying to save him. He said it many times, in hell, and afterward, in the broken world, and at the end. Tim was very good at forgiving people. He forgave his sister for a whole apocalypse.

Famine had never forgiven herself.

And every night, just after midnight, standing in the black glass at the center of the world, she felt it again. The moment she could not stop eating. The taste of it.

And underneath it, the hunger. Her own. The one that had never once in three thousand years been fed.


Chapter Three: The Girl With the Umbrella

The visitor came on the third anniversary of Famine becoming the nexus.

She came down the canyon path just after midnight, which was when nobody ever came. The guards at the top did not stop her. They did not see her. Nobody ever saw her, unless she wanted them to.

She was very small. A girl, maybe twelve, maybe a thousand. She wore a plain dark coat and carried an umbrella, black and old-fashioned, with a curved wooden handle. It was not raining. It was never raining in the canyon. But the umbrella was open anyway, held over her head, and from the tips of its spokes, very slowly, drops of something dark and clear were falling. Leaking. Landing on the red rock with small soft sounds, like a clock ticking.

Famine knew her at once.

"Death," she said.

"Hello, sister," said Death.

She came to the edge of the black glass hollow and stopped. She did not step onto the glass. Death never stepped onto anything she did not mean to end.

"You look tired," said Death.

"I'm the nexus," said Famine. "I'm holding up the world. Of course I look tired. What are you doing here? Nobody's dying. I'd feel it."

"Somebody's always dying," said Death mildly. "But no. I'm not here for that." She tilted the umbrella back a little, so that Famine could see her face. It was small and pale and very calm, with eyes the color of deep water. "I'm here because of what you feel every night. Just after midnight. I can feel it too, you know. It's very loud."

Famine went very still.

"That's private."

"Nothing's private from the Horsemen," said Death. "We're all made of the same thing. The end of the world. You're the hunger at the end. War's the fighting. Conquest's the taking. I'm the stopping." She looked at Famine with her deep-water eyes. "And you've been carrying a stopping for three years that isn't yours to carry. I came to take it."

"You can't take it."

"I'm Death," said Death. "I take everything. Eventually."

Famine laughed. It was not a nice laugh.

"You want to take Drake?" she said. "Go ahead. You already did. He's gone. I made sure of that. I ate—" Her voice broke. She did not finish.

Death was quiet for a long time, with her umbrella dripping.

"No," she said at last. "That's the thing, sister. That's why I'm here. He isn't gone. Not all the way." She stepped closer to the edge of the glass. "Everything that's killed gets sent somewhere. You know that. And Drake was killed by a Horseman, in hell, while he was corrupted. Do you know where that sends you?"

Famine knew. Every anti-god in every dimension knew.

"The Gray Abyss," she whispered.

"The Gray Abyss," said Death. "He's been there three years. Waiting. A statue among the statues. And every night, just after midnight, at the exact moment you feel it, he feels it too." The umbrella dripped. "He's hungry too, Famine. And he's been waiting for you to come and feed him."


Chapter Four: The Nexus Can't Move

"I can't go," said Famine.

"I know," said Death.

"I'm the nexus. If I step out of the glass, the world falls apart. Everything. The towns, the people, the girl at the bakery, the baby crying for milk. All of it."

"I know," said Death again.

"Then why did you tell me?" Famine's voice cracked into something rough and raw. "Why would you come down here and tell me he's waiting for me, when you know I can't go to him? Do you know what that's like? To be the hunger at the end of every world, and stand here holding up a world you can't leave, while the one person you—"

She stopped.

The one person you what? She did not know how to end the sentence. She had not known Drake. Not really. She had fought him, and starved him, and killed him. She had read him in Tim's memories. A loud boy who threw rocks at the river and punched a bully in the mouth. That was all she knew.

But he was the only person she had ever killed who she had been trying to save.

"I told you," said Death gently, "because you're not the only one who can carry the world."

Famine looked at her.

"There are others," said Death. "You know there are. You've felt them. Every hunger in the world runs through you. Including the ones that want to help." She tilted her umbrella toward the top of the canyon, toward the rim, where the first faint grey light of morning was just beginning. "You just never asked."


Chapter Five: Asking for Help

It turned out Famine did not know how to ask for help.

She had never once done it. In three thousand years. Horsemen did not ask. Horsemen rode. They came at the end of worlds, and they did what they were made for, and they left. You did not ask the hunger at the end of the world if she needed anything. The question did not make sense.

But Death stood at the edge of the glass all night, with her umbrella dripping, and did not leave. And when the morning came, and Rui came down the canyon path for her monthly visit, with a bag of oranges in one hand and her sister Tsui beside her, Famine opened her mouth.

And nothing came out.

"You look terrible," said Rui, stopping at the edge of the glass. "Are you cold again? Is it the freezing? I can light a fire—"

"No," said Famine. "It's not the cold."

Rui waited. Tsui waited. Death, invisible to both of them, stood beside them with her umbrella and waited too.

Famine stared at the fire anti-god and the water anti-god, the two sisters who had come down into this canyon two years ago and fed her the only real meal she had ever had. Who came every month and told her stories. Who were, she realized, slowly, the closest thing she had ever had to friends.

"I need," she said. And stopped. The word was so strange in her mouth. "I need someone. To hold the world. For a little while. One night. Maybe two. So I can go somewhere."

Rui and Tsui looked at each other.

"Where?" said Tsui.

"The Gray Abyss," said Famine.

Rui went pale. She had been to the Gray Abyss. She had come out of it with her own fire in the palm of her hand.

"Why?" she said quietly.

So Famine told them. About Drake. About hell. About the hunger she could not stop. About the three short plain lines in Tim's memory, and the feeling that came every night just after midnight, and the girl with the umbrella standing beside them right now, who neither of them could see.

When she was done, there was a long silence in the canyon.

Then Rui put down her bag of oranges.

"Okay," she said.

"Okay?"

"Okay. We'll hold it. Me and Tsui. And we'll get Esme, she's good with weird things, and Ena, she's got the residual corruption, she understands the nexus better than anyone. And Pia, because War will be furious if we leave her out of anything." Rui was already rolling up her sleeves. "We held the line against Galactix. We can hold a world for a couple of nights. Go."

Famine stared at her.

"Just like that?" she said. "You'll just—do it? I didn't even—you didn't even make me—"

"You asked," said Rui. "That's all you had to do. You just had to ask." She smiled, crooked, her scar pulling. "Took you long enough."


Chapter Six: Stepping Off the Glass

They came at dusk. All of them.

Rui and Tsui first, fire and water, standing at the north edge of the black glass hollow. Esme, eyes glowing gold, at the east. Ena, silver hair blowing, red light flickering around her fingers, at the south. And at the west, a thin pale girl in grey boots with one brown eye and one red one, and beside her, holding her hand, a girl in an apron with flour still on it and strawberry-pink eyes kept carefully down.

"Dolly wanted to come," said Pia. "She says she owes the world one. And War says if anyone's holding anything, she's holding it too."

"I don't owe anyone anything," said Dolly, blushing. "I just like Famine. She told me the grey jam was the best thing she'd tasted in three thousand years."

Famine looked at them all. Six of them. Seven, counting War. Standing around the black glass hollow in a ring, in the red dusk, at the center of the world.

"I don't know how to do this," she said. "I've never let go of it. Not once. I don't know what will happen."

"Neither do we," said Ena. She stepped forward, to the very edge of the glass. "But I know what it's like to hold something you can't put down. I held the corruption for my whole life. And the only time it ever got lighter was when someone else picked up the other end." She held out her hand. "Give us the other end."

Famine closed her eyes.

She felt the world. All of it. Every thread of the web, every heartbeat, every hunger. The baby in the east. The soldier on the wall. The old man alone in his house. The weight of it, hanging from her like a coat from a hook.

And then, very slowly, she reached out with her power, and found the edges of the web, and pushed them, gently, toward the ring of people standing around her.

Toward Rui's fire. Toward Tsui's water. Toward Esme's mind and Ena's corruption and Pia's stubborn quiet strength and War's iron and Dolly's soft pink warmth.

She felt them take it.

It was the strangest feeling of her life. Like setting down a weight she had carried so long she had forgotten it was a weight at all. The threads of the world slid from her hands into theirs, one by one, and she felt them catch it, all of them, together, braced in a ring at the center of everything, holding.

Famine opened her eyes.

She was standing on the black glass hollow at the bottom of the red canyon, and she was not the nexus anymore.

Death stood at the edge of the glass, holding out her hand.

"Come on, sister," she said. "Let's go see your boy."

And Famine, the second Horseman of the Apocalypse, the hunger at the end of every world, stepped off the glass for the first time in three years.


Chapter Seven: The Statue

The Gray Abyss was the same as it always was. Grey sky. Grey plain. Grey rubble in grey heaps. And everywhere, standing in the grey, the statues.

Death walked through it calmly, her umbrella dripping, the way someone walks through their own garden. Famine followed. She had never been here before. She had heard about it. Everyone who had ever been killed by a Horseman, or a master, or by their own hand, or by corruption, sent here to stand in grey stone forever, frozen in the act of their death.

She saw things she did not want to see. She kept her eyes on Death's umbrella and walked.

"Here," said Death at last, stopping.

It was a statue of two figures. One was a boy, maybe nineteen, in half-black, half-silver armor, glitching even in stone. His head was gone. Not broken. Just gone, as if it had never been there, as if something had taken it all at once.

The other figure was Famine.

She was reaching toward the boy with both hands. Her mouth was open. Her eyes were black and endless and starving. And her face—

Famine stopped breathing.

Her face, in the statue, was not cruel. It was not hungry, not the way she had always imagined it. It was terrified. It was the face of someone who had started pulling, trying to save someone, and realized, too late, that they could not stop. That they were going to take everything. That there was nothing in them strong enough to hold back.

"That's what I looked like," she whispered.

"That's what you looked like," said Death.

"I thought—I always thought I looked like a monster."

"You looked like someone who'd been starving for three thousand years," said Death. "And who'd never been fed. And who reached for something to save, and couldn't stop. That's not a monster, sister. That's just hunger."

Famine stood in front of the statue for a long time.

Then, from somewhere behind it, from inside the grey stone, came a voice. A boy's voice. Loud, and rough, and a little bit cracked.

"Took you long enough," it said.


Chapter Eight: What Drake Wanted

He came out of the statue the way a reflection comes out of a mirror. Grey at first, and then less grey, and then almost real. A boy, nineteen, in ordinary clothes, with a crooked nose and a grin that took up half his face. His head was there. Of course it was. This was the Abyss. Things here were what they had been, not what had been done to them.

"Drake," said Famine.

"In the flesh," said Drake. "Sort of. Mostly stone. Don't touch the elbow, it crumbles." He looked her up and down. "You're the one who ate my head."

Famine flinched.

"Yes," she said. "I'm so sorry. I'm—"

"Oh, no, don't do that," said Drake. "Don't do the sorry thing. Tim does the sorry thing. Ena does the sorry thing. Everybody does the sorry thing. I've been stuck in a statue for three years listening to people be sorry from across the multiverse. You know what nobody's ever done?" He grinned. "Brought me a sandwich."

Famine stared at him.

"A—"

"A sandwich," said Drake. "I'm starving. I've been starving for three years. Every night, about midnight, I feel it, this enormous hunger, and I thought it was mine at first, and then I realized it was coming from you. Through the nexus. You're the hunger at the end of the world, right? Well, it turns out when the hunger at the end of the world is sad about you, you feel it." He sat down on a piece of grey rubble. "It's been really annoying, honestly."

Famine did not know what to say. In three thousand years, she had never once not known what to say.

"You're not angry?" she managed.

Drake thought about it.

"I was," he said. "At first. Really angry. I was corrupted, I didn't even get to choose anything, and then this Horseman comes along and eats my head, and I end up in a statue in a grey wasteland. Yeah. I was angry." He shrugged. "But then I watched. From in here. You can see things, from the Abyss, if you look hard enough. I watched you help Tim. I watched you try to starve Galactix. I watched you volunteer to be the nexus, so the world wouldn't fall apart. I watched you stand there for three years. Not eating. Not moving. Just holding." He looked up at her. "You're not a monster. You're just really, really hungry. And you've never once in your whole life let anyone feed you."

Famine sat down beside him on the grey rubble. She did not trust her legs anymore.

"Nobody feeds the hunger," she said. "That's not how it works. I'm what's left when there's nothing to eat."

"That's stupid," said Drake. "Everybody gets hungry. Even hunger." He nudged her with a grey stone elbow, which crumbled slightly. "Rui and Tsui fed you. I saw it. You said it was the best meal you ever had."

"That was different. That was—"

"That was them giving you something they loved," said Drake. "That's all food is, really. Somebody giving you something they love." He grinned again. "So. I don't have a sandwich. I'm a statue. But I've got something."

"What?"

Drake leaned over, and very gently, very carefully, put his grey stone hand over Famine's.

"I forgive you," he said. "Not because Tim told me to. Not because it was the corruption. Just because you're hungry. And I know what that's like. And somebody should feed you." He squeezed her hand. "So here. Eat."


Chapter Nine: Fed

It was not food. It was not fire, or water, or grey jam. It was not anything Famine had ever eaten.

It was a boy in a statue in the Gray Abyss, holding her hand, telling her she was forgiven.

And it went into her. All the way in. Down past three thousand years of empty belly and failed harvests and cupboards with nothing in them. Down past the moment in hell when she could not stop pulling. Down to the very bottom of her, where the hunger had always lived, and where nothing had ever reached.

And for the first time in her whole existence, the hunger at the end of every world was full.

Famine did not cry. Horsemen did not cry. But something happened in her chest that was very like crying, and she bent forward on the grey rubble with her hand under Drake's and shook, and shook, and could not stop.

Death stood a little way off, with her umbrella, and did not say anything. She only watched, with her deep-water eyes, and the dark drops fell from the spokes of the umbrella onto the grey ground, slow and steady, like a clock.

When it was over, Famine sat up.

"Thank you," she said. Her voice was rough. "Thank you. I don't—I don't know what to give you. In return. I don't know how this works."

"You don't have to give me anything," said Drake. "That's how it works."

"But you're still here. In the Abyss. Still stone."

"Yeah," said Drake. "That's kind of what happens. When you die. I'm not mad about it anymore." He looked out over the grey plain at the statues. "Although honestly? I wouldn't mind seeing Tim. Just once. He's not here. He's not anywhere I can find. But I know he's somewhere. He died a while after the war. Virus, Ena said. Residual corruption. I keep looking for him in here and he's never in here." He shrugged. "Maybe he went somewhere nicer."

Death stepped forward.

"He did," she said.

Drake looked up at her. So did Famine.

"I took him," said Death. "I take everyone, eventually. He went where people go when nobody killed them and they didn't kill themselves and they just—ran out. Somewhere quiet. Somewhere with a river." Her umbrella tilted. "He throws rocks in it, sometimes. He's waiting for someone to throw them with."

Drake was very quiet.

"Can I go?" he said at last. Very small. Not loud at all.

Death looked at Famine.

"You're not mine to take," she said to Drake. "You were killed by a Horseman. You belong to the one who killed you. So it's her choice." She looked at her sister. "Famine? Will you let him go?"

Famine looked at the boy beside her. Grey stone, mostly. Crumbling at the elbow. Grinning.

She thought about holding on. She thought about the three years she had spent standing in the black glass, thinking about him every night. She thought about how, if she let him go now, she would never see him again.

And then she thought about Ena at the tower, holding on to her brother. About Ivy Calder, holding on to eleven thousand leashes. About the old mage at the hermitage, writing I let go on the last page of his book.

"Go," she said. "Go throw rocks."

Drake grinned so wide his stone face cracked.

"Thanks, Famine," he said. "For the meal." And then he stood up, and turned toward Death, and Death opened her umbrella wide, wide, wider than an umbrella should open, and underneath it there was not grey but blue, and green, and the sound of a river, and somewhere in the distance, very faintly, someone skipping a stone across water.

Drake stepped under the umbrella, and was gone.

The statue behind them, the one of the headless boy and the starving Horseman, cracked down the middle and fell into grey dust.


Chapter Ten: The Weight

When Famine came back up the canyon path the next morning, they were all still there.

Seven of them, in a ring around the black glass hollow, holding the world. They looked exhausted. Rui's hands were smoking. Tsui was soaked to the knees. Esme had her eyes closed and her lips moving, murmuring to herself, or to someone else in her head. Ena's silver hair was standing straight up with red static. Pia was swaying on her feet, and War's red eye was barely open. Dolly was asleep standing up, leaning on Pia's shoulder, with flour still on her apron.

But they were holding it. The world was still there. The canyon was still red. Somewhere in the east, a baby was crying for milk, and somewhere on the northern wall a soldier was yawning, and somewhere in a big house, an old man was, for the first time in a month, putting a kettle on.

"You're back," said Rui. Her voice was hoarse. "Did you find him?"

"I found him," said Famine.

"And?"

Famine stepped down onto the black glass. She felt the threads of the world, hanging there in the ring of hands around her, waiting for her to take them back.

"He fed me," she said.

Rui stared at her. Then she smiled, slow and crooked, the scar pulling.

"Good," she said. "About time someone did."

Famine reached out with her power, and took the world back. All of it. Every thread. It settled on her shoulders the way it always had, heavy and familiar. The baby's hunger. The soldier's. The old man's. The girl at the bakery, who had forgotten breakfast again.

But it felt different now. Not lighter. The world was exactly as heavy as it had always been. But she was not empty underneath it anymore. There was something at the bottom of her, where the hunger had always been, something warm and steady and full. A boy's stone hand on hers. I forgive you.

"Thank you," she said, to the ring of exhausted people around the black glass. "All of you. I didn't know I could ask."

"Well, now you know," said Ena. She was smiling too. "So ask again. Next time. Before it's three years."

Famine nodded.

"I will," she said. And then, because it was true, and because she had learned in the Gray Abyss that some things are worth saying out loud: "I'm hungry."

They all stared at her.

"Not like that," said Famine quickly. "Not like the end of the world. Just—normal hungry. For breakfast." She looked at Dolly, who had woken up and was blinking at her sleepily. "Is there any of that grey jam left?"

Dolly beamed.

"I'll bring a whole jar," she said.


Chapter Eleven: Midnight

That night, just after midnight, Famine stood in the black glass hollow at the bottom of the red canyon with her arms open and the whole restored world hanging from her, and waited for it.

The feeling. The one that came every night at this time. The moment she could not stop pulling. The taste of it.

It did not come.

She waited a long time. An hour. Two. The stars turned overhead. The canyon was quiet. At the top of the path, a guard was humming to himself, and somewhere in the east the baby had finally stopped crying and was sleeping with a full belly.

The feeling did not come.

In its place there was something else. Small. Steady. Like a stone skipping across water, far away, once, twice, three times.

Famine closed her eyes and listened to it.

At the edge of the glass, a small figure with an umbrella was standing in the dark. Dark drops fell from the spokes onto the red rock, soft and slow, like a clock.

"Is he happy?" Famine asked, without opening her eyes.

"He's throwing rocks," said Death. "With a boy in silver armor. They're arguing about who throws farther. They're both terrible at it." The umbrella tilted. "Yes. He's happy."

"Good," said Famine.

They stood together in the dark, the hunger at the end of every world and the stopping at the end of every life. Two sisters. Two Horsemen. One holding up the world, and one holding an umbrella over it.

"Will you visit again?" said Famine.

Death considered.

"I visit everyone eventually," she said. "But yes. I'll come sooner than that. Maybe I'll bring a sandwich."

And Famine, standing at the center of the world with her arms open, laughed, a deep, rough, full laugh that echoed off the red walls of the canyon. The laugh of someone who had finally, after three thousand years, been fed.
