Incarnate: Relapse
A spinoff of the Corruption Saga

Trigger warning: This story contains addiction and relapse, self-hurt themes, survivor's guilt, grief, violence, a cover-up, and a narrator who lies to you as easily as she lies to everyone else.
Read at your own risk.



Chapter One: Day Forty-One

Day forty-one.

That's what I tell the cat. Every morning. I wake up on the roof, on the mattress I dragged up seven flights of stairs, under the tarp that leaks on the left side, and the cat is sitting on my chest like a loaf of bread, staring at me, and I tell him the number.

"Day forty-one, Tim."

He's orange. He's missing half an ear. He's a jerk. I named him after my brother. I don't know if that was a good idea. Some days it feels like keeping a light on. Some days it feels like poking a bruise on purpose so you remember where it is.

Day forty-one since the last time I used.

That's the line, anyway. That's the line I say out loud at the meetings, at the laundromat on Fifth with the dead machines, sitting on a folding chair with a styrofoam cup of the worst coffee left in the universe. Hi, I'm Ena. I'm forty-one days clean. And everybody claps, the little thin clapping of nine people who used to be monsters, and Marguerite who runs it nods at me like she's proud, and I sit down and I feel nothing, nothing, nothing.

Here's the truth. Since nobody else is getting it, you might as well.

It's day nine.

I used on day thirty-two. Just a little. Just to take the edge off. And then I lied about it, and the lie was easier than the using, and that should have scared me more than it did.

Hey, that's me. Harumi. Hi. She doesn't like to introduce me. I'm the part of her that's honest. I'm the part that remembers what it felt like to end a world.

Shut up, Harumi.

See? Rude.



Chapter Two: What It Is

I should explain for anybody who wasn't there. Which is everyone. Everyone who was there is dead.

There was a prophecy about me. Girl with shining blonde hair. Beloved sister. Fueled by grief. Would end everything. Everybody knew it. People looked at us funny in the grocery store. My brother thought it was bullshit. I thought it was bullshit. And then I got sick, and my eyes went red, and I started screaming backward words, and my brother, my best friend, the only person I had after what happened to our parents, stuck a knife in my chest.

And then everything the prophecy said happened anyway. Because that's how prophecies work. You run from them right into them.

I came back as Corrupt. I came back with a mission to kill him for killing me. I did a lot of things. Whole cities. Whole resistances. My friends, one by one. I'm not going to list them. The list is long and I've said it out loud enough times at enough graves.

What matters is: corruption feels good.

Nobody says that part. In all the stories, in all the warnings, Beware of Corruption-027, the end is coming, say your prayers, nobody ever says: it feels so fucking good. It feels like being sure. Like every bad thing you ever felt, every guilt, every grief, every tired morning, just goes quiet, and in the quiet there's this huge bright red hum, and the hum says: you're right. Whatever you do next, you're right. You're not a girl whose brother stabbed her. You're a weapon. Weapons don't feel bad.

When it ended, when Galactix fell and Tombstone fell and the restored world got sealed shut and everybody else got their happy ending, I got clean. Mostly. They pulled most of it out of me. But there's always residue. There's always residual corruption, little black shards of it, in the rubble where the corrupted fell. Like glass. You can find it if you look. You press a shard into your palm and it melts in, and for an hour, the hum comes back.

I know where all the good rubble is. I was the one who made it.



Chapter Three: The Tin

The tin is under a loose brick behind the water tank on my roof.

It used to hold mints. It's red and white and dented. There are fourteen shards in it. I counted this morning, after I told the cat it was day forty-one. Fourteen. They're black and shiny and about the size of a fingernail, and they hum, very quietly, if you hold the tin up to your ear. I don't hold it up to my ear. That's a rule.

I have a lot of rules. I break most of them. The rules are mostly for the feeling of having rules.

Rule: Don't open the tin before noon.

Rule: Don't open the tin on days you go to the grave.

Rule: Don't tell Eri.

That last one's the only one I've never broken.

Eri comes by on Tuesdays. She climbs the seven flights because she says the teleporting makes her nauseous now, after everything, and she brings me things. A can of peaches. A paperback with half the pages. Batteries. She sits on the edge of the roof with her legs dangling over the ruined street and she asks me how I'm doing, and I say, "Forty-one days," and she grins with her one eye, the one she has left, and she bumps her shoulder into mine.

"Proud of you, babe."

"Don't."

"I'm going to. You can't stop me. I'm a demon of the mind, I'll just be proud of you directly into your brain."

And I laugh. And I mean the laugh. And then she leaves, and I watch her go down the fire escape, and the second her head disappears I'm behind the water tank with the brick in my hand.

It's not like I'm hurting anyone.

It's not.

She's hurting me, a little, Harumi says. Doesn't count, though. Nobody counts me.



Chapter Four: The Grave

Tim's grave is on the hill above what used to be our town.

I don't know why I keep calling it his grave. There's nothing in it. There was nothing left of him to put in it. But I made a stone. I carved it myself with a piece of rebar over three days, and it's not very good, the letters lean, but it says TIM, and under that it says BROTHER, and under that it says I'M SORRY, and under that, very small, because I ran out of stone, it says ME TOO.

I go there Thursdays. I sit in the dead grass with my back against the stone and I tell him things. The cat sometimes comes and sometimes doesn't. Today he came. He sat on the stone like he owned it.

"Hey," I said. "Day forty-one."

The wind moved the grass.

"That's a lie. It's day nine. I don't know why I'm lying to a rock. I guess I'm practicing."

The cat yawned.

"I go to the meetings. Eri thinks I'm doing great. Marguerite thinks I'm doing great. There's this kid, Dov, nineteen, he used to be one of Tombstone's runners, he looks at me like I'm the success story. Like I'm proof it can be done. Like if I can get clean, the girl who ended the world, then anybody can."

You know what I want to tell him? Harumi said. I want to tell him to go find his own rubble. I want to tell him you can't get clean of something you were born to be. You're not an addict, baby. You're a prophecy. Prophecies don't get sober. They get fulfilled.

I didn't say anything for a long time.

"I think about you," I told the stone. "I think about how you must have felt. Standing there with the knife. Knowing what I was going to become. Doing it anyway because you thought it was right. And then being wrong. Being wrong forever."

The cat jumped down and walked off into the grass.

"I think about how I've done that to so many people now," I said. "Done something because the hum told me it was right."

I didn't open the tin that day. Thursdays are grave days. Rules.

I opened it Friday.



Chapter Five: The Meeting

Marguerite runs the meeting like a drill sergeant who's been through too much to yell anymore.

She's sixty, maybe. Gray buzz cut. Burn scars up both arms from when the corruption came out of her. She used to be a captain under Tombstone. She doesn't talk about what she did. She says she'll talk about it when she's ready, and she's been saying that for two years.

The laundromat's got nine of us. A man named Petrosyan who used to be able to turn people's bones to salt. Two sisters who don't talk, just hold hands. A woman who was a Whispering One and still sometimes speaks in a voice that isn't hers and apologizes. Dov, with his skinny arms and his patchy beard and his habit of picking at the black scars on his wrists. Me.

Tonight Dov stood up to share.

"I almost used," he said. "Tuesday. I found some in the old rail yard. Like a whole vein of it. Shards everywhere." His voice was shaking. "I stood there for, like, an hour. Just looking."

Everybody waited.

"And I didn't," he said. "I thought about Ena." He looked at me. "I thought, she's forty-one days now. Ena's been through way worse than me. If she can do it, I can do it. So I left."

The clapping. The thin clapping.

I clapped too. I smiled at him. I had a shard in me right then, under my sleeve, in my left palm, melted in that morning. Day nine had become day one again that morning, and I was sitting in the meeting humming with it, and the hum was saying: you're a good person, Ena. Look at that kid. You saved him. You're the reason.

That's what it does. It takes the worst thing about you and tells you it's the best thing.

"Ena?" Marguerite said. "You want to share?"

"Forty-one days," I said. "Still going."

She nodded. Proud. Everybody nodded. Dov grinned at me with his whole stupid face.

And then the windows came in.



Chapter Six: The Hounds

I knew what they were before I saw them. You can smell it. Like burnt hair and pennies.

Tombstone's hounds. The corruption-dogs. When he fell, a lot of what he made just kept going without him, nobody to call them home, and they roam the ruins in packs. Big, black, glassy, too many joints in the legs, mouths that open sideways. They follow the smell of residual corruption. That's how they hunt. They smell it in the rubble. They smell it in people who used to have it.

They smell it in people who still do.

Six of them came through the front windows in a shower of glass, and the laundromat was suddenly screaming. Petrosyan went down first. One of the sisters. The woman who used to whisper started whispering again, her old voice, the not-hers voice, and it didn't help. Marguerite had a gun, she always has a gun, and she emptied it into the biggest one, and it shook the bullets off like water.

They didn't go for me. That's the thing. They walked around me. They sniffed at me, at my left hand, at the shard, and they walked around, the way dogs walk around the alpha. Because I made them. Not these exact ones. But I'm their kind.

Dov was in the corner behind the dead dryers. One of them had him by the ankle. He was screaming my name.

"Ena! ENA! Help me!"

And I had a choice.

I could have fought them the way I am now. Just me. Just Ena. A girl with no powers left to speak of and a folding chair. I'd have died, probably. Maybe I'd have bought Dov thirty seconds to run.

Or.

Or, Harumi said, so soft. So kind. You could use the rest of the tin.



Chapter Seven: Fourteen

I ran home. Seven blocks. I don't remember the streets. I remember the stairs, all seven flights, and my lungs on fire, and the brick, and the red-and-white tin, and I didn't even pick out one shard. I tipped the whole thing into my palm. Thirteen. Thirteen shards. Three hours ago I'd used the fourteenth.

They went in like ice going into a hot pan. Hissing. And the hum came up out of my hand and went through my arm and into my chest and into my head and the whole world turned red.

Oh, hi, Harumi said. Out loud this time. With my mouth.

I don't remember running back. I remember being there. I remember the laundromat and the hounds turning to look at me all at once, all six heads, like they'd heard their name. I remember my eyes. I could see the light coming out of them on the walls. I remember laughing. Not my laugh. The old one. The prophecy laugh.

And I remember it was easy.

That's what you have to understand about it. It's so easy. Six hounds. I didn't even need to move. I just thought at them, and the corruption in them heard the corruption in me and turned against itself, and they came apart. Like wet sugar. Like ash. One by one. Collapsing into black glass on the laundromat tile. New shards. Fresh ones. Dozens of them. The hum said: look at all that. Look at all of that for later.

I stood in the middle of it, glowing, and I felt right. I felt so right. I'd saved them. I was the hero. I was forty-one days clean and I'd saved everyone.

And then Dov said, "Ena?"

He was alive. He was standing up from behind the dryers. His ankle was torn up but he was standing. And he was looking at me. At my eyes. At the light coming off my skin. At my left hand, still black and steaming where thirteen shards had gone in.

His face did a thing I've seen before. On my brother. In the second before the knife.

"You're using," Dov said.

And Harumi moved my hand.



Chapter Eight: What I Told Them

I want to tell you I tried to stop it. I want to tell you there was a struggle. A war inside my head, me screaming no, Harumi screaming yes, a big dramatic fight like in the stories.

There wasn't. It was one second. It was the hum saying: he'll tell. He'll tell Marguerite. He'll tell Eri. Everyone will know. And I thought, so fast I didn't even hear myself think it, I can't let Eri know.

That's what I thought. That's the thing I can't take back. Not "he's in danger." Not "Harumi's in control." I thought: Eri can't know.

And the corruption went out of my hand the way it went into the hounds, and Dov came apart like wet sugar, and he didn't even scream, he just looked surprised, and then he was a little pile of black glass on the tile, mixed in with the others. You couldn't tell which ones were him.

Marguerite was in the doorway to the back room. She was bleeding from her head. She'd been knocked out by the first hound. She'd only just come to. She saw the hounds gone. She saw the shards. She saw me standing there, glowing, starting to cry.

She didn't see Dov.

"Where's the kid?" she said.

And I said, "They got him. I was too late. I'm so sorry. They got him before I got here."

I said it in my own voice. Ena's voice. With real tears. They were real. That's the thing. They were completely real. I was crying for him while I lied about him. You can do both at once. Did you know that? I didn't, until that night.

Marguerite looked at me for a long time. At my eyes, still red at the edges. At my hand.

"You used," she said.

"I had to. For them. To save everyone."

She nodded slowly. "Yeah," she said. "Yeah. You did. Okay. We'll reset your count. That's okay. We'll reset it together. That's what it's for."

And she hugged me. And I let her.



Chapter Nine: Day One

There was a funeral for Dov. We didn't have a body. We buried a jar of the black glass from the laundromat floor. Marguerite picked out a handful of shards at random. She didn't know. Nobody knew. It might have been him. It might have been dogs.

I spoke. I said he was brave. I said he was the best of us. I said he'd stood in front of a whole vein of corruption in the rail yard on Tuesday and walked away from it, and that was harder than anything I'd ever done. Which was true. I said it with tears running down my face. Eri was there. She held my hand the whole time. Her hand was warm.

"You did everything you could," she said, after. On my roof. Legs dangling over the street. "You hear me? You used to save people. That's not a relapse. That's a sacrifice."

"Yeah," I said.

"Day one, huh?"

"Day one."

"I'll be here for day two," she said. "And three. And every one after. I'm not going anywhere, Ena."

She bumped her shoulder into mine. Then she climbed down the fire escape, and her head disappeared.

I sat there for a long time.

There's a new tin. I found it in the rubble two blocks over. It's blue and it used to hold buttons. And there are twenty-two shards in it, because I went back to the laundromat in the middle of the night and picked up the ones Marguerite didn't put in the jar. Every last one. On my hands and knees on the tile, in the dark, with the cat watching me from the broken window.

I don't know which ones are dogs.

I don't know which ones are Dov.

I put the tin under the brick behind the water tank, and I lay down on my mattress under the tarp that leaks on the left side, and the cat climbed up on my chest like a loaf of bread.

"Day one, Tim," I told him.

That's a lie. You know it's a lie. It was a lie when I said it, because I'd already opened the blue tin once before I lay down. Just one. Just to sleep.

I'll tell Eri day two on Tuesday.

Don't tell her. Please.

She won't, Harumi said, very gently, from inside. Who's she going to tell? Everybody else is dead.
