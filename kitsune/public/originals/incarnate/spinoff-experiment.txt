Incarnate: Experiment
A spinoff of the Corruption Saga

Trigger warning: This story contains hell, torture, eye trauma, betrayal, the selling of a friend, experimentation, adult language, and a narrator who knows exactly what she's doing.
Read at your own risk.



Chapter One: Number Nine

Before I had a name I had a number, and the number was nine.

Himo gave it to me. She gave all of us numbers. She had a lab at the bottom of the first war, under everything, where the fighting couldn't reach, and she sat in it with her cold hands and her long white coat and she made things. Experiments. She wanted to know what a mind was. She wanted to know if you could build one from nothing and then take it apart again and put it back together in a different order. She wanted to know if you could make a thing that could reach into other things' heads.

Experiments one through eight didn't work. I met some of them. In jars. Eight was still twitching.

I worked.

I opened my eyes on a steel table, both eyes back then, and the first thing I heard was Himo's thoughts. Not her voice. Her thoughts. Cold and neat and humming like a refrigerator. Finally. Finally. Don't get attached.

I was two minutes old and I already knew she'd never love me.

She trained me for a long time. Years, I think, but there aren't years down there. I learned to go into minds. Soldiers. Prisoners. Other experiments. I learned to find what people were afraid of and turn it up. I learned to find what they loved and turn it off. I learned to pull a memory out of somebody like a splinter and hold it up to the light and decide whether to give it back.

I was very good. I was the best thing she ever made, she said once. Not to me. To a jar.

And then the war turned. Something went wrong. Himo started a plan, then a plan B, then a plan C, and she was going to have to run, and she couldn't take her experiments with her, and she couldn't leave them for her enemies.

So she threw us away.

I remember her hand on my back. Cold. I remember the edge of the pit. I remember her thought, neat and humming: Don't get attached.

And then I was falling, and I fell for a long time, and at the bottom of the falling was hell.



Chapter Two: Business

Hell isn't what you think.

It's not fire everywhere. It's fire in places. It's mostly crowded. It's like a city that nobody planned, built on top of itself over and over, the damned stacked in apartments made out of their own regrets, demons running the corners, everyone hustling, everyone hungry, everyone trying to get a little something to make forever go by faster.

And what people in hell want most, more than water, more than rest, more than anything, is to forget.

That's my business.

I set up in a cave mouth off the Fourth Ring, by the river that's not water. I put up a sign. I didn't need a sign. Word gets around. The damned would come to me in a long line, gray and bent and staring, and they'd sit on my stool, and I'd put two fingers on their forehead, and I'd go in.

"What do you want gone?"

"The day I drove drunk."

"The look on her face."

"The sound he made."

And I'd find it. I'd pull it out. I'd hold it up in the red light, a shining wet little thing like a pearl made out of pain, and they'd go slack and grateful on my stool, a little lighter, a little emptier, and they'd shuffle off.

And I'd keep the pearl.

That's the business part. Because there's somebody in hell who wants everything. And some demons, the big ones, the dukes, the ones with the palaces at the Ninth Ring, they love other people's memories. They eat them. They wear them. They trade them like money. A really good memory of a really bad thing can buy you a lot down there. A room with a door that locks. A night without the screaming. Information.

I didn't feel bad about it. Why would I? I was giving them what they asked for. The damned walked away lighter. The dukes walked away full. I walked away with a little more than I came in with. Everybody wins.

Except, you know. Everybody was still in hell.

Including me.



Chapter Three: Lute

Lute used to sing on the bridge over the river that's not water.

He'd been there a long time. Longer than me. He was a busker when he was alive, in some city with trains, and he'd done something to end up down here, something with a car and a girl and a bottle, I never asked, and he didn't have a guitar anymore, so he just sang. All day. Every day. Old songs, sad ones mostly, in a voice that was cracked down the middle like a plate.

The demons hated it. They'd throw things at him. Rocks. Bones. Sometimes fire. He'd just keep singing with blood on his face.

He came to my cave once. Sat on my stool.

"What do you want gone?" I said.

He thought about it. "Nothing," he said. "I just wanted to meet you. Everyone says you take the bad stuff out. I wanted to see what someone who does that looks like."

"Well," I said. "Here I am."

"You look tired," he said.

Nobody had said that to me. Not ever. Not Himo. Not the dukes. I sat there with my two fingers halfway to his forehead and I didn't know what to do with my face.

He started coming every day after that. Not to sell. Just to sit. He'd sit on the rock outside my cave between customers and sing, and sometimes I'd tell him to shut up, and he'd sing quieter, and I didn't tell him to leave.

He asked me once what I was before.

"Experiment," I said. "Number nine."

"That's not a name," he said.

"It's the one I got."

He thought about it. "Eri," he said. "You look like an Eri."

"That's stupid."

"It's better than nine."

I kept it. I didn't tell him I kept it. I just started using it, with customers, with the dukes, and after a while it was just my name, and nobody knew where it came from except him.

He was the first person in hell, or out of it, who never wanted anything from me. I could read his mind, I tried, and there was nothing in there about using me. Just the songs. Just a lot of old songs and a city with trains and a girl he was sorry about.

That's the problem with someone like that. They make you notice what everybody else is.



Chapter Four: The Duke

Duke Vasselgrave sent for me on a day I'll call Tuesday, because I need to call it something.

You go when a duke sends for you. Vasselgrave's palace was at the Ninth Ring, built out of something that looked like bone and smelled like a hospital. He sat on a throne made of other people's teeth, and he was huge and gray and wore about forty other people's memories on him like jewelry, little pearls hanging off every part of him, glowing, whispering.

"Little Eri," he said. "Himo's ninth. I knew her, you know. Before. Cold hands."

I didn't say anything. You don't talk first with dukes.

"I hear you want out," he said.

I hadn't told anybody that. Not even Lute. But he was a duke and dukes know things, and I could feel him poking around in the front of my mind with a big clumsy thumb, and I let him, because you let them.

"There's a way," he said. "A door. Old. From the first war. A portal that goes up, out of here, to the world of the living. It opens for very few. I have a key."

He held it up. It was small and black and shaped like a tooth.

"What does it cost?"

He smiled. He had a lot of teeth. Not as many as were in the throne.

"There's a soul on the Fourth Ring," he said. "On the bridge. Sings all day. Annoying. But bright. Very, very bright. Do you know how rare that is down here? A soul that's been in hell for forty years and still hasn't gone dim? It's like finding a candle at the bottom of the ocean."

I didn't say anything.

"I want it," he said. "All of it. Every memory. Every song. Bring me that soul in a jar and you can have the key."

"And if I say no?"

He shrugged. Forty memories jingled. "Then you stay," he said. "Forever. Selling other people's worst days by the river. Until you're like everyone else here. Until you're dim."



Chapter Five: The Math

I thought about it for three days.

I want credit for that. I want somebody to write down that I thought about it for three days, and that I sat on my stool and didn't take any customers, and that Lute sat on his rock outside and sang and kept asking me if I was okay, and I said I had a headache.

I did the math. Himo taught me math.

On one side: Lute. One soul. Already in hell. Already damned. Already forty years into forever. What did he have, really? A bridge. Rocks thrown at his face. Songs nobody wanted to hear. If I left him there, he'd keep doing that for a billion years and then a billion more. If I took him to the duke, it would just be over. That was almost kind. That was almost mercy.

On the other side: out. Out of hell. Up. The world of the living. Sky. Real water. Being somebody who wasn't number nine and wasn't a memory-dealer by a fake river.

One soul for a way out.

People make that trade all the time. Wars are made of that trade. Himo made it every single day of her life. She made it with me.

It's a sacrifice we have to make.

That's what I told myself. I said it out loud in my cave. It's a sacrifice we have to make. I'd say it again later, years later, to a girl with shining blonde hair, standing in a room full of empty anti-gods, and she'd scream at me, and she'd be right. But I didn't know that yet. I just knew the words sounded good. They sounded like something a hero would say.

On the third night Lute came in and sat down on my stool. Not on the rock. On the stool.

"Okay," he said. "You win. I've got one."

"One what?"

"Something I want gone." He looked at his hands. "The girl. The one I'm sorry about. I don't want to forget her. But the last thing I saw, before the car went over. Her face. Can you take just that part? Just that one second? I've been carrying it forty years and I'm so tired, Eri."

He'd never asked me for anything. Not once. And now he was asking, and he was looking at me with total trust, and he had no idea.

I put two fingers on his forehead.

"Sure," I said. "Close your eyes."



Chapter Six: The Jar

It wasn't one second. I took everything.

I went in and I found the girl's face, the last second, the car going over, and I took it. And then I kept going. I took the girl. I took the city with the trains. I took his mother and the guitar and the first song he ever learned. I took the bridge and the rocks and the forty years. I took his name. I took every single song, every one, one at a time, and they came out of him glowing and wet and humming and I put them in a jar.

He didn't fight. He didn't know to fight. He just sat on the stool with his eyes closed and got lighter and lighter and lighter.

At the very end, when there was almost nothing left, he said, very small, "Eri?"

And I took that too. I took my own name out of him. He was the only one who knew where it came from, and then he didn't, and then nobody did.

Then he opened his eyes. And there was nothing in them. Gray. Flat. Dim. He looked at me like a stranger, and then like a wall, and then like nothing at all. He stood up. He walked out of my cave and he walked down to the river and he stood on the bank, and he didn't sing. He just stood there.

The jar was so bright I had to wrap it in my coat.



Chapter Seven: The Fine Print

Vasselgrave was delighted. He held the jar up to the red light and the songs swam around in it like fish, and he laughed, and the teeth in his throne rattled.

"Oh, it's gorgeous," he said. "Oh, little Eri, you have no idea."

"The key," I said.

He tossed it to me. Small and black and shaped like a tooth. It was warm.

"There's just one tiny thing," he said, with his hand already in the jar, already pulling out a song and holding it up to his ear like a seashell. "The door only opens from the other side."

"What?"

"The portal. Old one. First war. It only opens for someone coming in. Someone from the living world. Someone who was alive yesterday and is in hell today. When they come through, the door's open for a second, and if you've got the key, you can go out as they come in." He popped the song into his mouth like a grape. "Do you know how rarely the living fall into hell? Directly, I mean? Not dying first? Once in a thousand years. If that. Could be tomorrow. Could be never."

I stood there with the key in my hand.

"You knew," I said.

"Of course I knew." He reached into my head with his big gray thumb, not even gentle, and pressed, and the whole palace spun. "And you didn't check. Himo's best experiment, the great mind demon, and you didn't even look in my head first to see if I was lying. Why is that, Eri?"

I knew why. He knew why. He said it anyway.

"Because you didn't want to know," he said. "Because if you looked, and it was a trick, you'd have had to keep him. And you didn't want to keep him. You wanted an excuse."

I went for him.

I don't remember deciding to. I was just on him, on the throne, in his head, ripping. Pulling memories off him like scabs. He was a duke. He was older than me and bigger than me and full of forty other people. He laughed while I did it, and then he stopped laughing, and he reached up, very calmly, and put one long gray finger into my left eye, and took it.

"For the inconvenience," he said, and he ate it.

They threw me out of the Ninth Ring with blood running down my face. I still had the key. He let me keep the key. That was the joke.



Chapter Eight: The Bridge

I went back to the Fourth Ring.

I don't know what I thought I'd do. Put it back? I couldn't. The jar was gone. The songs were gone. He'd be wearing Lute on his throne by now, a little glowing pearl for every year of him.

Lute was still standing on the bank of the river that's not water. Same place. Same slack face. A demon threw a rock at him to see if he'd sing. He didn't. The demon got bored and left.

I stood next to him for a long time.

"Hey," I said.

Nothing.

"I'm Eri," I said. "You named me that. You don't remember. It's fine. I don't deserve for you to remember."

Nothing.

"I want to tell you I'm sorry," I said. "And I am. But I need you to know that I'd do it again. If the key worked. If the door opened tomorrow. I'd do it again in a second and I'd walk out of here and I'd never look back. I just wanted to tell somebody that. Out loud. So somebody knows the truth about me."

He didn't turn his head.

I put my hand on his shoulder. Then I went back to my cave and I sat on my stool, and when the first customer came that night, a gray woman who wanted to forget the sound her baby made, I put two fingers on her forehead, and I took it, and I kept the pearl.

Business. Business doesn't stop.

So say Kio and move on.

That's an old word. From the war. One of Himo's words. It means something like: sorry, but the war is over. It means: it's done, let it go, walk away. I said it to the bridge every night after that. I never once meant it.



Chapter Nine: The Living Boy

It was a long time before anyone fell.

I don't know how long. Long enough that my eye socket healed into a scar. Long enough that I built up a little empire of pearls by the river and had a room with a door that locked. Long enough that I stopped carrying the key in my hand and started wearing it around my neck on a string, like a joke I told myself every morning.

And then one day the sky of hell split open and somebody came screaming through it.

A man. Young. Human-ish, but not quite. His eyes were doing something, glowing, swiveling. He hit the ground hard and rolled and came up running, and behind him, through the rip in the sky, I saw it. Just for a second. The door. The old door from the first war, standing open, and behind it, a world. Gray and broken and dead, but up. Out.

And then it closed. Before I could move. Before I could even reach for the key.

I stood there staring at the place where it had been.

He'd come through it. He'd come through it alive. Which meant he was connected to it. Which meant, maybe, maybe, if he went back, if somebody helped him find the way, it would open again for him.

And for whoever was standing next to him.

I looked at the man. He was running across the rocks now, jumping over fire, dodging the demons who were starting to notice him. He was crying. He was so obviously alone. He was so obviously the last of something.

I reached into his mind, just at the very edge, the way I used to with customers, just to see.

A girl with shining blonde hair. A knife. A grave. A resistance, gone. Friends, gone. Everyone, gone. A name. Tim.

Oh, I thought. Oh, he's so sad. He's so, so sad.

He's perfect.

I went after him. He ran, of course. They always run from me. I called out, and he kept running, and I sprinted and caught up and when he turned around, wild, with his glowing eyes and his tear-streaked face, I made mine soft. I made it sorry. I knew how. I'd learned it from watching Lute.

"I will help you," I said, "since I am trapped here too." I let my voice crack, a little, just the right amount. "Sorry about your friends. It must hurt, being the last individual on Earth."

I watched it land. I watched him not trust me, and then want to trust me, and then decide to.

The key was warm against my chest under my shirt.

I didn't feel bad. I want to be honest, because I've lied about this so many times since, to him, to Ena, to everyone who thinks I'm the good one, the friend, the demon with a heart. I didn't feel bad. I felt the way I felt in Himo's lab when an experiment finally worked.

Finally. Finally.

Don't get attached.
