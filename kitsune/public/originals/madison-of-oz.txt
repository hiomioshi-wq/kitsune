PART ONE

The last thing Madison Pryce did in Kansas was talk a gas station clerk out of a free iced coffee.

It was not hard. It was never hard. She leaned on the counter at the Gas-N-Go off Route 54, pushed her sunglasses up into her hair, and touched two fingers to the back of the clerk's hand while he was counting change.

"Wait," she said. "Kyle? Oh my god. It's me. Remember when your car died outside the Sonic and I jumped it for you in the rain? You were literally crying. You said you owed me forever."

Kyle blinked. Behind his eyes, something soft and grey turned over, the way a sleeper turns over, and settled.

"Oh, wow," he said slowly. "Yeah. Yeah, the Sonic. Dude. You saved my life."

"I mean, I wouldn't say saved, that's so dramatic. But kind of."

"The coffee's on me," said Kyle. "Seriously. Get a large."

Madison got a large. She also got a bag of the good chips and a phone charger, because once Kyle remembered owing her forever he wanted to start paying it off right away, and it would have been rude to stop him. She walked out into the parking lot sipping through the straw, feeling the warm little hum behind her own eyes that came after she did it, like the buzz after a really good song.

She did not think of it as stealing. She had never once taken anything. People gave it to her. They gave it to her because they remembered, clearly and fondly and in detail, a time she had been kind to them. The fact that the time had never happened was, in Madison's opinion, kind of a technicality.

She had been able to do it since she was eleven. She had never told anybody. Who would she even tell? Hey Mom, so, fun fact, I can edit people's memories by touching them and telling a story, it's giving low-key cult leader but make it retail. No. Absolutely not. She just did it. Little things. A free coffee. A better seat. A teacher who remembered she had turned in the essay. A landlord who remembered she'd paid. It was fine. It was lowkey fine. Nobody got hurt.

The sky over the parking lot was doing something weird.

Madison noticed it the way you notice a phone buzzing in another room. A sort of shimmer, high up, like heat over a road, except it was cold, and it was moving in a slow spiral, and the light underneath it had gone the color of a bruise.

"Okay," said Madison. "Okay, no. That's not a tornado. Tornadoes are, like, a funnel. That's a—"

The asphalt under her sneakers opened like a mouth.

She did not fall so much as get pulled, the way a straw pulls the last of a drink. The parking lot went up, and the Gas-N-Go sign went up, and her large iced coffee went up and hung in the air above her, ice cubes turning slowly in the bruise-colored light, and then there was nothing above her at all but a long silver seam in the dark, closing like a zipper.

The last thing she thought, before she stopped thinking, was: I didn't even get to finish my coffee.


PART TWO

She woke up in a field of red flowers with a man made of tin standing over her.

No. Wait. Not tin. Red. Everything was red. The flowers were red, the grass was a deep wine color, the little round farmhouse at the edge of the field had a red roof and red shutters and a red door, and the man standing over her was not made of tin at all but was just an extremely short, extremely round person in a red coat with red buttons and a hat shaped like an upside-down flowerpot, holding a red watering can and looking at her with enormous concern.

"Oh, you poor thing," he said. "You've come down out of the sky. Are you hurt? Are you a witch? Please say you aren't a witch. We've had quite enough of those."

Madison sat up. Her head hurt. Her phone was in her back pocket and the screen said NO SERVICE, which honestly tracked. Her sunglasses were somehow still on her head. Her coffee was gone, which was the real tragedy here.

"Not a witch," she said. "Where am I? Is this, like, a farm thing? An Airbnb? Are you a mascot?"

"This is the Quadling Country," said the little man, "in the south of the Land of Oz. I am Burl Tumbletoe, and this is my strawberry field, which you have landed in rather heavily."

Madison looked down. She was sitting in a crater of crushed strawberries. Her white jeans were never going to recover.

"Oz," she said. "Like, the Oz. Yellow road, flying monkeys, there's-no-place-like—"

"You've heard of us!" said Burl, delighted. "Then you'll know that people who fall into Oz generally go to the Emerald City to see the ruler. That's Princess Ozma, these days. It's a long walk, I'm afraid. Five days at least."

Five days. On foot. In ruined white jeans. Madison thought about it for exactly one second, and then she stood up, brushed strawberry off her knees, and put two fingers very gently on the back of Burl Tumbletoe's round red hand.

"Burl," she said warmly. "Babe. Don't you remember me?"

Something soft and grey turned over behind his eyes.

"Remember when the river flooded," Madison went on, making it up as she went, which was always the fun part, "and your strawberries were all floating away, and I waded in up to my waist and saved, like, the whole crop? You said I was the best thing that ever happened to this farm. You said if I ever needed anything, I should just say."

Burl's face went slack, then soft, then glowing. His eyes filled with tears.

"The flood," he whispered. "Oh, the flood. How could I forget? You were magnificent. You were—you were soaking wet and you didn't complain once."

"I mean, I complained a little."

"You saved us," said Burl Tumbletoe. "You saved us all. Whatever you need. Anything at all."

Madison smiled. The warm hum came up behind her eyes, the good-song feeling, and she let herself enjoy it.

"Okay, so, slay," she said. "Do you have a cart? And a horse? And maybe some clean pants?"


PART THREE

She was in the Emerald City in two days instead of five, and she arrived in style.

Burl Tumbletoe had given her his best cart and his only horse, a sweet-tempered red mare who, it turned out, could talk, which Madison found deeply unsettling for about an hour and then got used to. The horse's name was Pimpernel and she had opinions about everything, mostly the weather, and she was the first creature in Oz Madison had met that she did not need to use the touch on, because Pimpernel liked her right away for free.

"You've got a nice voice," said the horse. "Very confident. Like someone who's never had to wait in a queue."

"I literally have never," said Madison. "Like, ever."

Along the way she collected things. A red silk dress from a dressmaker in the last Quadling village, who now remembered that Madison had once found her lost wedding ring at the bottom of a well. A basket of food from a family of farmers on the border, who remembered that Madison had sat up all night with their sick grandmother. A pair of green-lensed spectacles from a peddler at the edge of the green country, who remembered that Madison had lent him money when he was broke and never asked for it back. Every time, the soft grey thing turned over behind their eyes, and every time, they wept a little, and every time, they gave her more than she asked for.

It's giving gratitude economy, Madison thought. It's giving main character. It's giving I deserve this.

And then the walls of the Emerald City came up over the green hills, and Madison stopped thinking anything at all for a moment, because it was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen.

The walls were green and studded with emeralds that caught the sun. The towers rose behind them like stalks of glass. The gate was green and taller than ten houses, and the light that came off the whole city was so bright and so green that the air itself seemed to sparkle.

"Okay, that ate," Madison whispered. "That absolutely ate."

At the gate there was a guardian with green whiskers and a green uniform and a big green ledger, and a line of about thirty people waiting to be let in.

Madison did not wait in lines. She climbed down from the cart, sashayed past all thirty of them in her red silk dress, and laid two fingers on the guardian's wrist.

"Hi, oh my god, hi," she said. "Do you not remember me? From the thing? With the fire? When I carried your little niece out of the burning bakery?"

The soft grey thing turned over.

The guardian of the gates of the Emerald City burst into tears, and opened the gate for her personally, and bowed so low his whiskers brushed the green marble.

Behind her, somebody in the line said, "Hey! She cut!"

Madison turned around, still smiling, and touched that person too, very briefly, on the shoulder.

"Don't you remember?" she said. "I'm the one who held your place for you last time."

"Oh," said the person in the line, and blinked, and smiled. "Oh, right. You're so nice. Go ahead."


PART FOUR

She got a room in the best inn in the Emerald City. She got a wardrobe of green gowns from the best dressmaker. She got dinner every night at the best restaurant, where the owner remembered that Madison had once helped him through the worst year of his life, and wept into the soup, and would not take a single coin.

She got all of it in one week. And somewhere in that week she started to notice something strange about Oz.

Nobody was old.

There were grown-ups, of course, and there were people with grey whiskers and spectacles, like the gate guardian. But there was nobody who was old the way people in Kansas were old. Nobody with a cane. Nobody in a wheelchair. Nobody who forgot where they had put their keys, except the ones Madison had made forget.

When she asked the restaurant owner about it, he looked puzzled.

"Nobody grows old in Oz," he said. "Nobody dies of sickness, either. Princess Ozma's grandmother's grandmother made it so, a long time ago, with a great enchantment. Everybody knows that."

"Everybody knows that," Madison repeated. "Okay. But, like, how do they know that?"

"Because they remember," he said, as if it were the simplest thing in the world.

It was the word remember that got under her skin. She lay awake that night in her green bed in the best inn in the Emerald City and thought about it. Because she knew how remembering worked. She knew it better than anybody. She knew that remembering was a soft grey thing that turned over when you touched it, and that it would believe almost anything if you told it warmly enough.

And the thing about Oz, the thing she had been noticing all week without putting words to it, was this: when she touched people here, it was too easy.

In Kansas, there was always a little resistance. A little catch. Like turning a key in a stiff lock. People's memories pushed back, just slightly, before they turned over. Sometimes, if she was tired, a memory wouldn't turn at all, and she'd have to laugh and say sorry, wrong person.

In Oz, there was no catch. Nothing pushed back. Every memory she touched turned over instantly, smoothly, like a door on oiled hinges. As if it had been designed to turn. As if someone had turned it, very many times, long before she ever got here.

"Okay, that's creepy," said Madison to the ceiling. "That's actually so creepy. I hate that."

The ceiling did not reply. But in the morning, there was a summons on her breakfast tray, written on green paper in elegant golden ink.

HER MAJESTY OZMA, PRINCESS OF OZ, REQUESTS THE PLEASURE OF THE COMPANY OF THE YOUNG LADY WHO SAVED THE CITY FROM THE NOME KING.

Madison read it twice.

She had never told anyone she'd saved the city from the Nome King. She didn't even know who the Nome King was. Somebody, somewhere along the chain of people she'd touched, must have embroidered the story—remembered her rescue of their niece or their soup or their wedding ring, and then remembered more, filling in gaps, the way memories do, until the girl in the red silk dress had become a hero of the whole Emerald City.

That had never happened before. Her stories didn't spread. They stayed where she put them.

In Oz, they were growing on their own.

"Oh, I'm so cooked," said Madison, and went to get dressed.


PART FIVE

The throne room of the Emerald City was green glass and gold, and it was full of people who loved her.

That was the first thing she noticed, and it should have felt amazing. Every face in the room turned toward her as she came in, and every face was shining with the same soft, damp-eyed gratitude she had seen on Burl Tumbletoe and the gate guardian and the restaurant owner. Courtiers. Soldiers. Ladies in green silk. A man made of pumpkins who kept bowing and whose head kept nearly falling off. They all remembered her. They all remembered her saving them.

None of them had ever met her.

On the throne sat a girl about Madison's age, with dark hair and a gold circlet and a face that was kind and serious all at once. Princess Ozma. Beside her stood the Tin Woodman, polished so bright he hurt to look at, and on the step below the throne sat a man made of a stuffed sack in a blue coat, with a painted face and a hat, chewing thoughtfully on nothing.

"You are the young woman who drove back the Nome King's army at the western gate," said Ozma, rising. "The whole court remembers it. I confess I do not remember it myself, which troubles me, because I am very sure I would remember such a thing. Will you tell me what happened?"

Madison opened her mouth. Then she closed it. Then she did what she always did, which was walk up to the throne, smiling, and reach out two fingers toward the back of the Princess's hand.

"Your Majesty," she said, "you totally remember. You were there. You—"

"Excuse me," said the Scarecrow. "I wonder if you would mind touching me first."

Madison stopped.

"Only," the Scarecrow went on, getting up rather loosely from the step, "I have been watching people's faces when they talk about you, and they all have exactly the same look, and I have only ever seen that look once before, in a very old book, and I have been wanting to test a theory. My brains, you see, were made by the Wizard out of bran, mixed with a great many pins and needles. They are excellent brains. But I don't believe they remember things in quite the same way that a meat brain does." He held out his soft cloth hand. "Would you be so kind?"

The whole court was watching. Ozma was watching. The Tin Woodman was watching, and the light ran over his tin face so that Madison could not tell what his expression was.

She touched the Scarecrow's hand.

"Don't you remember," she said, "when I saved you from the crows?"

Nothing turned over.

There was nothing soft there, nothing grey. Touching the Scarecrow's mind was like putting her hand into a sack of meal and finding, here and there, a sharp little prick of a pin. Bran and needles. Thoughts like pinpoints. No place to put a memory at all.

"I'm afraid I don't," said the Scarecrow, very gently. "Do you know, I don't think I've ever been saved from crows. I've frightened a good many. But I'm very curious what it felt like when you tried. You flinched."

The court murmured. Madison pulled her hand back as if she'd touched a stove.

"It's a whole thing," she said, too fast. "It's, like, a vibe thing, it doesn't work on everyone, it's not—"

"Madison Pryce," said a new voice from the doorway. "Please don't touch anyone else."

Everyone turned.

A woman stood at the far end of the throne room. She was tall and very beautiful and dressed all in white, with red-gold hair falling to her waist, and she carried in both arms an enormous book, bound in leather and clasped in silver, so heavy that two young women in red uniforms walked beside her to help bear its weight.

"That's Glinda the Good," whispered the Pumpkinhead, to nobody in particular. "The Sorceress of the South."

"I know who she is," said Madison. "Everybody knows who she is. Why does she know who I am?"

Glinda came down the length of the throne room and set the great book down on a green marble table before the throne, and opened it.

"This is the Great Book of Records," she said. "It is kept in my castle in the Quadling Country, and in it is written, as it happens, everything that happens anywhere in Oz. Every word spoken, every deed done, every footstep. It cannot be lied to. It cannot be flattered. It cannot be touched." She turned the pages. "Shall I read what it says about the Nome King's attack on the western gate?"

Madison said nothing.

"It says nothing," said Glinda, "because there was no attack. And shall I read what it says about a flood in the strawberry fields of Burl Tumbletoe?"

Madison said nothing.

"There was no flood," said Glinda. "There was no burning bakery. There was no sick grandmother. There was no lost wedding ring. There was a young woman who fell out of the sky, and walked across Oz in a borrowed cart, and touched every person she met, and told them a story, and the stories went into their memories as smoothly as water goes into sand." She looked up. Her eyes were not angry. They were very old, much older than her face, and very sad. "Do you know, Madison, the Book has not recorded anything like you in a very, very long time?"


PART SIX

They did not throw her in a dungeon. Madison had been fully expecting a dungeon. She had been planning how to talk her way out of the dungeon. Instead, Ozma dismissed the court, which went out sniffling and glowing and still, every one of them, fondly remembering things that had not happened, and then Ozma and Glinda and the Scarecrow and the Tin Woodman and Madison sat down around the green marble table with the Great Book of Records open between them.

"I'm not going to apologize," said Madison immediately. "I want to put that out there. Like, nobody got hurt. Everybody gave me stuff because they wanted to. They were happy. Literally look at them, they were crying, they were so happy."

"Were they happy," said the Tin Woodman quietly, "or did they only remember being happy?"

"Is there a difference?"

"I have a heart that the Wizard put in me," said the Tin Woodman. "A silk heart stuffed with sawdust. I did not have one before. And I have spent a great deal of time since wondering whether the love I feel is real, or only something the heart makes me remember feeling." He paused. "I decided it does not matter, for me, because I chose to have the heart. I asked for it. Did Burl Tumbletoe ask for his flood?"

Madison looked away.

"I would like to tell you something," said Glinda. "It is not something I have ever told anyone. Not even Ozma." She glanced at the Princess, who nodded slowly. "But I think you, of everyone in Oz, deserve to hear it. Because you are the first person in a very, very long time who can do what the Founders did."

"The who?"

Glinda turned the Book of Records back. And back. And back, through thousands of pages, each one dense with tiny writing—every sneeze in Oz, every song, every argument over a fence—until the pages grew yellow, and then brown, and then something that was not quite paper, thin and cool and faintly shining, like the inside of a shell. The writing on these pages was not in letters at all. It was in little spirals, and lines, and dots.

"The Book records everything that happens in Oz," said Glinda. "Everything. Since the very beginning. And most people believe that the beginning was when a great fairy, Lurline, came through this land and enchanted it, and made it magic, and made it so nobody grows old. That is the story everyone in Oz remembers."

"Is it, like, not true?"

"It is a memory," said Glinda. "It is a very old, very beautiful memory that was put into the people of Oz on purpose, a long time ago. The Book records what actually happened. It took me a hundred years to learn to read these pages. I wish, some days, that I never had."

She laid her hand on the shining page.

"Oz was not made by a fairy," she said. "Oz was settled. By people. By humans, Madison. Humans like you."


PART SEVEN

This is the story the Great Book of Records told, in its spirals and lines and dots, and that Glinda read aloud by the green light of the throne room while Madison sat with her mouth open.

A very long time ago, longer ago than any history in the outside world remembers, longer ago than the pyramids or the cave paintings or the first fire, the human family was not one family. It was many branches, growing out of the same old root. Some branches were tall and some were short. Some went north and some went south. Most of them withered, one by one, until only one branch was left, the branch that became the people of Kansas and Paris and Peking and everywhere else.

But one branch did not wither. It was cut off.

It was a small branch, a few thousand people, living in a green valley on the far side of a great ocean. And it had grown a gift that no other branch had. They could touch one another's minds. Not read them. Not control them. Remember them. They could reach into another person's remembering and lay something there, gently, the way you lay a blanket over a sleeping child. A memory of a meal they had not eaten. A memory of a kindness they had not received. A memory of a place they had never been.

They used it, at first, the way Madison had used it. For favors. For little things. And then for bigger things. And then they stopped being able to tell which of their memories were real.

"They forgot what had actually happened to them," Glinda read. "They forgot who had hurt whom and who had helped whom. They forgot which of their ancestors had been heroes and which had been thieves, because the thieves had all laid memories of being heroes. They started wars over things that had never happened. And when the ice came down out of the north, and the other branches of the human family came over the ice, hungry, and found the green valley, the people of the valley could not even agree on whether they had been attacked before, or by whom, or why."

"So what happened?" said Madison. Her voice had gone small.

"Their wisest people met together. They called themselves the Rememberers. There were nine of them. And they made a choice. They decided to cut the valley off from the rest of the world, forever. To make a border so terrible that nothing could cross it. They poured everything they had into the cutting. All their gift, all at once, into the ground around the valley." Glinda's finger moved across the shining page. "The ground died. It became the Deadly Desert. The sand that destroys anyone who touches it. It is not a natural thing. It is a scar. It is where the Rememberers cut Oz away from the human family, like a gardener cutting a branch off a tree and planting it somewhere new. Debranched. That is the word the Book uses."

The Scarecrow's painted face had gone very still.

"How long ago?" he asked.

Glinda looked down at the page for a long moment.

"The Book counts in seasons," she said. "I have counted them three times. Each time, the answer is the same. A little over two million years."

Nobody spoke.

"Two million years," whispered Ozma. "Oz is two million years old?"

"Oz is two million years old," said Glinda, "and the people of Oz are humans, Ozma. A different branch. A cousin branch. Cut off from the others before the others even learned to make fire. Kept alive inside the desert all this time, growing differently. The animals talk because the Rememberers laid language into the minds of the creatures who lived in the valley with them, so that they would not be lonely after the cutting. Nobody grows old because the Rememberers laid into every mind in Oz, forever, the memory of being young, and in Oz, minds and bodies are not as separate as they are outside. And magic—" She smiled, very sadly. "Magic, in Oz, is only the old gift, gone strange over two million years. Every witch and wizard and sorceress in Oz, myself included, is simply a Rememberer's great-great-grandchild, doing very badly what the Rememberers did very well."

"And Lurline?" said Ozma. "The fairy queen? The enchantment? I remember learning it as a child. Everyone remembers it."

"That," said Glinda, "is the last thing the Rememberers ever did. When the cutting was done, they were dying. They had spent too much. And they knew that if the people of Oz remembered being cut off—remembered the wars, and the lies, and the ice, and the terrible choice—they would never be happy in the valley. They would always be looking over the desert, wondering what was on the other side. So the nine of them, with their last strength, laid one memory into every mind in Oz. A beautiful memory. A fairy queen who came through and made the land magic, and loved it, and left. And then they made it so that every memory in Oz would turn easily. So that the story would never be questioned. So that it would stay."

Madison felt something cold go down her back.

"That's why it's so easy here," she said. "That's why nothing pushes back. Oh my god. Oh my god, they oiled the hinges. They oiled the hinges on everybody's brain two million years ago, and nobody ever noticed, and then I showed up and just walked right in."


PART EIGHT

There was a long, long silence in the throne room of the Emerald City.

Then Ozma said, "There's something else. Isn't there? You keep looking at Madison. Not at the Book. At her."

Glinda turned the shining pages forward again, slowly, through the long ages, until they turned to paper, and the spirals turned to letters, and then forward again, very fast, through the last few hundred years. She stopped at a page about halfway through and laid her finger on a short line of tiny, crabbed writing.

"The Deadly Desert is a scar," she said. "But scars are not perfect. Every so often, perhaps once in a thousand years, the cut opens, just a little, along the seam. Usually at the bottom of a great storm. That is how the Wizard came, in his balloon. That is how a little girl from Kansas came, in her house. That is how a great many people came, over the years, whom nobody in Oz ever wrote about."

"Okay?"

"The cut opens both ways, Madison."

She ran her finger down the page.

"Three hundred and eleven years ago, a Gillikin girl named Aspen Thrale was picking berries at the very edge of the northern desert during a storm, and the seam opened under her, and she fell through. The Book records her falling out of Oz. It does not record where she landed, because the Book can only see Oz. But it records that she was a great-great-great-granddaughter, many hundred times over, of one of the nine Rememberers. And that the old gift was very strong in her family."

Madison stared at the page.

"Aspen," she said. "That's—my mom's middle name is Aspen. It's, like, a family name. It goes back forever. My grandma's grandma had it. There's a whole thing about it in our family Bible, the first Aspen who came out of nowhere during a storm in, like, the seventeen hundreds and married a farmer in—" She stopped. "In Kansas."

"You didn't fall into Oz by accident," said Glinda gently. "You fell home. The seam knows its own. It opened under you because you are one of the very last people anywhere in the world who carries the old gift. The gift of the branch that was cut off."

Madison put her face in her hands.

"Okay, this is so much," she said through her fingers. "This is way too much. I came here for, like, free coffee energy. I came here to get a nicer dress. Now I'm a two-million-year-old—what, a cousin species? Of my own species? With hereditary brain magic that literally broke an entire civilization? This is not giving what I thought it was giving."

The Scarecrow reached across the table and patted her arm, very gently, with his soft cloth hand. There was nothing soft and grey there for her to touch. Just bran and pins. Just a friend.

"Do you know," he said, "I have very good brains, and I have been thinking with them this whole time, and I think the question is not what you are. I think the question is what you are going to do about it."


PART NINE

"There are two things we could do," said Glinda.

They had been talking for hours. The light in the throne room had gone from green to deep green to the color of a forest at night. Somebody had brought tea, which nobody drank.

"The first is to do what the Rememberers did," said Glinda. "To keep the secret. To put the Book away. To let Oz go on remembering Lurline and her enchantment and never knowing what it really is, or how it came to be, or what it cost. Oz would stay happy. Nothing would change."

"And the second?" said Ozma.

"The second," said Glinda, "is to tell the truth. To everyone. That Oz is a colony of human beings, cut off two million years ago, living inside a scar. That the fairy queen never existed. That every memory in Oz turns too easily, and has been turning, for all that time, for anyone who knows how to turn it." She looked at Madison. "Anyone at all."

Ozma was quiet for a long time.

"I am the ruler of Oz," she said at last. "I have always believed that my people were mine to protect. And I think a part of me would very much like to put the Book away. To let them be happy." She looked down at her own hands. "But they would only be remembering being happy. And I have spent my whole reign trying to be a ruler who does not lie to her people. If I put the Book away now, knowing what I know, then I am only the tenth Rememberer. Laying a blanket over everyone, so they don't have to see."

"So you'll tell them?" said Madison.

"I'll tell them," said Ozma. "But that isn't the hard part. The hard part is that once they know, the hinges will still be oiled. Anyone with even a little of the old gift could walk into Oz and do what you did. Make them remember anything. Make them believe anything. The Rememberers made Oz defenseless against exactly the thing that ruined it in the first place."

"Unless someone takes the oil off," said the Tin Woodman.

Everyone looked at him.

"I don't know very much about magic," he said, a little embarrassed. "But I was made of tin, piece by piece, and I have rusted, and been oiled again, many times. And I know that if a hinge is oiled for too long, nobody remembers how to open the door by themselves. They just wait for it to swing." He turned his polished face toward Madison. "If you can lay a memory in someone's mind, can you also lift one out?"

Madison stared at him.

"I've never tried," she said slowly. "I've never, like, wanted to. Taking them out isn't how you get free coffee."

"No," said the Scarecrow. "But it might be how you get something better."


PART TEN

It started with Burl Tumbletoe.

Madison went back to the Quadling Country herself, in the red cart, with Pimpernel the horse pulling and offering opinions the whole way. It took five days, because this time she did not touch anyone to make it shorter. She ate food she had bought with coins Ozma had given her, and she slept in an inn where she paid for her bed, and when the innkeeper asked if they had met before, she said no.

It felt terrible. It felt like walking on a broken ankle.

"You're very quiet," said Pimpernel on the fourth day.

"I'm, like, detoxing," said Madison. "From being a horrible person. It's not giving fun."

"I didn't think you were horrible," said the horse. "I liked you without any of that business."

"Yeah, well," said Madison, and found, to her horror, that she was about to cry. "You're a horse, babe. Your standards are literally grass."

Burl Tumbletoe was in his strawberry field when she came up the road. He waved at her, beaming, and came hurrying over with his watering can.

"The hero of the flood!" he cried. "Oh, it's so good to see you! You'll stay for dinner? You must stay for dinner. After what you did for us—"

"Burl," said Madison. "I need you to hold still. And I need to tell you something, and you're going to be so mad, and that's okay. That's actually valid. You should be mad."

She put two fingers on the back of his hand. And this time, instead of laying something down, she reached for the soft grey memory she had put there and lifted it, very gently, the way you lift a blanket off a sleeping child in the morning. It came away easily. It had never really belonged there.

Burl blinked.

He looked down at his field, which had never flooded. He looked at his strawberries, which no one had ever saved. He looked at the empty place in his barn where his best cart and his only horse used to be.

He looked at Madison.

"You," he said. "You made me remember a flood."

"Yeah."

"There wasn't any flood."

"No."

"You took my horse!"

"Technically you gave me your horse, but I made you give me your horse, so, yeah. I took your horse." Madison swallowed. "She's right here. Her name's Pimpernel, she's amazing, she has, like, so many opinions about clouds. I brought her back. And the cart. And I'm going to pay you for the days I used them, with real money, that the Princess gave me, which I'm going to pay back by working. And I'm so sorry. I'm so, so sorry. I've never said that to anybody before and meant it."

Burl Tumbletoe stood in his strawberry field for a long time with his watering can in his hand.

Then he said, "Do you know, the thing I'm angriest about isn't the horse."

"No?"

"It's that I was happy," said Burl. "I was so happy, remembering that you'd saved my farm. I told everybody. I told my brother and my cousin and the whole village. I was so proud to know someone so brave. And none of it was true." He wiped his eyes with a red sleeve. "And now I have to go tell them all that I was wrong."

"I'll come with you," said Madison. "I'll tell them. It's on me. It's literally all on me."

And she did.


PART ELEVEN

It took her a year to undo it all.

Every person she had touched, from the Quadling Country to the Emerald City. Burl Tumbletoe and his whole village. The dressmaker with the lost wedding ring. The family with the sick grandmother. The peddler with the green spectacles. The guardian of the gates, who cried even harder when he remembered that she had never saved his niece than he had when he remembered that she had, and then, to her absolute shock, hugged her anyway.

"You came back to tell me," he said into her shoulder, his green whiskers tickling her ear. "Nobody ever comes back to tell you anything."

The restaurant owner made her wash dishes for a month to pay back all the dinners. She was so bad at washing dishes. She broke six plates. She learned to do it right. By the end of the month the restaurant owner had started saving her the burnt ends of the roast, because he said a person who was that bad at dishes and kept showing up anyway deserved the best part of the meal.

The court of the Emerald City was the hardest. There were so many of them, and the story of the Nome King's attack had grown so big, with so many embroideries that other people had added, that some of them had built whole parts of their lives around it. One young soldier had joined the guard because he remembered being inspired by her bravery at the western gate. When she lifted the memory out, he sat down on the green marble steps with his head in his hands and said he didn't know who he was anymore.

"You're the guy who joined the guard," said Madison, sitting down next to him. "That part was you. I just gave you the wrong reason. You can find a better one. Like, a real one."

He found one. It took him a while. But he found one.

And while Madison went from person to person, lifting her stories out of their minds, Glinda and Ozma did something harder still.

They told Oz the truth.

Ozma stood on the balcony of her palace above the great square of the Emerald City and read aloud from a copy of the shining pages of the Great Book of Records, which Glinda had spent a year translating into plain words. She read about the many branches of the human family and the one that was cut off. She read about the gift and the wars and the ice. She read about the nine Rememberers and the Deadly Desert, the scar around the world they lived in. She read about the fairy queen who had never been, and the oil on every hinge in every mind in Oz.

The square was silent for a very long time after she finished.

Then someone at the back of the crowd—it was the Patchwork Girl, who had come to the Emerald City long after the Rememberers and had never had any hinges in her at all, only stuffing—shouted out:

"So we're all humans? Even the Munchkins? Even the Winkies? Even the Hammer-Heads?"

"Even the Hammer-Heads," said Ozma.

"Two million years of being cousins and nobody knew!" cried the Patchwork Girl. "Well, that's the most wonderful family reunion I ever heard of!" And she turned a cartwheel right across the front row, and somebody laughed, and then somebody else laughed, and then the whole square was laughing and crying and shouting all at once, the way people do when the worst news and the best news turn out to be the same news.

It was not all laughing, afterward. There were people who were angry, and people who were frightened, and a great many long arguments in a great many village halls about what it all meant. Some people wanted to cross the desert and find the rest of the human family. Some people wanted to make the desert even wider. The Nome King, who lived underneath the desert and had always rather enjoyed being the scariest thing in that part of the world, sent a very long and very rude letter complaining that his neighborhood was being made to sound like a scar.

But the hinges began, very slowly, to stiffen. Memories in Oz began to push back, just a little, when somebody tried to turn them. People started arguing about what had really happened at last year's harvest festival, and checking with each other, and writing things down. It was messier. It was louder.

It was, Glinda said, much more human.


PART TWELVE

On the first anniversary of the day she fell out of the sky, Madison Pryce stood at the very edge of the Deadly Desert, in the north, where three hundred and twelve years earlier a Gillikin girl named Aspen Thrale had been picking berries in a storm.

The sand stretched away in front of her, grey and still, the long scar around the world. Somewhere on the other side of it was Kansas. The Gas-N-Go off Route 54. Her mom, who had been Aspen's great-great-something-granddaughter without ever knowing it. A clerk named Kyle who probably still remembered a rainy night at the Sonic that had never happened.

"You could go back," said the Scarecrow, who had come with her, because he liked edges. "Glinda thinks she could open the seam. Just once. Just for you."

"I know," said Madison.

"Will you?"

Madison thought about it. She thought about Kyle. She thought about her mom, and her friends, and her landlord, and her teachers, and every single person in Kansas who was walking around right now with a soft grey story in their head that she had put there. It would take years to undo all of it. It might take her whole life.

She thought about Burl Tumbletoe's strawberry field, and the restaurant owner's burnt ends, and the soldier on the steps. She thought about Pimpernel, who was waiting for her back at the cart, probably developing a new opinion about the wind.

"Not yet," she said. "Like, eventually. Yeah. There's so much I have to un-do back there. But not yet." She shoved her hands in the pockets of the green coat she had bought herself, with money she had earned, washing dishes she had mostly not broken. "There's still so many people here I haven't apologized to. And honestly? I kind of want to stay and see what happens. Two million years of everyone being chill and then suddenly everyone's arguing about harvest festivals? That's literally the most interesting place in the world right now. I'm not leaving. I'd be so FOMO."

The Scarecrow laughed, a soft, rustling, bran-and-pins sort of laugh.

"Do you know," he said, "I have very good brains. And I think that may be the most sensible thing I have ever heard anybody say."

They walked back together along the edge of the desert, the girl in the green coat and the man made of a stuffed sack, toward the cart and the talking horse and the long road back into Oz. Behind them, the Deadly Desert lay grey and silent under the evening sky, and for the first time in two million years, somebody in Oz was looking at it and remembering, clearly and truly and with all its pain, exactly what it was.

It wasn't giving fairy tale anymore.

It was giving real.

And that, Madison decided, slaps so much harder.
