PART ONE

The first hiccup happened at breakfast.

Tam Pottlebury was ten years old and the youngest kitchen-boy in the royal palace of the Emerald City, which meant that he got up before anybody else, and carried the eggs, and was shouted at by the head cook whenever an egg broke, which was often, because Tam was a Quadling from the red south and had grown up on a farm where eggs were things you collected from hens, not things you carried up four flights of marble stairs in a basket with a squeaky handle.

On the morning of the first hiccup he was halfway up the third flight with fourteen eggs, and he was being very, very careful, and then the whole palace went:

HIC.

It was not a sound, exactly. It was more of a feeling. A sudden jolt, as if the palace had sat up very straight and then slumped back down, all in an instant. The marble stairs jumped under Tam's feet. The chandelier above him swung. And the basket of eggs was simply not in his hands anymore.

Tam looked down at his empty fingers. Then he looked around.

The eggs were sitting on the stair in front of him, in a neat row, all fourteen of them, unbroken.

And the stair was not a stair. It was grass.

Tam was standing in the middle of a field. A purple field, with purple grass and purple clover and a purple fence, under a purple-tinged sky, and a cow was looking at him over the purple fence with an expression of mild surprise, chewing.

"Moo," said the cow, in a Gillikin accent.

Tam picked up his eggs very slowly, one by one, and put them back in the basket.

"Excuse me," he said to the cow. "Where am I?"

"North," said the cow. "Gillikin Country. Farmer Ambrell's lower pasture. Where did you come from?"

"The palace," said Tam. "The third flight of stairs."

The cow considered this, chewing.

"Huh," she said. "That's a long way to come for eggs."

Then the ground went HIC again, and the cow was gone, and the purple field was gone, and Tam was standing on the third flight of stairs in the Emerald City palace with a basket of fourteen eggs in his hands, exactly where he had started.

Except that there was a purple clover leaf stuck to his shoe.

And the head cook was standing at the top of the stairs, looking down at him, holding a frying pan, with a cow in the kitchen behind her.


PART TWO

By lunchtime the whole of Oz was hiccupping.

It happened every few minutes, with no warning at all. HIC, and the world jumped, and things that had been in one place were suddenly in another. Not everything. Not everyone. Just a few things, each time, chosen apparently at random, and swapped with something somewhere else.

In the Munchkin Country, a blue farmhouse was hiccupped into the middle of the Winkie Country, where it stood in a yellow field looking very startled, and a yellow tin-shop appeared in its place, which the Munchkins found so upsetting that they put a blue tarpaulin over it until it went back.

In the Quadling Country, a whole pond full of red fish was hiccupped up to the roof of the Scarecrow's corncob house, where it sloshed about uncomfortably, and the Scarecrow had to sit in the kitchen with an umbrella until the next hiccup took it away again.

In the Emerald City, the Royal Army of Oz, which consisted of twenty-seven officers and one private, was hiccupped into the middle of the Deadly Desert, where it stood for about four seconds in great alarm before the next hiccup brought it back, minus one officer's hat, which had touched the sand and turned to dust.

And Tam Pottlebury was hiccupped four more times before noon. Once into the Tin Woodman's tin bathtub (empty, thankfully). Once into a tree in the Quadling Country, where a family of talking squirrels told him off for sitting on their dinner. Once into the very middle of Princess Ozma's throne room, in front of the whole court, with a dishcloth on his head. And once, very briefly, to the top of the highest tower in the Emerald City, where he clung to a green flagpole for two whole minutes in a high wind, screaming, before the next hiccup brought him down again.

"Why me?" he wailed to the head cook, back in the kitchen, still clutching the dishcloth. "Why does it keep happening to me?"

"It's happening to everybody," said the head cook, who had been hiccupped twice herself, once into a bakery in the Winkie Country and once onto the back of a very surprised elephant. "Stop complaining and peel those potatoes."

But it wasn't happening to everybody. Not as much. Tam had heard the other kitchen-boys talking. Most people had been hiccupped once, or twice. Nobody had been hiccupped five times in one morning except Tam.

And when he looked down at the potatoes, he saw that they had all, at some point while he wasn't watching, turned purple.


PART THREE

Princess Ozma called a meeting.

The meeting was held in the great green throne room, and it was attended by the Princess, the Wizard of Oz, the Scarecrow (who had dried off from the pond), the Tin Woodman, Jack Pumpkinhead, the Patchwork Girl, a large and very hungry tiger who kept asking whether hiccups were edible, and Tik-Tok, the clockwork man, who had been specially wound up for the occasion and stood ticking quietly in the corner.

And, because he had been hiccupped into the throne room in the middle of the meeting and nobody had thought to make him leave, Tam Pottlebury, still holding a potato.

"It is not only the Emerald City," said Ozma. "My Magic Picture shows hiccups in every corner of Oz. Things are being moved. People are being moved. So far nobody has been hurt, but it is getting worse. They were coming every ten minutes this morning. Now they come every five."

HIC.

The throne room jumped. A chandelier swapped places with a chandelier in some other palace, a much smaller and pinker chandelier, which looked ridiculous. The Hungry Tiger vanished and was replaced by a very confused sheep. Then HIC again, and the sheep vanished, and the Tiger was back, looking puzzled and licking his lips.

"That sheep smelled delicious," he said. "Is there a way to make it happen again?"

"Absolutely not," said Ozma.

"Well," said the Wizard, rubbing his bald head. "I've looked in every book of magic I own. I've looked in some books of magic I don't own, which I'm going to have to return to the library with an apology. And I can tell you this much: these are not magical hiccups. Nobody is casting a spell. Nobody is doing it on purpose."

"Then what is it?" asked the Tin Woodman.

The Wizard hesitated.

"I believe," he said slowly, "that it is Oz."

There was a pause.

"I'm sorry?" said Ozma.

"Oz," said the Wizard. "The land itself. I believe the Land of Oz has the hiccups."

"Lands can't have hiccups," said Jack Pumpkinhead, who was not very clever, but was very sure of things. "Lands don't have stomachs."

"In most places, no," said the Wizard. "But Oz is not most places. Oz is a fairyland. Its trees talk, its rivers sing, its animals give opinions about the weather. Why shouldn't the land itself have a sort of body? And if it has a body, why shouldn't it get the hiccups?" He spread his hands. "What I don't know is why. People get hiccups when they've eaten too fast, or swallowed something wrong. What could a whole country have swallowed?"

Nobody said anything.

Then, very quietly, from the back of the room, Tam Pottlebury said:

"I think it was a star."


PART FOUR

Everyone turned to look at him.

Tam went very red, which for a Quadling is saying something, because Quadlings are fairly red to begin with.

"Go on," said Ozma kindly. "What do you mean, a star?"

"Last night," said Tam. "I couldn't sleep. The kitchen-boys sleep in the attic, and there's a little window, and I was looking out of it, because I miss the sky at home. In the Quadling Country you can see all the stars, but here the city's so green and bright you can only see a few. And I was looking at them, and one of them fell."

"Stars fall all the time," said the Scarecrow thoughtfully. "Or at least, little bits of sky-stone do. They burn up in the air."

"This one didn't burn up," said Tam. "It came straight down. It was big and bright and kind of greenish-gold, and it came right down into the middle of the city, and I thought I heard it land. Like a—" He thought about it. "Like a gulp. A big gulp. And then everything was quiet. And then this morning I was carrying the eggs, and the first hiccup happened."

The Wizard had gone very still.

"Where?" he said. "Where in the middle of the city?"

"I don't know exactly," said Tam. "It looked like it went down near the fountain in the Great Square. But it didn't hit the fountain. It went into the ground. Right through the paving stones. Like it was being swallowed."

"The Great Square," said Ozma slowly. "That's the very center of the Emerald City. And the Emerald City is the very center of Oz."

"The very center of Oz," said the Wizard, "would be where a land keeps its stomach." He turned to Tam. "Young man, I think you may have just solved the greatest mystery in the country's history. Do you know what you saw?"

Tam shook his head.

"I think," said the Wizard, "you saw Oz swallow a star. Too fast. Without chewing. And now it's got the hiccups. And unless we can get that star out again, I'm afraid they are going to keep getting worse, until—"

HIC.

The throne room vanished.


PART FIVE

When it came back, Tam was not in it.

He was sitting on a rock in a grey, empty place, under a grey, empty sky, and there was nobody near him at all. The ground around him was a hard, dark, glittering stone, and very far off in every direction he could see the shimmer of the Deadly Desert, like a heat-haze on the horizon, all the way around.

And it was dark. Not night-dark. Underneath-dark. As if he were at the bottom of something.

Tam's heart was pounding. He had been hiccupped six times now, and every time before, the next hiccup had brought him back. He sat on his rock and clutched his potato and waited for the next HIC.

It came. The ground jumped. But Tam stayed exactly where he was.

"Oh no," he whispered. "Oh no, oh no."

He waited for the next one. And the next. They came faster now, every minute or so, every one a little bigger than the last, each one making the dark glittering ground jump and shudder. But nothing swapped. Nothing moved. It was as if, wherever Tam was, he was already in the place that everything else was being hiccupped away from.

And then, after the seventh or eighth hiccup, Tam noticed the light.

It was very faint at first. A greenish-gold glow, coming from somewhere below the rock he was sitting on. He knelt down and pressed his face to the glittering stone and looked, and the glow was there, deep down, pulsing. Brighter with every hiccup, and then fading again.

It was the star.

He was in the middle of Oz. Under the Great Square. Inside the stomach of the land.

And the star was right underneath him.

"Hello?" said Tam to the glow, feeling very foolish. "Hello? Are you a star?"

There was a pause. Then a voice came up out of the stone, faint and bright and high, like a bell ringing a long way off.

"Am I?" it said. "I don't know. I don't know anything. It's very dark in here. Who are you?"

"I'm Tam," said Tam. "I'm a kitchen-boy. I saw you fall."

"I fell," said the voice. "I remember that. I was up very high, with all the others, and then I was falling, and it was so fast, and then something big opened up underneath me, and I went down, and down, and now I'm stuck." It sounded very small. "Is it always this dark down here?"

"I think you're inside Oz," said Tam. "I think Oz swallowed you by mistake. And now it's got the hiccups. And everything's being moved around, all over the country. And it's getting worse."

"Oh," said the star. "Oh, I'm so sorry. I don't mean to. I'm only trying to get out. Every time I push, there's a big jump, and I go up a little, and then I fall back down." Its light pulsed, faint and frightened. "I'm so tired. I don't think I can push much more."

HIC.

The ground jumped. Tam was knocked flat on his rock. The glow beneath him flared, bright green-gold, and then, very slowly, sank back down, dimmer than before.

"See?" whispered the star. "I can't do it. I'm not strong enough."

Tam lay on the rock with his cheek against the cold glittering stone and thought.

He thought about eggs. About carrying fourteen eggs up four flights of marble stairs, one careful step at a time, with a basket with a squeaky handle. About how, when he first came to the palace, he had tried to run up the stairs, and broken every single egg, and been shouted at. And how old Bessamy, the second cook, a very fat and very kind Munchkin woman with blue ribbons in her apron, had taken him aside afterward and said, quietly:

"You can't carry eggs fast, Tam. Not up stairs. You have to carry them slow. One step, and then rest. One step, and then rest. If you try to do it all at once, you'll drop the lot."

"Star," said Tam. "Listen. I've got an idea."


PART SIX

Up in the throne room, everyone was searching for Tam.

They had searched every corner of the palace. They had searched the kitchens, where the head cook had burst into tears, which surprised everybody, especially the head cook. They had searched the attic, and the gardens, and the stables. The Wizard had looked in a crystal ball, and the Scarecrow had thought very hard, and Ozma had stood in front of her Magic Picture and asked it, over and over, to show her the Quadling kitchen-boy named Tam Pottlebury.

And the Magic Picture showed her only a faint green-gold glow, deep in the dark, and a small shape lying on a rock beside it, and nothing else.

"He's under the city," said Ozma. "He's down where the star is. He must have been hiccupped there, and now he can't get out."

"Then we'll dig," said the Tin Woodman, lifting his axe. "We'll dig through the Great Square, stone by stone, until we find him."

"You can't," said the Wizard. "If we dig a hole in Oz's stomach, we'll make the hiccups ten times worse. We could break the whole country apart."

HIC.

The palace jumped so hard that the throne fell over. A wall of the throne room swapped with a wall of a barn in the Gillikin Country; through it, astonished pigs stared in at the court.

"They're getting worse," said the Patchwork Girl, who had been turning cartwheels to keep calm and had stopped now because she kept being hiccupped mid-cartwheel into other people's living rooms. "Much, much worse. Soon there won't be any Oz left that's in the right place."

Ozma stood in front of the Magic Picture with her hands clenched.

And then the picture changed.

The faint glow in the dark grew a little brighter. The small shape on the rock sat up. And then, very slowly, as Ozma watched, the glow began to rise.

Not in a jump. Not in a hiccup. Slowly. Steadily. A little at a time. It rose a few inches, and stopped, and pulsed, as if resting. Then it rose a few inches more. And stopped. And rested.

And the shape that was Tam Pottlebury, very small and very far down, rose with it, holding onto the glow with both arms, like someone carrying a basket up a flight of stairs.

"What is he doing?" whispered the Scarecrow.

The Wizard was staring at the picture with his mouth open.

"He's counting," he said. "Look at the rhythm. Up, and rest. Up, and rest. He's teaching it to climb."

"And the hiccups," said Ozma. "Listen."

They listened.

The palace was quiet. A minute passed. Two. No HIC came. Through the hole in the wall, the Gillikin pigs blinked, as if they too were waiting for something that had stopped happening.

"The star isn't pushing anymore," said the Wizard softly. "That's what was causing it. Every time it pushed all at once, Oz jumped. But if it climbs a little at a time, a little and then a rest, a little and then a rest, then Oz doesn't jump. Oz just—"

He paused, listening to the deep silence underneath the palace.

"Oz just breathes," he said.


PART SEVEN

It took the whole night.

Tam did not know it was a whole night, because there was no night or day down in the dark, only the glow of the star and the cold of the stone. He only knew that his arms ached from holding on, and his legs ached from bracing, and his throat ached from counting.

"One step," he would say. "And rest."

And the star would rise, a few inches, and stop.

"One step. And rest."

Rise. Stop.

It was very boring. It was the most boring thing Tam had ever done, more boring than carrying eggs, more boring than peeling potatoes. Every so often the star wanted to hurry, wanted to push hard and get out all at once, and Tam had to say, "No, no, slow, slow, remember the eggs," and the star would say, "What eggs?" and Tam would explain about the eggs again, and the four flights of stairs, and Bessamy with the blue ribbons in her apron.

"She sounds nice," said the star, after the fifth time.

"She is," said Tam. "She's the only person in the palace who isn't always shouting at me. She gives me the burnt bits of the bread, because she knows I like them."

"I've never had bread," said the star.

"When we get out, I'll give you some," said Tam. "The burnt bits. I'll save them for you."

"Can stars eat bread?"

"I don't know," said Tam. "We'll find out. One step. And rest."

Somewhere around the middle of the night, he felt the dark change. The glittering stone above them, which had been hard and solid and cold, began to feel softer, and warmer, and there was a faint grey light in it, coming from somewhere very far above.

"What's that?" whispered the star.

"I think it's up," said Tam.

"Is up nice?"

"Up's lovely," said Tam. "Up has sky. Up has all the other stars. You'll like it. One step."

The star took one step.

"And rest," said Tam.


PART EIGHT

The sun was just coming up over the Emerald City when the paving stones in the middle of the Great Square began to glow.

The whole court was there. They had been standing there since midnight, Ozma and the Wizard and the Scarecrow and the Tin Woodman and the Patchwork Girl and Jack Pumpkinhead and the Hungry Tiger and Tik-Tok, who had been wound up three times during the night and was running a little fast. The head cook was there too, in her nightgown, with her frying pan, which she had brought without quite knowing why. And beside her stood Bessamy, the second cook, with blue ribbons in her apron, holding a small basket covered with a cloth.

The glow grew brighter. Green-gold. Pulsing. Rising.

The paving stones in the center of the square went soft as jelly. They bulged upward, slowly, slowly.

And then, very gently, without any HIC at all, the Great Square of the Emerald City opened like a mouth taking a breath, and up out of it, into the cool morning air, rose a star.

It was not very big. It was about the size of a pumpkin. It was round and glowing and greenish-gold, and so bright it made everyone squint, and it hung in the air above the square, turning slowly, as if looking around in wonder at everything it could see.

And hanging off the bottom of it, with both arms wrapped around it and his legs dangling, covered head to toe in glittering black dust, was Tam Pottlebury.

"One step," he croaked. "And rest."

The star rested. It hung in the air, three feet above the paving stones. Tam let go, and dropped, and landed on his bottom on the green marble, and lay there.

The square was silent.

Then the head cook dropped her frying pan with a clang, and ran across the square in her nightgown, and scooped Tam up in her arms and squeezed him so hard that he squeaked.

"You stupid boy," she sobbed. "You stupid, stupid boy. Where have you been? Don't you ever—don't you ever—"

"Sorry," said Tam into her shoulder. "I was carrying something."

Ozma came across the square. She did not run, because princesses do not run, but she walked very fast. She knelt down on the green marble beside the head cook and Tam, and put her hand on his black-dusted hair.

"Tam Pottlebury," she said. "You have saved Oz."

"No," said Tam. "The star did. I just counted."

"Sometimes," said Ozma, "counting is the most important thing anybody can do."

Above them, the star had begun to drift upward. Slowly. Gently. Rising toward the pale morning sky, where the last of the night's stars were fading.

"Wait!" Tam struggled out of the head cook's arms. "Wait! Star! You can't go yet!"

The star stopped.

"I promised you," said Tam. He turned. "Bessamy. Did you bring—"

Bessamy, the second cook, came forward with her basket and pulled off the cloth. Inside was a heap of bread. Not good bread. The burnt bits. The ends of the loaves, the crusts gone black and crunchy at the edges, the bits nobody else in the palace wanted.

"You told me," said Bessamy, a little shyly, "when you were—when you were gone. The Wizard's picture showed us. We could see you talking. And the Wizard can read lips. He said you'd promised it the burnt bits. So I saved them."

Tam took the biggest, blackest crust out of the basket and held it up as high as he could reach.

The star drifted back down, very slowly, until it was hanging just above his hand. It glowed brighter for a moment, green and gold.

And then the crust was gone. Not eaten, exactly. More like it had been folded up into the light.

"Oh," said the star, in its high, faraway, ringing voice. "Oh. That's wonderful. Is that what bread is?"

"That's what the burnt bits are," said Tam. "They're the best part."

The star turned once in the air, slowly, as if thinking about it. Then it said:

"I'll remember you, Tam. I'll remember the eggs. One step, and rest." It began to rise again. "If I ever fall again, I'll come down slowly."

And it rose, slowly, up and up over the green towers of the Emerald City, into the pink morning sky, until it was only a pinprick of green-gold light, and then it found its place among the last fading stars, and it stopped there, and stayed.


PART NINE

Everything that had been hiccupped went back where it belonged. Not all at once, because that would have been another hiccup. A little at a time, over the next few days. The blue farmhouse floated gently back to the Munchkin Country. The pond full of red fish drained down off the Scarecrow's roof and trickled back to the Quadling Country, singing as it went. The throne room wall swapped places with the barn wall again, and the Gillikin pigs, who had grown rather fond of the court, were sorry to see it go.

The officer's hat that had touched the Deadly Desert did not come back. But Ozma had a new one made for him, with a green feather, and he said it was nicer anyway.

And Tam Pottlebury was given a medal, and a room of his own with a window that looked out on the sky, and the official title of Royal Star-Counter of Oz, which nobody had ever been before, and which mostly meant that he was allowed to sit up as late as he liked, every night, watching the sky over the Emerald City for falling stars.

He was still a kitchen-boy, too, because he wanted to be. Every morning he carried the eggs up four flights of marble stairs, one step at a time, and rested. He never dropped one again.

And every evening, before he went up to his room with its window full of sky, he stopped by the kitchen, where Bessamy had saved him the burnt bits of the bread. He ate most of them. But he always kept the biggest, blackest crust, and carried it upstairs, and set it on his windowsill.

In the morning, it was always gone.

And sometimes, if he looked very carefully at the sky just before dawn, when the other stars were fading, Tam thought he could see one small greenish-gold one, a little to the left of the Emerald City's highest tower, that seemed to glow just a little brighter than the rest.

Just for a moment.

And then rest.
