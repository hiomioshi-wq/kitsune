PART ONE

In the glasshouse behind the Wizard's workshop in the Emerald City, among the orchids and the lemon trees and the pots of herbs that whistled when the wind was in the east, there grew a very small plant that nobody had ever paid much attention to.

It came from a land far beyond the Deadly Desert, a land of red earth and grey-green bush, at the bottom of the world. The Wizard had carried it back years ago in his coat pocket, in a tin that had once held peppermints, because he had thought it was the most peculiar thing he had ever seen growing, and the Wizard had a weakness for peculiar things. He had planted it in a clay pot of wet moss on a shelf beside the window, and labeled the pot in his careful, slanting hand:

CEPHALOTUS FOLLICULARIS.
THE ALBANY PITCHER PLANT.
DO NOT FEED IT YOUR FINGERS.

And then, being the Wizard, and having a great many other things to think about, he had more or less forgotten it.

The plant did not mind being forgotten. It did not, at that time, mind anything, because it did not, at that time, know that it was anything. It sat on its shelf in the warm green light of the glasshouse and did what Albany pitcher plants do, which was to grow, very slowly, a little crowd of tiny pitchers around its base: fat, hairy, dark-red cups no bigger than the end of a thumb, each one with a ribbed lid that stood open like a little door, and a rim of glossy teeth curving inward around the mouth, and inside, at the bottom, a pool of sweet sticky water.

Ants came to the pitchers because they smelled the sweetness. They walked along the ribs, and leaned over the glossy rim to drink, and slipped on the teeth, and fell in.

They did not climb out again.

That was how the plant ate. It was not cruel about it. It was not anything about it. It was a plant.

Then one afternoon in early spring, the Wizard was working at his long bench in the workshop next door, mixing a fresh batch of the Powder of Life.

The Powder of Life is one of the most powerful substances in Oz. It was first made long ago by a crooked old magician in the Gillikin Country, who spent six years stirring it in a kettle, and it has a very simple and very alarming effect: anything that is sprinkled with it, and has the right words said over it, comes alive. Over the years it had brought to life a man made of pumpkins, a sawhorse, a girl made of patchwork, and at least one very bad-tempered phonograph. The Wizard had, after a great deal of trouble, learned to make it himself, and Princess Ozma had allowed him to keep a small amount on hand, strictly for emergencies, in a stoppered blue bottle on the highest shelf of his workshop.

He was just reaching up to put the bottle away when a sneeze came over him.

It was not a small sneeze. The Wizard was a small man with a large nose, and the dust of six different powders was floating in the sunbeams around his bench, and the sneeze, when it came, was enormous. His arm jerked. The bottle flew out of his hand, sailed through the open door into the glasshouse, struck the edge of the shelf beside the window, and burst.

A little cloud of glittering dust rose in the green light, and drifted, and settled, very gently, on a clay pot of wet moss.

The Wizard, who was by then halfway under his bench with his eyes watering, said a word that is not fit to print.

As it happens, the word was "Weaugh."

And as it also happens, "Weaugh" is the first of the three magic words that you must say to bring something to life with the Powder of Life. The other two are "Teaugh" and "Peaugh," and the Wizard, in the course of the next minute, blowing his nose and getting up and hitting his head on the underside of the bench and getting up again, said both of them.

He did not notice. He went to fetch a broom.

On the shelf beside the window, in a clay pot of wet moss, something that had never before known that it was anything opened every one of its little red lids at once, and thought, for the very first time:

Oh.

And then, almost immediately:

I am hungry.


PART TWO

The first thing the Cephalotus learned about being alive was that it could move.

Not very fast. Not very far. But it found, after a great deal of effort, that it could pull its roots up out of the wet moss, one by one, like a person pulling their feet out of mud, and stand on them, swaying, on the rim of the clay pot. And then, after a great deal more effort, it found that it could walk.

It walked the way a very small crab walks: sideways, on a cluster of pale rootlets, with its little crowd of pitchers bobbing on top like a bunch of red grapes on a stalk. Each pitcher had its own lid, and the Cephalotus found that it could open and close them separately, and also that it could see out of them, sort of, a little, in a dim, round, wet sort of way, as though it were looking at the world from the bottom of six different wells at once.

The second thing it learned was that it could talk.

This it discovered by accident, when it walked off the edge of the shelf and fell into a pot of mint. It said "Oof" out of four pitchers at once, in four tiny, gurgling voices, slightly out of time with each other, like a small choir that had not rehearsed.

The third thing it learned was that it was still hungry.

There were no ants in the pot of mint. There were no ants anywhere it could see. The Cephalotus climbed out of the mint and set off across the floor of the glasshouse on its rootlets, opening and shutting its lids, tasting the air, and the further it went the hungrier it got, until by the time it reached the door into the Wizard's workshop it could think about nothing else at all.

That was where it met the hen.

She was a small, neat, yellow hen with bright black eyes and a very upright way of standing, and she was pecking at some spilled seed under the Wizard's bench. When she saw the Cephalotus come sidling through the door on its pale roots, she stopped pecking and looked at it, head on one side.

"Well," said the hen. "What in the world are you supposed to be?"

The Cephalotus thought about it. It had only been something for about twenty minutes, and it had not yet had time to wonder what.

"Hungry," it said, out of three pitchers.

"Everybody's hungry," said the hen. "That's not what you are, that's how you are. I'm Billina. I'm a hen. I came to Oz from Kansas in a chicken coop that fell off a ship, years and years ago, and I've lived here ever since, and I've seen a great many strange things, but I've never seen anything quite like you. Are you a vegetable? You look like a vegetable."

"I live in a pot," said the Cephalotus.

"Then you're a vegetable." Billina nodded firmly. "What do vegetables eat? Dirt?"

"Ants," said the Cephalotus.

There was a short pause.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Ants," said all six pitchers at once, a little louder, and they opened their lids wide, wide, so that the glossy teeth around their rims caught the light, and the sweet sticky smell came drifting out across the workshop floor.

Billina took one very large step backward.

"Well," she said. "That's not a very nice thing for a vegetable to eat."

"Why not?"

"Because—because vegetables are supposed to be eaten, not to eat. It's the whole point of a vegetable." The hen fluffed up her feathers. "You're a vegetable that eats animals. That's backward. That's upside down. That's—well, frankly, that's a bit monstrous."

The Cephalotus did not know the word. But it understood, from the way Billina had said it, and the way she was standing, very upright, with her wings held a little out from her sides as if she were ready to run, that it was not a good word.

It shut all six of its lids at once.

"Oh, now, don't take on," said Billina, softening a little. "I didn't mean it that hard. I'm sure you can't help what you are. Only you won't find any ants in here. The Wizard keeps a very clean workshop. And I'm not an ant, in case you were wondering."

"I wasn't," said the Cephalotus, which was true. Billina was far too big to fit in any of its pitchers. But it had been hungry long enough now that it was beginning to understand something about being alive that it had not understood while it was only growing.

It was beginning to understand that the hunger would not stop.

When it had been only a plant, the ants had come to it, and it had eaten, and that was that. Now it could walk, and talk, and think, and the hunger walked and talked and thought along with it, and it would have to go and find ants, every day, for the rest of its life, and every creature it met would look at its teeth and take one large step back.

"Is there anything else I could eat?" it asked. "Anything that isn't monstrous?"

Billina considered.

"I eat seeds," she said. "And worms, sometimes, though I'd rather you didn't mention that to anybody. Most people eat bread and cheese and that sort of thing. You could try those."

So the Cephalotus tried. It climbed onto the Wizard's bench, where the Wizard's lunch lay half-finished on a plate, and it dropped a crumb of bread into one pitcher and a crumb of cheese into another, and waited.

Nothing happened. The crumbs lay at the bottom of the sweet water and went soggy. The hunger stayed exactly where it was.

"That's no good," said the Cephalotus sadly. "I'm made for ants."

And at that moment the Wizard came back with his broom, and saw a small red pitcher plant sitting in the middle of his lunch with its lids open, and dropped the broom on his own foot.


PART THREE

"Oh dear," said the Wizard of Oz. "Oh dear, oh dear, oh dear."

He was a small, bald, kindly man with a waistcoat full of pockets and a face that had spent a great many years pretending to be grand and powerful before it gave up and settled for being friendly. He sat down heavily on his stool and looked at the Cephalotus, and the Cephalotus looked back at him out of six dim wet wells, and Billina stood on the floor between them and explained, in some detail, what had happened.

"The Powder of Life," said the Wizard, putting his head in his hands. "On a carnivorous plant. Of all the things in that glasshouse. I could have brought a lemon tree to life. I could have brought the whistling basil to life. I'd have quite liked a talking basil. But no."

"Is that bad?" asked the Cephalotus.

"It isn't bad, exactly," said the Wizard, looking up. "It's only—well. You're alive now. Properly alive, the way the Sawhorse is, and the Patchwork Girl, and Jack Pumpkinhead. You'll never go back to being just a plant. And you're the sort of creature that has to eat other living creatures to stay alive, which is a bit unusual in the Emerald City, where most of us eat soup." He sighed. "I'm very sorry. It was a mistake. I hope you'll forgive me."

The Cephalotus did not know what forgiving was either. But the Wizard had said sorry, and had not stepped backward, and had not said monstrous, and so it opened one lid, cautiously, and said, "It's all right."

"That's very generous of you," said the Wizard. "Now. The question is what we're going to do about lunch."

He found an old jam jar and took it out into the palace gardens, and came back half an hour later, rather muddy at the knees, with a dozen black ants and two small beetles in the bottom of it. He tipped them gently onto the bench in front of the Cephalotus, and then, without being asked, turned around and looked out of the window and whistled until it was over.

Billina did not turn around. She watched the whole thing with her head on one side, very seriously.

"Well," she said, when it was done. "That wasn't nearly as horrible as I thought it would be. It was quite tidy, actually."

"Thank you," said the Cephalotus. It felt warm all over, for the first time since it had woken up. Full. It closed its lids, one after another, and swayed a little on its roots.

"But you can't go collecting ants for it every day, Oscar," said Billina to the Wizard. "You've got far too much to do. And the gardeners won't like you digging up the anthills."

"No," the Wizard agreed. "No, they won't. Something will have to be arranged." He looked down at the small red plant sitting contentedly among his lunch things, and his face grew thoughtful. "Perhaps I'd better take it to see Ozma. She's very good at arranging things. And she ought to know, anyway, that there's a new citizen of Oz who came to life in my glasshouse this morning by accident." He rubbed his nose ruefully. "She's going to be very cross with me about the sneeze."


PART FOUR

Princess Ozma was not cross about the sneeze.

She was cross about something else entirely, and she had been cross about it for three days, and when the Wizard came into the throne room with a jam jar in one hand and a small red pitcher plant riding on his shoulder, she looked up from a great pile of letters with an expression that was much more tired than it was cross.

"Oh, Wizard," she said. "Thank goodness. I was just about to send for you. Something terrible is happening to the gardens of Oz, and nobody can tell me what it is."

She held up a letter. Then another. Then a whole handful.

"From the Munchkin Country. From the Winkies. From the Quadlings and the Gillikins. All saying the same thing." She read from one at random. "Dear Princess, something is eating our cabbages in the night. We cannot see it. We set traps and it is not caught. We sit up with lanterns and it does not come. But every morning there are fewer cabbages, and the ones that are left have holes in them as round as coins." She put the letter down. "It's not just cabbages. It's orchards. It's wheat fields. It's the rose gardens of the Tin Woodman's castle. The Scarecrow wrote this morning to say that his whole field of corn was eaten to the stalks overnight and he only noticed because he was standing in the middle of it at the time, and he didn't feel a thing."

"Good gracious," said the Wizard.

"I looked in my Magic Picture," said Ozma, "and it shows me the fields, and the holes in the leaves, but it doesn't show me what makes them. It's as though whatever it is doesn't want to be seen. And I asked Glinda to look in her Great Book of Records, and the Book says only: In the night, small things came over the Desert, and began to eat." She rubbed her eyes. "Small things. That's all it says. I don't know what to do. If it goes on, there will be no harvest anywhere in Oz this year."

The Cephalotus had been sitting very still on the Wizard's shoulder, listening, with all its lids half open.

Now it said, quietly, out of one pitcher: "What do they smell like?"

Ozma looked at it for the first time. Her eyes went wide.

"Wizard," she said slowly, "what is on your shoulder?"

The Wizard explained. He explained about the sneeze, and the Powder of Life, and the three magic words, and the ants in the jam jar. He apologized several times. Ozma listened to all of it with her chin in her hand, looking at the Cephalotus, and the Cephalotus looked back at her out of six dim wet wells and waited for her to take one large step back.

She didn't.

"Welcome to Oz," she said instead. "I'm sorry your arrival was such an accident. But you're very welcome all the same. What should we call you?"

The Cephalotus thought about this. It had not occurred to it that it might need a name.

"The Wizard's label says Cephalotus," it said.

"Then Cephalotus it shall be. You asked a question just now. What did you mean, what do they smell like?"

"The small things," said the Cephalotus. "The ones that are eating the gardens. Everything has a smell. Ants smell sweet and sharp, like vinegar and honey. Beetles smell like old wood. When I was only a plant, I couldn't see anything, not really. I just smelled, and waited, and things came. I knew what they were by how they smelled when they came." It opened all six lids, wide. "If you could get me a leaf with one of the holes in it, I think I could tell you what made it."


PART FIVE

The leaf came the next morning, in a silk envelope carried by a winged monkey from the Munchkin Country. It was a cabbage leaf, large and pale and green, and right in the middle of it there was a hole as round as a coin.

The Cephalotus climbed onto the leaf and walked around the edge of the hole on its rootlets, very slowly, with all six of its lids open, smelling.

Ozma watched. The Wizard watched. Billina watched from the arm of the throne, where she had settled herself as though she had every right to be there. And in the doorway, having heard that something strange had come to life in the Wizard's glasshouse, the Patchwork Girl was watching too, standing on one foot, which was the way she watched things she found particularly interesting.

"Well?" said Billina at last.

"It smells like dust," said the Cephalotus slowly. "Like grey dust. Like sand that's been very hot, a long time, and then gone cold. And under that it smells sweet, but wrong-sweet, like fruit that's started to go bad. And under that—" It shut its lids, and opened them again. "Under that it smells hungry. Very, very hungry. Hungrier than me."

"Sand," said the Wizard. "Hot sand gone cold. Ozma, that's the Desert."

"The Book did say they came over the Desert," said Ozma.

"But nothing lives in the Desert," said Billina. "Nothing. Anything that touches that sand turns to dust. Everybody knows that."

"Something lives there now," said the Cephalotus. "And it smells like moths."

The Patchwork Girl came cartwheeling across the throne room and landed with her button eyes about an inch from the pitcher plant.

"Moths!" she cried. "How do you know they're moths? Have you met a moth? Did it say hello?"

"Moths come to pitchers at night," said the Cephalotus, not at all bothered by being stared at by buttons. "Sometimes. When I was only a plant, they used to come down to drink, and they smelled dusty, like this. But much smaller. This smell is very big." It walked to the edge of the hole in the cabbage leaf and leaned over it, all its lids wide open. "These are the biggest moths I ever smelled. And there are a great many of them."

"But why can't anybody see them?" said Ozma. "Why can't the Magic Picture show them?"

The Cephalotus did not know. But the Wizard was frowning, and tapping his fingers on the arm of his chair, and after a moment he said slowly:

"Desert moths. Grey as the sand. Born in a place where everything that's seen gets eaten by the sand, so they've learned not to be seen. Their wings would be the exact color of shadow. And their dust—moth wings are covered in dust, you know, tiny scales—their dust would be the Desert's dust. Perhaps that's what blinds the Magic Picture. They're wrapped in the one thing in the world that even magic can't see through."

"Mistmoths," said Billina. "That's what my grandmother in Kansas used to call the grey ones that came to the porch light. Mistmoths."

"Mistmoths," Ozma repeated softly. "Coming over the Desert in the night, wrapped in its dust, and eating every garden in Oz." She turned to the Cephalotus. "Can you tell us anything else? Anything at all?"

The Cephalotus thought about the smell. The wrong-sweet, rotting-fruit smell, under the dust.

"They're dying," it said. "I think. That smell is what moths smell like when they've flown too far and there's nothing left in them. They're not eating the gardens because they're bad. They're eating the gardens because they're starving." It closed its lids. "I know what that's like now."


PART SIX

The plan, when they made it, was very simple. And it was the Cephalotus's idea.

"Moths come to light," it said. "And they come to sweetness. That's what my pitchers are for. The sweet smell and the open lid and the shine on the teeth, that's how I catch them. But I only catch little ones. If I were very, very big—"

"Absolutely not," said Billina. "You are not eating a Mistmoth. They'd eat you first."

"I don't want to eat them," said the Cephalotus. "I want to catch them. Somewhere they can't get out. And then we can see them, and talk to them, and find out why they're here."

The Wizard looked at the little red plant for a long moment. Then he stood up so suddenly that his stool fell over.

"The Powder of Life makes things alive," he said. "But I have another powder, much older, that makes things grow. Very large. Very fast. I have never had a good reason to use it." He looked at Ozma. "Your Majesty, with your permission, I believe I could make our friend the size of a house for one night. And then shrink it back down again in the morning. It would not hurt."

"Would you like that?" Ozma asked the Cephalotus. "You don't have to. Nobody would think less of you."

The Cephalotus thought about it.

It thought about being the size of a house, with pitchers as big as bathtubs, and teeth as long as swords, and a smell so sweet it could be smelled across the whole of the Munchkin Country. It thought about every creature in Oz who would look up and see it, standing in a field in the moonlight, and take one large step back.

Monstrous, Billina had said.

"I'll do it," said the Cephalotus.


PART SEVEN

They chose a cabbage field at the eastern edge of the Munchkin Country, a mile from the Deadly Desert, where the holes in the leaves had been worst of all.

They went at dusk, all of them: the Wizard with a small iron box of growing powder; Ozma in a plain grey traveling cloak; Billina on Ozma's shoulder; the Patchwork Girl, who would not be left behind under any circumstances; the Cephalotus riding in its clay pot in the bottom of a wheelbarrow, which the Patchwork Girl pushed, very fast, over every bump she could find; and, because word of it had gotten out, the Scarecrow and the Tin Woodman, who wanted very much to see what was eating Oz's corn.

The Munchkin farmers had been told to stay indoors and close their shutters. But Munchkins are curious people, and every shutter in the village had a small blue face peering out through the slats.

The Wizard set the clay pot down in the middle of the field. He opened his iron box.

"You're sure?" he said.

"I'm sure," said the Cephalotus.

He sprinkled the powder.

It happened very fast. The Cephalotus felt its roots go down through the bottom of the clay pot, which cracked and fell apart like an eggshell, and on down into the soft black earth, deeper and deeper. It felt its pitchers swell. It felt its lids stretch and rise, higher than the cabbages, higher than the fence, higher than the Wizard's head and the Tin Woodman's funnel hat and the roof of the nearest barn. It felt its teeth grow long and glossy and curved, every one of them shining in the last of the light, and it felt the sweet water rise inside each pitcher until it was deep enough to drown a horse in.

And it smelled. Oh, it smelled. The sweetness came pouring out of it like a song, rolling over the field and out across the dusk, east and east and east toward the edge of the Desert.

When it was over, the Cephalotus stood in the middle of the Munchkin cabbage field, as tall as a house, six great dark-red pitchers rising on six thick stalks, every lid wide open to the rising moon.

On the ground far below it, very small, its friends were staring up.

And then, one by one, they took a large step back.

All except Billina. Billina stayed exactly where she was, on Ozma's shoulder, with her head on one side.

"Well," she said, after a moment. "I've seen worse. You should have seen the Nome King on a bad day."

The Cephalotus would have laughed, if it had known how. Instead it opened one great lid a little wider, which was the closest thing it had.

Then, from the east, from the edge of the Desert, came a sound like wind in dry grass.

And the Mistmoths came.


PART EIGHT

There were hundreds of them.

They came low over the fields in a long grey cloud, so grey and so soft-edged that at first the watchers could not tell where the moths ended and the dusk began. They were enormous, each one as big as a dinner plate, with wide wings the color of shadow and long feathery feelers and bodies that looked, in the moonlight, as though they had been sculpted out of ash. They made no sound except that soft dry rustle, like a great many pages being turned at once, and the air around them shimmered with grey dust, so that looking at them made the eyes water and slide away.

They smelled the Cephalotus, and they came straight to it.

The first one landed on the rim of the nearest pitcher. It leaned over the glossy teeth toward the sweet water below, its feelers trembling. It leaned further. Its feet slipped on the shining rim—

—and the Cephalotus shut the lid.

Gently. Gently. It did not let the moth fall into the water. It held it there, in the dark inside the pitcher, between the teeth and the lid, the way you might hold a moth in your cupped hands to carry it out of a house.

The second one came. And the third. And the fourth. The Cephalotus opened and shut its lids, one after another, catching and holding, catching and holding, until all six pitchers were full of soft beating wings, and every other Mistmoth in the cloud was circling it, around and around, frantic, desperate, drawn to the sweetness and too frightened to land.

"Now!" called Ozma from the ground. "Now we can see them! Wizard, the lantern!"

The Wizard held up his lantern. And in its light, at last, the Mistmoths circling the great red pitcher plant could be seen clearly for the first time, every one.

They were starving. Anyone could see it. Their bodies were thin as paper, their wings ragged at the edges, their feelers drooping. Some of them could barely stay in the air. As the watchers stared, one of them faltered, and fell, and lay among the cabbages with its wings slowly opening and closing.

The Patchwork Girl ran to it before anyone could stop her and knelt down in the dirt.

"Oh, you poor thing," she said. "Oh, you poor, poor thing. You're only bones and dust."

The Mistmoth turned its great grey head toward her. And then, very faintly, in a voice like a sigh through a keyhole, it spoke.

"Please," it said. "Please. We did not want to come. There is nothing left. There is nothing left."


PART NINE

It took most of the night to hear the whole story, because the Mistmoths were very weak, and they could only speak a little at a time, and the Cephalotus had to keep letting the ones in its pitchers out and catching new ones, gently, so that each could have a turn to rest in the dark and the sweetness and say a little more.

This is what they said.

Far out in the Deadly Desert, further than any traveler had ever gone, there was a place where the sand was not quite so deadly. A hollow, where a little water collected under the dunes, and a strange grey flower grew. A desert flower, no bigger than a fist, that bloomed only in the dark, and gave off a sweet smell, and filled itself with a sweet grey nectar. The Mistmoths had lived on that flower, and nothing else, for longer than they could remember. They had been born among its roots and had drunk from its cups and had laid their eggs in the shadow of its petals, generation after generation, safe in the one patch of the Desert that would not turn them to dust.

And then, one year, the flower stopped blooming.

"We do not know why," said the Mistmoth that lay in the Patchwork Girl's lap. "Something changed in the sand. The water went away. The flowers closed, and dried, and did not open again. We waited. We waited one season, and then another. And then the children began to die."

"So you came looking for food," said Ozma softly.

"We came to the edge of the sand," said the Mistmoth, "and we smelled the gardens of Oz. So green. So many. We were so hungry." Its wings fluttered weakly. "We did not want to take them. We know they are not ours. But the cabbages and the corn and the roses—they are not our food. They fill our bodies but not our hunger. We eat and eat, and we are still starving. We are made for the grey flower. Nothing else is right."

The Cephalotus was quiet for a long time, holding a Mistmoth in the dark of its biggest pitcher.

Then it said, out of all six pitchers at once, in its small gurgling unrehearsed choir:

"I know what you are."

Every head in the field turned toward it.

"I'm made for ants," said the Cephalotus. "Only ants. I tried bread, and I tried cheese, and they just went soggy at the bottom of me. Nothing else is right. And when I came alive, a hen told me that a plant that eats animals is monstrous, and I thought she was right. I thought there was something wrong with me." It let the Mistmoth in its pitcher out, very gently, and caught it again on the rim, so that it could rest. "But there isn't anything wrong with me. I'm just made for one thing. So are you. That's not monstrous. That's just being something."

"I take it back," said Billina from Ozma's shoulder, quite loudly. "About monstrous. I want that on the record."

"But your flower's gone," said the Scarecrow to the Mistmoths. He had been thinking very hard all night with his excellent brains, and his painted forehead was wrinkled with it. "Unless—" He turned to the Cephalotus. "Unless. Your sweet water. You're a plant that makes sweetness to draw creatures in. And their flower was a plant that made sweetness too. A grey flower. In a dry place. That bloomed in the dark." He turned to the Wizard. "Wizard, what does a desert flower smell like, that lives where nothing lives, and fills itself with sweet water, and draws moths to it in the night?"

The Wizard stared at the great red pitcher plant towering over the moonlit field.

"It smells," he said slowly, "very much like an Albany pitcher plant."


PART TEN

The Cephalotus went into the Desert.

Not right away. It took three weeks of planning, and a great many arguments, mostly between Billina and the Tin Woodman about whether it was too dangerous, and a long night in the royal library with Glinda the Good, who came up from the south with her Great Book of Records to read everything that had ever been written about the hollow where the grey flowers grew.

What they learned was this: the grey flowers were cousins of the Cephalotus. Very distant cousins. Long ago, so long ago that the Book recorded it only as a single line, a seed from a land of red earth at the bottom of the world had blown across the Desert on a great wind and come down in the one damp hollow where it could live. Over thousands of years, in the dust and the dark, it had changed. It had grown grey. It had learned to bloom at night. It had learned to make a sweet grey nectar instead of a trap.

It had learned, in other words, to stop being something that ate, and become something that fed.

"But they're still pitcher plants," said Glinda, closing the Book. "Underneath. And pitcher plants need wet roots. When the water went away from the hollow, the grey flowers dried up. They're not dead, the Book says. Only asleep. Waiting for water. If someone could carry water to the hollow, and stand in it, and spread their roots through the sand, and hold the wetness there, the way pitcher plants do—"

"Someone who's already alive," said the Cephalotus. "Someone who knows how to wake up."

So the Wizard made it a pair of boots.

They were very small boots, six of them, one for each cluster of rootlets, and they were made from a rare kind of leather that Glinda had brought from her castle, the hide of a dragon that had lived long ago at the edge of the Desert and grown so used to the deadly sand that its skin could walk on it without harm. The Patchwork Girl sewed them, with enormous stitches in every color she could find. They did not fit very well. But when the Cephalotus put them on and stepped out onto the grey sand at the edge of the Desert, nothing happened at all.

It did not turn to dust.

"Well," said Billina, who had come to see it off, and who was trying very hard not to look as though she minded. "Well. You'll come back?"

"When the flowers are awake," said the Cephalotus. "And the Mistmoths have something to eat. I'll come back to the glasshouse and sit in my pot and let ants fall into me, and everyone will think I'm just a plant."

"I won't," said Billina fiercely. "I'll know." And she pecked it, very gently, on its smallest lid, which is the closest thing a hen has to a kiss.

Then the Mistmoths came, every one of them, thin and ragged and grey, and settled all around the small red pitcher plant on the sand, and lifted it, very carefully, between a hundred pairs of soft dusty feet. And they rose into the air in a great grey cloud, carrying a little red cup of sweetness and a jar of the Wizard's best water between them, and flew away east into the Deadly Desert, until they were only a smudge on the sky, and then nothing at all.


PART ELEVEN

The gardens of Oz grew back.

It took a season. The cabbage fields of the Munchkin Country came up green again in the spring, and the Scarecrow's corn grew higher than it had ever grown, and the rose gardens of the Tin Woodman's castle bloomed so thickly that the Tin Woodman had to oil himself twice a day from cutting them. No more holes appeared in the leaves. No more letters came to the Emerald City. The Magic Picture showed fields, and fields, and fields, all of them whole.

And in the glasshouse behind the Wizard's workshop, the shelf beside the window stayed empty.

The Wizard did not move the label. It stayed where it was, in his careful slanting hand: CEPHALOTUS FOLLICULARIS. THE ALBANY PITCHER PLANT. DO NOT FEED IT YOUR FINGERS. Every morning he looked at it, and sighed, and went back to his bench. Every evening Billina came and sat on the shelf beside it for a little while, saying nothing in particular, and then went home.

A year went by. Then most of another.

And then, one night at the very end of summer, when the glasshouse was dark and the lemon trees were asleep and the whistling basil had stopped whistling, there came a sound from the open window like wind in dry grass.

The Wizard woke up. Billina woke up. They both came running in their nightclothes, which in Billina's case was the same as her daytime clothes but with more indignation, and they stood in the glasshouse door and stared.

The window was full of moths.

Not grey, starving moths. These were fat and soft and silvery, with wings like the inside of a seashell, glowing faintly in the dark, and they were laughing, if moths can be said to laugh, in a sound like a hundred pages turning at once. They came through the window in a great shining cloud and circled the glasshouse once, twice, three times, and then they settled, very gently, on the empty shelf.

And when they lifted again, there was a clay pot of wet moss sitting there. And in it was a very small Albany pitcher plant, with six dark-red lids, every one of them shut tight.

And round about it, tucked in among the moss, were six tiny grey flowers, no bigger than the end of a thumb, glowing faintly in the dark, filled to the brim with sweetness.

"Well," said Billina, in a voice that was not quite steady. "Well. I suppose you think you're very clever."

One of the six red lids opened, just a crack.

"The hollow's wet again," said the Cephalotus, out of one small gurgling pitcher. "The flowers woke up. There are thousands of them now. The Mistmoths said I should bring some home. So the glasshouse would have something sweet for them, if they ever came to visit."

"Of course they'll come to visit," said the Wizard, wiping his eyes on the sleeve of his nightshirt. "Of course they will. And so will everybody. Ozma will want to throw you a parade. The Scarecrow will want to ask you a thousand questions. The Patchwork Girl will want to make you more boots."

"I don't need a parade," said the Cephalotus. Another lid opened. And another. "I'd quite like some ants, though. I've been eating moth-flower nectar for a year. It's very nice. But it isn't right." All six lids stood open now, the glossy teeth shining in the faint grey glow. "I'm made for ants."

The Wizard laughed, and went to find his jam jar.

And Billina settled down on the shelf beside the clay pot of wet moss, and the little grey flowers, and the small red plant that had gone into the Deadly Desert and come back, and she tucked her head under her wing, and she said, just before she went to sleep, so quietly that only the Cephalotus heard her:

"Not monstrous. Never was."

And the Cephalotus, which had been many things in its short life—a plant, and a mistake, and a house-sized trap in a moonlit field, and a little red cup of sweetness carried across the deadliest place in the world—shut its lids one by one, contentedly, in the warm green dark of the glasshouse, and was, for the rest of that night, simply and entirely itself.
