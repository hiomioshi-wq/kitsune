NINE UMBRELLAS

A Kitsune Original. Text released under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0.


PART ONE

The old badger's umbrella shop stood at the bottom of Rain Lane, where the drainpipes of four streets all emptied into one gutter, and for that reason it was the wettest corner in the town. This was good for business. It was also why the badger, whose name was Tamba, had never in forty years owned a dry pair of socks.

He sold ordinary umbrellas to ordinary people: black ones for funerals, red ones for weddings, clear plastic ones for schoolchildren who would lose them by Thursday. But on the top shelf, behind the counter, where nobody could reach without the ladder, he kept nine umbrellas that were not for sale.

They were oiled paper, bamboo-ribbed, each one painted with a single picture: a crane, a carp, a pine, a moon, a fox, a wave, a bridge, a lantern and a closed door. Tamba's grandfather had made them. The rule about them was very simple and had been passed down with the shop: if someone comes in out of the rain and asks for one by name, you lend it to them. You do not ask why. You do not charge. And you do not expect to see it come back the same day.


PART TWO

The first borrower Tamba remembered was a young woman in a soaked kimono who came in during a typhoon and said, "The bridge, please."

He climbed the ladder and brought it down. She opened it in the doorway, and through the gap between the paper and the frame he saw, for an instant, not Rain Lane but a long wooden bridge over a river he did not know, and someone waiting at the far end of it. Then she stepped out and was gone.

The bridge umbrella came back three weeks later, leaning against the shop door one morning, perfectly dry, with a single cherry petal stuck to the paper.

After that he started paying attention.

The pine went out with an old man who smelled of sawdust and came back with sap on its handle. The wave went out with a child who never said a word, and came back smelling of salt and with sand in its ribs. The lantern went out with a fox in a red scarf, a mountain courier, who returned it herself the following spring and stayed for a cup of tea and told him a long story about a lighthouse.

The closed door never went out at all. Tamba sometimes took it down and looked at the painting, a plain wooden door with an iron ring for a handle, and wondered who would one day ask for it, and whether he would want to let them.


PART THREE

In his seventy-first year, during the long rains of early summer, a girl came into the shop with water running off her hair and asked for the fox.

Tamba knew her. She was the daughter of the noodle shop on the corner, eleven years old, and a nuisance. She had once tried to buy every clear umbrella in the shop with the intention of building a greenhouse. She had no business knowing the names.

"Who told you about the umbrellas?" he said.

"Nobody. They're on the shelf. I can read." She pointed. "That one's a fox. I want to borrow it."

"Why?"

She looked at him with the deep impatience of eleven. "You're not supposed to ask why."

That was true. It was the rule. Tamba climbed the ladder, his knees complaining, and brought the fox umbrella down, and the girl opened it in the doorway, and he leaned over the counter to see what was on the other side.

It was Rain Lane. Just Rain Lane, wet and grey, with the four drainpipes gurgling.

"Nothing happened," the girl said, disappointed.

"Maybe it's broken," said Tamba, who did not believe that for a second.

"Or maybe," said the girl, "it only works if you're going somewhere," and she stepped out into the rain and walked away up the hill with the painted fox bobbing above her, and she did not disappear at all. She went home. He watched her go in through the side door of the noodle shop.


PART FOUR

The fox umbrella came back the next day, wet, with a note tied to the handle in a child's handwriting.

The note said: I went to the place where my mother is. It is not far. She says to tell you thank you, and that the soup is too salty again.

Tamba read it twice, standing in the doorway in his wet socks. The girl's mother had died the winter before. Everyone on Rain Lane had brought food. Tamba had brought an umbrella, because that was the only thing he knew how to make.

He went down the street to the noodle shop. The girl's father was alone behind the counter, stirring a pot, his eyes red.

"Your daughter," Tamba began.

"She's asleep," the father said. "First time in months she's slept through the afternoon. She said she went for a walk." He tasted the soup, and frowned, and added water. "Too salty," he said, to nobody. "Her mother always said."


PART FIVE

When Tamba died, very old, in a dry bed for once, the shop passed to the noodle girl, who was grown by then and who had, after all, built her greenhouse out of clear umbrellas on the roof of the noodle shop, where it was much admired.

She kept the nine umbrellas on the top shelf. She kept the rule. She did not ask why.

The closed door went out for the first time on the day of Tamba's funeral. Nobody saw who took it. It came back a week later, leaning against the shop door, perfectly dry, and on the paper, just beside the painted iron ring, someone had added in very small brushwork a pair of muddy socks hung out to dry.
