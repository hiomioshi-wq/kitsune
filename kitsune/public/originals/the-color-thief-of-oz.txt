PART ONE

In the Munchkin Country everything is blue, and nobody there has ever thought it strange. The fences are blue and the farmhouses are blue, the bonnets are blue and the cornflowers bluer still, and when a Munchkin baby first opens its eyes the cradle-quilt above it is the blue of a robin's egg, so that the world begins in that color and goes on from there.

Wren Tallowmere was the lamplighter's apprentice in the village of Hob's Hollow, which sat in a fold of the hills where three blue roads met. She was twelve years old, small even for a Munchkin, with hair the color of wet straw that refused every ribbon, and she owned a long brass pole with a hook at one end and a wick-cup at the other, which she had polished so often that her own face looked back at her out of it, bent and cheerful.

Every evening Wren walked the three roads and lit the lamps, and every morning she walked them again and put the lamps out, and in between she slept, and ate porridge with blueberries in it, and asked old Master Pennick, who had lit the lamps for fifty years, a great many questions he could not answer.

"Why are the lamps blue?" she asked once.

"Because the glass is blue," said Master Pennick.

"But why is the glass blue?"

"Because the sand it was made from was blue."

"But why was the sand—"

"Because this is the Munchkin Country," said Master Pennick, "and if you want a country where the sand is some other color, you are welcome to walk to one, and I will light the lamps alone."

So Wren stopped asking about the color, because she did not want to walk anywhere. She loved Hob's Hollow. She loved the way the lamps came up one by one along the dusk like a string of blue beads, and the way the moths came to them, and the way the farmers raised their hats to her as she passed, as though the person who brought the light were somebody worth raising a hat to.

That was why she was the first to notice, on an evening at the end of summer, that the third lamp on the Mill Road was not blue anymore.

It was grey.

Not broken, not dirty. Wren took it down from its hook and rubbed the glass with her sleeve and held it up against the sunset, and it was simply grey, the flat dull grey of a puddle on a cloudy day, as though someone had come in the night and drunk the blue right out of it.

She lit it anyway. The flame inside was grey too.

"Master Pennick," she said that night, setting the lamp on his kitchen table, "something has happened to the Mill Road."

The old man looked at the lamp for a long time. Then he took off his spectacles, which were blue, and polished them on his waistcoat, which was blue, and put them back on.

"I have seen this once before," he said slowly. "When I was a boy younger than you. A grey came into the hills, a little at a time, and then it went away again. My grandmother said it was the Sallow Man passing by." He frowned. "I always thought she made him up to frighten me into bed."

"Who is the Sallow Man?"

"Nobody," said Master Pennick. "A story. Go to sleep."

But in the morning, when Wren went out to put the lamps out, the fourth lamp on the Mill Road had gone grey as well, and so had the gate of the Widow Abernook's garden, and so had half of the Widow Abernook's hollyhocks, which drooped against the grey fence looking like pressed flowers in an old book.


PART TWO

By the end of the week a quarter of Hob's Hollow had faded.

It went like frost goes across a window, in feathery patches that joined up when nobody was watching. One morning the miller's door was blue and the next it was grey. The bluebirds that nested in the church eaves went grey and stopped singing and sat hunched on the gutter like little stones. The river still ran, but in the grey places it ran without any sparkle at all, as though it were only pretending to be water.

Worst of all, the people who stood too long in the grey places began to go quiet. The miller, who had been the loudest man in three villages, stood in his grey doorway and could not remember the words to his own favorite song. The Widow Abernook sat among her grey hollyhocks and said she was not sad, exactly, but that she could not quite recall what being glad had felt like, and would somebody please tell her if they found out.

The Mayor of Hob's Hollow called a meeting in the village hall, which was still blue, and everybody shouted for an hour, and at the end of the hour they decided to write a letter.

"To whom?" asked Wren, from the back.

The whole hall turned to look at her, as halls do when a child speaks at a meeting.

"To the Emerald City, of course," said the Mayor. "To Princess Ozma. She rules all of Oz, and she has a Magic Picture that shows her anything in the land she wishes to see. She will look at us and understand at once."

"How long will the letter take to get there?"

The Mayor counted on his fingers. "The post goes to the Yellow Brick Road on Tuesdays. Then along the road to the city gates, perhaps eight days. Then there is a queue, I am told, because a great many people write to the Princess. Perhaps three weeks altogether."

Wren thought about the grey creeping up the Mill Road one lamp a night. There were thirty-one lamps on the Mill Road. There were three roads.

"That's too long," she said.

"Then what," said the Mayor, a little sharply, because he had been rather pleased with the letter idea, "do you suggest?"

Wren had not meant to suggest anything. But she found she was standing up, holding her brass pole the way she held it when the wind was against her on the hill roads.

"I'll take it," she said. "I'll walk. I know the roads at night. I'll go faster than the post."

There was a silence in which every grown-up in the room considered whether to forbid it.

Then Master Pennick, who had said nothing all evening, stood up from his bench by the wall.

"Let her go," he said. "She's the best lamplighter I've ever taught, and lamplighters are the only people in this village who are used to walking toward the dark on purpose."

So it was decided. And the next morning, before the sun was fully up, Wren Tallowmere set out from Hob's Hollow with a satchel of bread and cheese and blueberries, the Mayor's letter in an oilskin pouch, her brass pole on her shoulder, and in her pocket one small thing she had taken without asking anybody: the first grey lamp-glass from the Mill Road, wrapped in a handkerchief.

She thought the Princess ought to see what the grey looked like up close.


PART THREE

The Yellow Brick Road begins in the Munchkin Country and runs all the way to the Emerald City, and it is the most famous road in Oz. Wren had seen the edge of it once, at a fair, and had thought it rather showy. Now, when she came down out of the hills and found it gleaming in the morning sun, she could have kissed every brick.

Because the grey had not touched it. Not yet.

She walked all that day and half the night, using her pole as a staff and lighting her own little lamp when the dusk came down. On the second day the farms on either side of the road began to change color. The Munchkin blue gave way, field by field, to green, faint at first like the green at the bottom of a pond and then brighter and brighter, until the fence posts were green and the scarecrows in the fields wore green coats, and Wren understood that she had come to the edge of the land around the Emerald City.

And on the second day, too, she began to see the grey.

It was not only in the Munchkin Country. It was here, in small places, as though it had been sprinkled out of a pepper pot by somebody walking along the road a few days ahead of her. A grey milestone. A grey well with a grey bucket. A grey cow, standing quite still in a green field, chewing nothing and looking at nothing.

At a crossroads where a signpost pointed four ways to the four countries of Oz, Wren found that all four arms of the signpost had gone grey, and the words on them had faded so badly she could barely read them. She stood looking at it for a long time, and found that she did not feel like going on. It seemed a very long way to the Emerald City. It seemed very likely that the Princess had more important things to do than read a letter from a village nobody had heard of. It seemed, in fact, that she might as well sit down on the grey grass by the grey signpost and have a rest, and then perhaps another rest after that—

"Excuse me," said a voice above her. "You appear to be stepping on my foot."

Wren jumped. She had not noticed that the signpost had a scarecrow tied to it.

At least, she thought at first that it was tied. Then it climbed down, rather loosely, the way a sack of oats might climb down a ladder if it had decided to, and stood in front of her on two soft blue-trousered legs, and bowed. It had a face painted on a stuffed sack, with one eye a little larger than the other, and a hat that had seen a great deal of weather.

"I'm very sorry," Wren said. "I thought you were part of the signpost."

"That happens to me more often than you'd think," said the Scarecrow. "It is one of the drawbacks of a career that began on a pole. But you looked like you were about to sit down in the grey, and I have been watching this crossroads all morning, and I have seen what happens to people who sit down in the grey. They don't get up again for a very long while. So I thought I'd better say something about my foot, even though, strictly speaking, I can't feel it."

Wren looked down at the grey grass, and shivered, and stepped back onto the yellow bricks. At once the long-way-to-go feeling went out of her like water out of a sieve.

"You're the Scarecrow," she said. "The one from the stories. The one who went to see the Wizard."

"I am the one who went to see the Wizard," said the Scarecrow, pleased. "I was given brains, which have proved enormously useful, chiefly for working out that most of the cleverness I needed was there already. These days I live in a corncob house in the Winkie Country, and I come out to the crossroads when I want to think. And lately there has been a great deal to think about." He gestured at the grey signpost with a floppy hand. "Do you know what this is?"

"Master Pennick called it the Sallow Man," Wren said. "But he thinks it's only a story."

The Scarecrow's painted eyebrows went up so far that one of them nearly came off.

"Does he," he said thoughtfully. "Does he indeed. Young lady, I think you had better come with me to the Emerald City at once, because I have been trying to remember where I heard that name, and you have just reminded me, and I don't much like the memory."


PART FOUR

The Emerald City, when they reached it on the evening of the third day, was so green that Wren had to squint.

The walls were green and studded with emeralds. The gate was green. The guard at the gate wore green and had green whiskers, and when he saw the Scarecrow he saluted so hard that his hat fell off. The streets inside were paved with green marble, the shop windows were green glass, and even the sky above the city seemed to have taken on a faint green tinge, as though it were reflecting everything underneath it.

But here, too, there was grey.

It lay in the gutters of the side streets like dirty snow. A fountain in one square had gone grey and stopped. A whole row of houses near the western wall had faded, and the people who lived in them sat on their grey doorsteps with their chins in their hands, staring at nothing, and did not look up when the Scarecrow passed.

"It started here a week ago," the Scarecrow said quietly. "A little at a time. Ozma has been watching it in her Magic Picture day and night, and it shows her the grey, but it will not show her who is making it. That has never happened before. The Picture shows everything."

The palace stood at the heart of the city, all spires and green glass, and they were brought at once into a high round room where a girl about Wren's age sat on a throne carved from a single emerald, looking very tired.

Princess Ozma of Oz had dark hair bound with a circlet of gold, and she wore a gown of white gauze, and she was the most beautiful person Wren had ever seen. But what Wren noticed most was her face, which was kind and frightened at the same time, the way a grown-up's face looks when they are trying not to let a child see they are worried.

Beside the throne stood a man made entirely of tin, so polished that the green light ran over him like water, holding an axe with a jeweled handle. And on the steps below the throne, sprawled in a heap, was a girl made of a patchwork quilt, with button eyes and hair of brown yarn, who sprang up the moment they came in and turned a cartwheel for no reason at all.

"Visitors!" cried the Patchwork Girl. "Real ones! Not grey ones! Hooray and hooray and three times more for luck!"

"Scraps," said the Tin Woodman gently. "Let them speak."

Wren took the Mayor's letter out of its oilskin pouch, and then, because it suddenly seemed a very small thing to have walked so far with, she took the grey lamp-glass out of her pocket as well, and unwrapped it from the handkerchief, and held it out to the Princess on the flat of her hand.

"I'm Wren Tallowmere, from Hob's Hollow in the Munchkin Country," she said. "I'm the lamplighter's apprentice. This was blue a week ago. Half our village is like this now, and the people in the grey places are forgetting how to be glad, and I walked here because the letter would have taken three weeks and I didn't think we had three weeks. Please. What is it?"

Ozma came down from her throne. She did not take the letter. She took the lamp-glass, and turned it in her fingers, and held it up to the green light of the great window.

"Sallow," she said softly. "The Scarecrow told you the name?"

"My teacher did. He said his grandmother told it to him."

"Then his grandmother was a wise woman," said Ozma, "because almost everybody else in Oz has forgotten. Come. All of you. There is something in the royal library I need you to see."


PART FIVE

The royal library of the Emerald City is the largest in Oz, and in the very back of it, behind the books of magic and the books of history and the books of recipes for things that do not exist anywhere else, there is a shelf of books that nobody reads because they are written in a language that nobody speaks anymore.

Ozma took down the oldest of them. It was bound in leather that had once been every color and was now no color at all. When she opened it, the pages were blank.

"This is the Book of the First Painting," she said. "It is older than the Wizard, older than the witches, older than the royal line of Oz. When I was very small, my father's old librarian read it to me, before the words faded. It tells how Oz was made."

She turned the blank pages slowly, as though she could still see what had been written there.

"In the beginning, Oz was a great valley with no color in it at all. Grey mountains, grey rivers, grey grass. And there came into the valley a painter. Nobody knows where from. He carried a satchel of pigments and a set of brushes made from the tails of comets, and he loved the valley so much that he decided to give it color, a little at a time.

"He painted the east blue, because he thought the people there would be gentle and steady and fond of quiet evenings. He painted the west yellow, because he thought the people there would be warm and hard-working and fond of metal and bright things. He painted the north purple, because purple is the color of mountains far away and of dreaming. He painted the south red, because red is the color of hearths, and laughter, and ripe things. And in the very center he painted green, because green is the color that every other color can live beside.

"When he was done, he was very tired, and very old, and he lay down in the hills to rest. And the people of the valley grew up in the colors he had made for them, and they loved the colors, and they built houses and fences and lamps to match them. But—" Ozma stopped.

"But they forgot him," said Wren.

Ozma looked at her. "Yes. They forgot him. They thought the colors had always been there. They thought they belonged to the land, the way the rivers do. Nobody remembered that somebody had made them, carefully, one brushstroke at a time, out of love. And a painter who is forgotten—" She closed the book. "A painter who is forgotten grows sallow, the book said. Pale and sour and thin. And one day he wakes up and remembers that the colors were his, and he comes to take them back."

"Seventy years ago, when Master Pennick was a boy," Wren said slowly. "The grey came and went away."

"That was the last time he woke," said Ozma. "I don't know why he went back to sleep. The book does not say, and the old librarian died before I was old enough to ask the right questions." She turned to the window. Below them, at the western edge of the city, another street had faded while they talked. "But this time he is not going back to sleep. The Magic Picture cannot show him to me, because he is the one who painted the Picture's colors in the first place. And he is taking them all."

The Tin Woodman shifted, with a soft creak.

"Then we shall have to find him without the Picture," he said. "I may have no heart, Your Majesty—"

"You have the best heart in Oz, Nick Chopper, and everybody knows it."

"—I may have, as I was saying, a heart that the Wizard put in rather than one I was born with," the Tin Woodman went on, a little pink at the edges where the light caught him, "but I know what it is to be made by somebody and then left to rust in a forest and forgotten. If I were this painter, I should be very angry indeed."

"And I," said the Scarecrow, "know what it is to be put up on a pole in a field and have everyone walk past without saying good morning. I should be angry too."

Scraps the Patchwork Girl spun around three times on her cotton heel.

"I was sewn together out of a quilt that a lady made over many years," she announced, "and when they brought me to life, the first thing I did was look at myself in the mirror and think I'm the most beautiful thing in the whole world, and I never once wondered who did all those tiny stitches. Oh. Oh dear. I should be angry with me." She sat down abruptly on the floor. "I don't like this story very much."

Wren looked down at the grey lamp-glass, still in Ozma's hand.

"Then we don't have to find him to fight him," she said. "We have to find him to say thank you."

The four of them looked at her.

"Out of the mouths of lamplighters," said the Scarecrow at last. "Your Majesty, I believe we have a plan."


PART SIX

They left the Emerald City at dawn: the Princess of Oz, the Scarecrow, the Tin Woodman, the Patchwork Girl, and Wren Tallowmere from Hob's Hollow, who had never before been farther from home than the fair on the Yellow Brick Road and was now walking beside a princess through a country losing its colors.

They did not know where the Sallow Man was. But Wren had an idea, and it was the kind of idea that comes from walking the same roads every night for years.

"The grey started in our village," she said, "on the Mill Road. And it's been spreading outward from there, toward the city. Like ripples in a pond. If you want to find the stone that made the ripples, you go to the middle."

"The book said he lay down in the hills to rest," said Ozma.

"Hob's Hollow is in the hills," said Wren. "It's in a fold where three roads meet. And Master Pennick says nobody remembers who built the three roads, they were just always there."

So they went back the way Wren had come, east, into the Munchkin Country.

It was a hard journey. The grey had spread farther while Wren was away. Whole farms had faded. Whole forests stood grey and silent, without birdsong. And in every grey place, the same heaviness came over them, the long-way-to-go feeling, the why-bother feeling, so that they had to keep to the yellow bricks where they could, and hold hands where they couldn't, and talk to one another the whole time to keep from sitting down.

The Scarecrow talked about philosophy, which helped a great deal, because nobody could feel heavy and confused at the same time.

The Tin Woodman told the story of how he had been an ordinary woodcutter once, and fallen in love, and how a wicked witch had enchanted his axe so that it cut away pieces of him one by one, and a tinsmith had replaced each piece with tin until at last there was nothing of the old man left but his name and his love, and then he had rusted in the forest for a year with nobody to hear him calling, until a little girl from Kansas came by with an oilcan.

"And the strange thing," he said, "is that I spent so long angry at the witch who cursed me that I quite forgot to be grateful to the tinsmith who saved me. I went back to thank him, years later. He had moved away. I never found him." His tin jaw creaked. "I think about that more than I think about the witch."

Scraps made up rhymes, most of which did not scan and some of which were rude, and turned cartwheels across the grey fields, and every time her bright patchwork tumbled through a grey place, a little color seemed to cling to the grass behind her for a moment before it faded again.

And Ozma walked quietly beside Wren, and asked about Hob's Hollow: about the lamps and the moths, and the miller who sang, and the Widow Abernook's hollyhocks, and the porridge with blueberries in it. She listened to the answers as though they were the most important thing in the land, which, Wren slowly understood, was exactly what made her a good princess.

On the fifth night they came over the last ridge and looked down into the fold where the three roads met.

Hob's Hollow was entirely grey.

Every house, every fence, every tree. Every lamp on all three roads hung dark and grey and cold. And in the very center of the village, where the three roads crossed in the little square in front of the village hall, something was sitting on the ground.

It looked like an old man, from far away. It was very tall even sitting down, and very thin, and it was the color of old candle wax, and around it, in a ring on the cobblestones, lay heaps and heaps of color: blue in great soft drifts like snow, yellow in piles like sand, red in glowing coals, purple in mist, and in the middle, close by its long hands, a small precious mound of green.

It was sorting them. Slowly. Carefully. The way a person sorts the belongings of someone who has died.


PART SEVEN

Wren went down alone.

The others wanted to come. The Tin Woodman held his axe and said that he would not let a child walk into danger, and Ozma said that as Princess of Oz the danger was hers to face, and Scraps said she was going whether anybody liked it or not and was already halfway down the slope before the Scarecrow caught her by one yarn pigtail.

But Wren said, "He doesn't know you. He doesn't know any of you. He made all of you, or the colors of you, and you never knew he existed. But I'm the lamplighter. I've been walking these three roads every night since I was seven years old. If he's been lying under them all this time—" She swallowed. "Then I've walked past him more than anybody else in Oz. Maybe he'll talk to me."

So she went down into the grey village with her brass pole on her shoulder, through the grey streets, past the grey houses where her neighbors sat silent in their doorways, and came to the square in front of the village hall, and stopped just outside the ring of colors.

The Sallow Man looked up.

His face was long and lined and the color of a page left too long in the sun. His eyes had no color in them at all. His hands were stained, every finger a different shade, where the paint of the whole world had once been worked into his skin, and as Wren watched, a fleck of blue fell from the tip of one finger onto the cobblestones and lay there, very small and very bright.

"Lamplighter," he said. His voice was dry as old brushes. "I know you. You walk above me every night. Every night. For years. Do you know how heavy a road is, when somebody walks it every night, and never once looks down?"

"I'm sorry," said Wren, because it was true, and because she could not think of anything else.

"Sorry." He picked up a handful of blue from the drift beside him and let it run through his fingers like sand. "Seventy years ago I woke, and I came up through the roads, and I began to take back what was mine. And a boy saw me. A small boy, with his grandmother's hand in his. And the boy said, Who are you? And I told him. And he said, Oh, then thank you for the blue, I like it best, and his grandmother made him say it again properly, with his hat off." The Sallow Man's colorless eyes closed. "It was enough. For seventy years, that was enough. I lay down and slept on the strength of one small boy saying thank you with his hat off. But it wore thin. Everything wears thin. And nobody else ever said it. Not once. Not in seventy years."

Master Pennick, Wren thought. The boy was Master Pennick. And he had grown up and grown old and forgotten, and remembered only that the Sallow Man was a story to frighten children into bed.

"I'm taking it all back," said the Sallow Man. "I'm going to sort it and pack it away and go somewhere else, somewhere new, and paint a valley for people who will remember. And this place can go back to what it was before I came. Grey. Grey mountains, grey rivers, grey grass. Let them see how they like it."

He reached out a long stained hand toward the small mound of green in the middle of the ring. The last of the Emerald City's color, Wren understood. When he picked that up, it would be over.

"Wait," Wren said. "Please. Wait. I didn't bring you a thank-you. I brought you this."

She reached into her pocket, and took out the handkerchief, and unwrapped it, and held out the first grey lamp-glass from the Mill Road.

The Sallow Man looked at it.

"That's empty," he said. "I took the blue out of it. It's nothing now."

"I know," said Wren. "I carried it all the way to the Emerald City and all the way back again, because I wanted the Princess to see what it looked like when the color was gone. And now I want you to see it too." She knelt down at the edge of the ring and set the grey glass on the cobblestones between them. "Because it's not nothing. It's a lamp. I lit it every night for five years. And every night when I lit it, people on the Mill Road could find their way home in the dark, and the moths came to it, and old Mister Fallowby used to stop under it to read his newspaper because his eyes weren't good anymore, and he always raised his hat to me. That lamp mattered. And it mattered because it was blue. Because somebody made it blue. Because you did."

The Sallow Man said nothing.

"I didn't know it was you," Wren went on, and her voice wobbled, but she kept going. "None of us knew. That's not good enough, I know that. But I know now. And I'll tell everybody. I'll tell them every single night, every lamp I light, I'll say: this blue was made by somebody, somebody who loved this valley before any of us were born. I'll make them all say thank you with their hats off. I promise. Only please don't take the green. Please don't take all of it away. Because if you do, there won't be anybody left who knows to remember you. They'll just think the world was always grey."


PART EIGHT

For a long time, nothing happened.

The grey wind moved through the grey village. The Sallow Man sat in his ring of heaped colors with his long hand still stretched toward the green, and Wren knelt on the cobblestones with the empty lamp-glass between them, and above on the ridge four figures stood very still against the grey sky and did not breathe, except that the Tin Woodman and the Scarecrow and the Patchwork Girl did not need to breathe anyway, and Ozma was holding her breath so hard her cheeks hurt.

Then the Sallow Man reached out, not to the green, but to the drift of blue at his side.

He took a single pinch of it between his stained finger and thumb. He leaned forward. He let it fall, very gently, into the grey lamp-glass from the Mill Road.

The glass went blue.

Not the faded blue of something old. It went the blue of the evening sky in the very last minute before the stars come out, deep and clear and glowing from the inside, and the blue ran up over the lip of the glass and out across the cobblestones like water spilled from a jug, and where it touched the edge of Wren's boot her boot went blue, and where it touched the hem of her coat the coat went blue, and she gasped, and found she was laughing.

"Again," she said. "Oh, please. Again."

And the Sallow Man laughed too. It was a dry, creaking laugh, a laugh that had not been used in a very long time, but it was a laugh, and as it came out of him a little color came into his cheeks, the faintest pink, like the inside of a shell.

"You'll keep your promise?" he said.

"Every night. Every lamp."

"Then call your friends down. I'm an old man, and there's a great deal of sorting to be done, and I'm afraid I've made rather a mess of the pantry."

So they came down from the ridge, the Princess and the Scarecrow and the Tin Woodman and the Patchwork Girl, and they knelt in the square in front of the village hall in Hob's Hollow, and the Sallow Man taught them how to put the colors back.

It was slow work. It took all night. You had to take the color in your fingers, the Sallow Man explained, and think about the thing you were giving it to, really think about it, about who used it and why it mattered and what it would feel like to see it again, and then let the color fall, and if you had thought about it hard enough, it would stay.

The Tin Woodman, it turned out, was wonderful at it, because he had spent so many years thinking carefully about what hearts were for. The Scarecrow was nearly as good, because he asked every house so many questions before he gave it back its blue that the houses practically begged for it. Scraps was terrible at it and splashed red and purple and yellow everywhere it did not belong, so that by morning the Widow Abernook's hollyhocks were striped like candy sticks and the miller's door was a sort of plaid, but nobody minded, because the Widow Abernook came out of her house laughing for the first time in two weeks, and the miller stood in his plaid doorway and sang the loudest song anybody in Hob's Hollow had ever heard.

And Ozma took the little mound of green in her two hands, very carefully, and asked the Sallow Man whether she might carry it back to the Emerald City herself.

"You may," he said. "But I should like to come with you. I have never seen the city since I painted it. I should like to see what you've built."

"You will be the most honored guest my city has ever had," said Ozma. "And I will have a statue made of you, in the very center of the square, and every child in Oz will learn your name in school."

The Sallow Man shook his head.

"No statue," he said. "Statues go grey too, after a while. Do what the lamplighter does. Remember me every evening, in small ways. That lasts longer."


PART NINE

When the sun came up over Hob's Hollow, it came up blue.

Not the sun itself, which was the ordinary gold it had always been, but the light that fell over the hills, the morning mist in the fold of the three roads, the dew on the grass and the smoke from the chimneys. Everything was blue again, and the villagers came out of their houses and stood in the street blinking at it, as though they were seeing it for the very first time, which, in a way, they were.

Master Pennick came out last. He was very old, and he had sat in his grey kitchen for a week not remembering how to be glad, and now he walked slowly into the square on two sticks, and stopped, and looked at the tall thin man with the stained fingers sitting on the steps of the village hall.

For a long moment they looked at each other.

Then Master Pennick took off his hat.

"Thank you for the blue," he said. "I still like it best."

And the Sallow Man, who was not quite so sallow anymore, stood up and bowed, very low, the way a painter bows at the end of an exhibition when someone has stood a long time in front of his favorite picture.

The Princess of Oz went home that afternoon with the green in her hands and the painter at her side, and with them went the Scarecrow and the Tin Woodman and the Patchwork Girl, who had decided she would like to be an apprentice painter and had already asked if she could have the left-over plaid. By the time they reached the Emerald City, every grey place along the Yellow Brick Road had its color back, because the Sallow Man stopped at each one and showed them how, and at every one of them, somebody came out and said thank you.

Some of them even took off their hats.

As for Wren Tallowmere, she stayed in Hob's Hollow, because she was the lamplighter's apprentice, and the lamps would not light themselves. But she kept her promise.

Every evening she walked the three roads with her brass pole on her shoulder, and every evening, as each blue lamp came up out of the dusk, she stopped for a moment underneath it and said, quietly, to the road beneath her feet:

"Thank you for the blue."

And sometimes, not often, if the night was very still and the moths were gathered thick around the glass, she thought she heard the road answer.

It said: You're welcome.

And it sounded glad.
