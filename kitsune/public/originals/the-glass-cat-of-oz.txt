PART ONE

In the Emerald City there lived a cat made entirely of glass, and she was, by her own account, the most beautiful creature in the Land of Oz.

Her name was Bungle. She had been made long ago by a magician named Dr. Pipt, out of clear glass blown thin as a soap bubble, with emerald eyes and a heart that was a small red ruby, and, most remarkable of all, a set of pink brains that could be seen working inside her glass head. When Bungle thought, the pink brains rolled and tumbled like marbles in a jar. When she thought very hard, they spun.

"You can see my thoughts," she liked to say to visitors, turning her head so the light came through it. "Nobody else in Oz can say that. Not the Scarecrow, whose brains are bran and needles. Not the Tin Woodman, who has a heart but no head for anything. Only me."

The visitors generally agreed that this was very remarkable, and Bungle generally agreed with the visitors.

She lived in the palace of Princess Ozma, where she had a velvet cushion by a sunny window and a little crystal dish that a footman filled every morning with nothing at all, because a glass cat does not eat. But she liked to have the dish. A cat ought to have a dish, she said. It was a matter of dignity.

And so it went for many years. Bungle sat on her cushion in the sun, and admired herself in the windowpane, and watched her own pink brains go round, and told anyone who passed how beautiful she was.

Until the morning she woke up with a crack.



PART TWO

It was a very small crack. It ran across her left shoulder like a single hair, thin and silver, and if you did not know to look for it, you would not have seen it at all.

Bungle knew to look for it.

She saw it the moment she woke up, because the first thing she did every morning was turn in the sunlight to see how beautiful she was, and this morning, when the light came through her shoulder, it did not come through clean. It bent. It broke into a tiny rainbow along the crack, like the edge of a broken mirror.

Bungle froze.

Her pink brains stopped rolling entirely.

Then they began to spin so fast that the maid who was dusting the windowsill dropped her duster and said, "Goodness, Bungle, whatever are you thinking about?"

"Nothing," said Bungle, very quickly, and turned her shoulder away. "I am thinking about nothing at all. Go away."

The maid went away, and Bungle sat very still on her cushion for the rest of the day, with her cracked shoulder pressed against the wall so no one could see.

She did not admire herself in the windowpane once.



PART THREE

You must understand that a glass cat is not like a flesh cat. A flesh cat, if she scratches herself on a thorn, will heal. A tin man, if he dents, can be hammered out. A scarecrow, if he tears, can be restuffed. Even a patchwork girl can be sewn up again with a needle and thread.

But glass does not heal. A crack in glass only does one thing.

It grows.

Bungle knew this. She had heard Dr. Pipt say it, long ago, when she was new and he was holding her up to the light to check for flaws. "Not a single crack," he had said, very pleased. "Good. Cracks in glass are like rumors. Once they start, they spread."

So on the second day, she did not move from her cushion, in case moving made it worse.

On the third day, she did not let anyone touch her.

On the fourth day, she heard a very faint ting, like a spoon tapping a teacup, and when she looked, the crack had grown a branch. It now ran from her shoulder halfway down her back, and it had split in two, like a river with a fork in it.

And Bungle, the most beautiful creature in the Land of Oz, put her glass head down on her velvet cushion, and for the first time in her very long life, she was afraid.



PART FOUR

It was the Patchwork Girl who found out.

Her name was Scraps, and she was made from a crazy quilt of every color there was, with buttons for eyes and yarn for hair, and she did not have one dignified bone in her body, because she did not have any bones at all. She had been brought to life by the same magician who made Bungle, and so the two of them were something like sisters, which is to say that they had disliked each other from the first minute they met.

Scraps came cartwheeling into the sunny room on the fifth morning singing a song she had made up about a turnip, and stopped, upside down, on her hands, and looked at Bungle.

"You've been sitting very still," said Scraps, upside down.

"I am being dignified," said Bungle.

"You've never been dignified a day in your life. You've been vain. That's different." Scraps flipped right side up and came closer. "Turn around."

"I will not."

"Then I'll walk around," said Scraps, and did, and saw the crack, which by now had grown three more branches and spread across Bungle's whole back like frost on a winter window.

Scraps did not laugh. Bungle had been certain she would laugh. Instead the Patchwork Girl sat down on the floor, cross-legged, and was quiet for a very long time, which for Scraps was nearly a miracle.

"Oh, Bungle," she said at last. "Oh, puss. How long?"

"Five days."

"And you didn't tell anyone?"

"I did not want anyone to see," said Bungle, and her voice went very thin. "I am the most beautiful creature in Oz. How can I be the most beautiful creature in Oz with a crack down my back? Everyone will look at me and see it. Everyone will say, there is Bungle, who is broken."

Scraps looked at her with her button eyes.

"Bungle," she said, "I am made of rags. I have a patch on my left knee that used to be a man's waistcoat and a patch on my right that used to be a baby's bib. My face is a pillowcase. My hair is yarn from a sweater somebody's grandmother unraveled because she didn't like the color. I am every broken, left-over, worn-out thing in the world sewn together, and I am perfectly marvelous."

"That is different," said Bungle. "You were always made of pieces. I was made whole."

Scraps stood up and put her floppy hands on her hips.

"Well," she said, "then it's time you learned how the rest of us do it. Come on. We're going to find somebody who can mend you."



PART FIVE

They went first to the Wizard.

The Wizard of Oz was a small bald man with a kind face and a great many tools, and he looked at Bungle's crack through three different magnifying glasses and a fourth one that was really a jam jar lid, and sighed.

"I am a very good humbug," said the Wizard, "and a passable magician, and I once made a balloon out of a circus tent. But I don't know a thing about glass. Glass is a stubborn material. It knows what it is, and it does not like to be told otherwise."

"Can't you glue it?" said Scraps.

"Glue would show," said the Wizard. "You would see it, a yellow line all down her back, and the light would not come through clean."

"Then no," said Bungle at once. "Absolutely not. I will not have a yellow line."

The Wizard looked at her kindly over the jam jar lid.

"Little cat," he said, "the crack is growing. It will not wait for you to make up your mind about what color the mending should be."

Bungle turned away and did not answer, and her pink brains went round and round in her head.

They went next to Glinda the Good, who lived in the south in a palace of rubies, and who was the wisest sorceress in Oz. Glinda looked at the crack in her magic mirror and her magic book and her magic crystal, and then she looked at Bungle with her own eyes, which were the wisest of all.

"I can't mend you," said Glinda.

Bungle made a small sound, like glass under a heel.

"I can't mend you," Glinda went on, gently, "because magic can make a thing new, but it can't make a thing unbroken. That's not the same. If I made you new, you would not be you. You would be a different cat who looked like you. You would not remember Dr. Pipt, or your cushion, or the way the light comes through your head in the mornings. You would not remember Scraps."

"I would not mind forgetting Scraps," said Bungle.

"Thanks a lot," said Scraps.

"But I can tell you who could help," said Glinda. "Far in the west of the Winkie Country, beyond the yellow fields, there is an old woman named Hesper who mends things. Not with magic. With her hands. She is the best mender in Oz. But I warn you, Bungle. She mends things so that you can see where they were broken. That's how she does it. She says that's the only honest way."

"Then I do not want her," said Bungle.

"Then the crack will keep growing," said Glinda, "and one morning you will turn in the sun to admire yourself, and you will fall to pieces."

Bungle said nothing.

Scraps said, "How far west?"



PART SIX

It is a long way across the Winkie Country, which is all yellow: yellow fields and yellow flowers and yellow-painted farmhouses and yellow-bellied birds singing in yellow trees. Scraps cartwheeled most of it. Bungle walked, very slowly, very carefully, and every time her paw hit a stone, she heard a tiny ting from inside her, and the crack grew.

By the third day, it had reached her chest.

By the fourth day, it had touched the edge of her ruby heart.

"It's going to break my heart," Bungle said, very quietly, on the fourth night. They were lying in a yellow haystack under the stars. "Isn't it? If it reaches my heart, my heart will crack in two."

"Probably," said Scraps. Scraps was always honest, even when it was rude. Especially then.

Bungle looked up at the stars. They came through her glass head and made little points of light in her pink brains, like fireflies in a jar.

"I have never done anything," she said. "Did you know that? In all these years. I have sat on a cushion and been beautiful. That is all I have ever done. Dr. Pipt made me to be admired, and so I let people admire me, and I never went anywhere, and I never helped anybody, and I never even had a friend. And now I am going to break, and the only thing anyone will say is: what a shame. She was so beautiful."

Scraps was quiet for a long time.

Then she said, "You've got one."

"One what?"

"A friend, you silly glass goose," said the Patchwork Girl, and she reached over and put her soft rag hand on Bungle's cracked back, very carefully, so it wouldn't ting. "You've been walking across the whole Winkie Country with one for four days. Your pink brains must be awfully slow if you haven't noticed."

And Bungle, who had never in all her life been at a loss for words, found that she did not have any.

Her pink brains turned over once, slowly, like a cat getting comfortable, and settled down.



PART SEVEN

Hesper the mender lived at the very end of the Winkie Country, where the yellow fields gave way to the grey sand of the Deadly Desert, in a little crooked house made of things other people had thrown away. The chimney was a stack of old teapots. The windows were bottle bottoms. The front door was made from the lid of a piano.

Hesper herself was very small and very old, with spectacles on a chain and hands covered in tiny white scars, and when she opened the piano-lid door and saw a cracked glass cat and a patchwork girl standing on her step, she did not seem surprised at all.

"Glinda sent you," she said.

"Yes," said Scraps.

"I don't do new," said Hesper. "I don't do invisible. I mend. You'll see where it broke, always, for the rest of your life. If that's not what you want, there's the door, and it's a long walk back."

Bungle looked up at her. The crack was at her heart now. She could feel it there, a tiny cold line against the ruby, waiting.

"Will it be ugly?" she asked.

Hesper considered this, the way a person considers a real question.

"No," she said. "But it won't be what you were. It'll be something else. Something that was broken and got put back. Some people find that ugly. I've never understood why."

She held out her scarred hands.

"Come in, cat. Let's have a look at you."



PART EIGHT

Hesper worked through the night.

She did not use magic. She used a little brush, and a little pot, and a fire that she kept very low, and something in the pot that glowed as it warmed, gold, like honey with the sun in it.

"In a land far away, beyond the desert, at the edge of the world," she told Bungle, while she worked, "there's an old way of mending broken bowls. You don't hide the crack. You fill it with gold. Gold so bright you can't miss it. Because the bowl broke, and was mended, and is still a bowl, and that's the most interesting thing about it. That's its story. Why would you hide a story?"

She painted the gold into the crack, very slowly, one branch at a time. Across Bungle's shoulder. Down her back. Over her chest. Up to the edge of her ruby heart, where the crack had just begun to bite into the red. She filled that too. A tiny line of gold, right across the ruby, like a vein.

It hurt a little. Not much. It felt warm, the way sunlight feels when it has been a long winter.

Scraps sat on a stool by the bottle-bottom window and watched, and for once in her life she did not sing.

By morning, it was done.



PART NINE

Hesper held up a mirror.

It was a big old cracked mirror, of course, mended with gold just like Bungle, because nothing in that house had ever been thrown away.

And Bungle looked.

She was still glass. Still clear, still thin as a soap bubble, still with her emerald eyes and her pink brains going slowly round. But now, all across her, there was gold. Branching lines of it, like rivers seen from very high up, like the roots of a tree, like lightning caught and held. Down her shoulder, across her back, over her chest, and right through her heart, which glowed red and gold together when the morning light came through the bottle-bottom window.

The light did not come through clean.

It came through better.

It broke on the gold lines and scattered, and threw little patterns of red and gold and green all over Hesper's walls, so that the whole crooked house lit up like the inside of a kaleidoscope.

Bungle stared.

Her pink brains spun once, very fast. Then they stopped, and she said, in a very small voice, a voice nobody in the Emerald City had ever heard her use:

"Oh."

"Well?" said Hesper.

"I am not the most beautiful creature in Oz," said Bungle.

Scraps opened her mouth to argue.

"I am something else," said Bungle. She turned in the light, and the patterns turned with her, all over the walls. "I am something that broke. And walked across the whole Winkie Country with a friend. And got put back." She looked at the gold line across her ruby heart. "I think that might be better than beautiful. I don't know yet. I've never been it before."

Hesper smiled and began to put away her brushes.

"That's the right answer," she said. "Most people take a lot longer."



PART TEN

They walked back across the Winkie Country, and this time Bungle did not walk carefully. She ran. She chased yellow butterflies through yellow fields. She climbed a yellow tree and fell out of it and landed with a loud ting, and checked herself, and found that the gold had held, and laughed so hard her pink brains rolled.

When they got back to the Emerald City, everyone came to look.

The maids. The footmen. The Wizard, with his jam jar lid. Princess Ozma herself, who knelt down in the green palace hall and looked at the gold rivers running through her glass cat and said, "Bungle. Oh, Bungle. You're more marvelous than ever."

And Bungle, who would once have said, "Yes, I know," said instead:

"Scraps carried me over a river. And a woman named Hesper stayed up all night. And I fell out of a tree. Would you like to hear about it?"

Everyone said that they would.

She still has her velvet cushion by the sunny window. She still has her crystal dish that the footman fills with nothing. She still turns in the light every morning.

But she doesn't turn to admire herself anymore.

She turns so the light will catch the gold, and break, and scatter across the walls, so that whoever happens to be in the room, a maid, a footman, a patchwork girl doing a handstand, gets to stand for a moment in a room full of little rainbows.

That's what she's for now, she says. That's what broken things are for.

And if you ever go to the Emerald City, and you see a glass cat in a sunny window, shot through with gold, ask her about the crack.

She'll tell you the whole story. She's very proud of it.

THE END
