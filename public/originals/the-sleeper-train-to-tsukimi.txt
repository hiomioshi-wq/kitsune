THE SLEEPER TRAIN TO TSUKIMI

A Kitsune Original. Text released under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0.


1. A Ticket You Can't Buy

The sleeper train to Tsukimi leaves from platform zero at eleven minutes past midnight, on the nights when the moon is exactly half full.

There is no platform zero on the station map. You find it by walking to the end of platform one, past the last bench, past the vending machine that only sells warm barley tea, and down three steps that are not there in the daytime.

Haru found it because she was crying and not looking where she was going.

She was a raccoon dog, fourteen years old, with a rucksack that was too heavy and a phone that had run out of battery an hour ago, which was the last thing in a long day of things going wrong. She had missed the last train home. She had argued with her mother over the phone before it died. She had said a thing she wanted very badly to take back.

And now she was standing on a platform that should not exist, beside a train the colour of night-time tea, with lamps in every window and steam curling around its wheels.

A conductor leaned out of the nearest door. He was a heron in a navy coat with brass buttons, and his cap had a tiny crescent moon embroidered on the band.

"Ticket?" he said.

"I don't have one," said Haru. "I don't even know where this goes."

"Tsukimi," said the heron, as if that explained everything. "And you don't buy a ticket. You pay for it."

"With what?"

He smiled, which on a heron is mostly in the eyes.

"With a story," he said. "A true one. You may tell it any time before we arrive."


2. The Dining Car

The inside of the train was all dark wood and green velvet and little brass lamps with paper shades. It smelled of toast and rain.

The heron showed Haru to a compartment with two bunks folded up against the wall and a window that showed nothing outside but dark, and, now and then, a lantern swinging past at a crossing that did not seem to be anywhere.

"Breakfast is served in the dining car," he said. "At whatever time it feels like breakfast. The other passengers will be glad of company. They always are."

When Haru went to the dining car, it did feel like breakfast, though her own watch said a quarter to one. There were four other passengers at the long table.

A tortoise in a cardigan, knitting something very long and blue.

A pair of sparrows who finished each other's sentences.

A tall, thin fox with silver at the tips of her ears and a courier's satchel on the seat beside her.

And a very small mouse, no bigger than a teacup, who sat on a folded napkin and did not say anything at all.

"New passenger," said the tortoise, without looking up from her knitting. "Sit down, dear. Have some toast. You look like a day that went wrong."

"It did," said Haru, and sat, and to her own surprise found herself eating three slices of toast with apricot jam.


3. The Rule of the Train

"Everyone pays," the fox explained. Her name was Kitsu, and she said she delivered letters, mostly in the mountains, but sometimes the post took her to strange places, and this was one of them. "Everyone tells one true story. When you've told it, the train knows where to let you off."

"And if you don't tell one?"

The sparrows looked at each other.

"Then you just—"

"—keep riding."

"Some people like that," said the tortoise placidly. "I've been aboard eleven years. I'm knitting a scarf long enough to reach the moon. I'll tell my story when it's finished."

Haru looked at the little mouse on the napkin. "What about him?"

"He hasn't said a word since he got on," said Kitsu. "Three nights ago."

The mouse looked at his paws.


4. The Sparrows' Story

The sparrows went first, that night, because they could never wait for anything.

They told the story of the spring they built their first nest in the gutter of a noodle shop, and the shop owner, a grumpy old badger, who climbed a ladder every morning pretending to fix the gutter and secretly left a single noodle on the edge for them. For three years. They never once thanked him, because they never once let on they knew.

"We should have—"

"—said thank you."

As they finished, the train slowed. Outside the window, for the first time, there was a station: a tiny platform with one lamp, and behind it, the warm yellow windows of a noodle shop.

The sparrows hopped down from their chairs, put on their small coats, and got off the train arguing happily about what to say first.


5. The Fox's Story

Kitsu told hers the next night.

It was about a letter with no name on it, which she had carried halfway across the mountains because she could not bear to leave it in the dead-letter box. It had turned out to be addressed to the sea.

"The sea?" said Haru.

"I'll tell you the whole thing another time," said Kitsu. "The point is, I nearly didn't take it. I nearly said: that's not my job. And I would have spent my whole life not knowing what the sea had to say back."

The train slowed. Outside, a platform appeared with snow on it and a signpost that read HOLLOW PINE, and Kitsu picked up her satchel.

At the door, she turned back.

"Whatever yours is," she said to Haru, "it doesn't have to be a big story. It only has to be true."


6. Haru Doesn't Tell

Haru did not tell her story the next night, or the one after.

She told herself it was because she didn't have one. Nothing interesting had ever happened to her. She went to school. She went to cram school. She argued with her mother about cram school.

But really it was because the only story she could think of was the one from the night she got on the train, and she did not want to say out loud what she had said on the phone.

So she helped the tortoise wind her wool. She ate a great deal of toast. She sat by the window and watched the lanterns swing past at crossings that did not seem to be anywhere.

And she noticed that the little mouse, every night, sat a little closer to her.


7. The Mouse

On the fifth night, the mouse spoke.

His voice was very small, and it shook.

"I can't tell mine," he said, "because if I tell it, it's true."

Haru looked down at him on his napkin. He was holding his tail in both paws, the way small children hold the edge of a blanket.

"Mine's like that too," she said.

They sat for a while. The train rocked. The tortoise's needles clicked.

"I'll tell mine," said Haru suddenly, "if you tell yours. We can go at the same time. Then neither of us has to be first."

The mouse thought about it for a long time. Then he nodded.


8. Two Stories

Haru's story was short.

She told about the last train, and the dead phone, and her mother's voice saying come home, it's late, I'm worried, and how she had been so tired and so embarrassed about missing the train that she had shouted, Stop treating me like a baby, I wish you'd just leave me alone.

And then the phone had died, and that was the last thing she had said.

"I didn't mean it," she said. "I don't wish that. I just wanted to be the one who was right for once."

The mouse's story was even shorter.

He had run away from home, he said, because his big sister had read his diary and laughed. And he had been so angry he had gone to the station and got on the first train he saw. And now he couldn't remember what had been in the diary that was so important.

"I just remember her face," he said, "when I ran out. She wasn't laughing any more."

The two of them sat in the green velvet dining car with the lamps swinging, and neither of them said anything for a while.

Then the train began, very gently, to slow.


9. Tsukimi

The platform was the same one. Platform zero, at the end of platform one, past the vending machine that only sold warm barley tea.

But it was daytime now, early, with the sky the pale colour of a peeled pear. And the steps that had not been there were already fading.

Waiting at the top of them, looking at every train that came in, were two people.

One was a tall raccoon dog woman with her coat buttoned wrong, as if she had put it on in a hurry in the dark, holding a phone she kept checking.

The other was a mouse, older than the first one, standing very straight and very still, with a small notebook held tight against her chest.

Haru stood in the train's doorway. Behind her, the heron conductor touched his cap.

"Your fare's paid," he said. "Both of you."

"Is this Tsukimi?" asked Haru. "I thought it would be somewhere else. Somewhere far."

"Tsukimi means moon-viewing," said the heron. "The point of moon-viewing was never the moon. It was who you were sitting next to when you looked at it."


10. The Scarf

Haru went up the steps. She doesn't remember what she said first, and her mother doesn't either. They agree it was probably both of them saying sorry at the same time, which is the best way.

The little mouse went up the steps behind her. His sister was holding out the diary. She didn't say anything. She didn't have to.

When Haru looked back, the steps were gone, and platform one ended at an ordinary wall, and the vending machine was selling ordinary cold drinks.

But on the last bench, where nobody had been sitting a moment before, there lay a very long blue knitted scarf, neatly folded, with a note pinned to it in shaky handwriting:

Finished at last. Thought someone might need it more than the moon does. Tell the next one: it only has to be true.


THE END
