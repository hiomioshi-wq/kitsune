THE CARNIVAL OF NINE LANTERNS

A Kitsune Original. Text released under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0.


PART ONE

There is a drawer at the bottom of the postmaster's desk in Hollow Pine that stays locked for a year at a time.

It holds the Carnival letters. Once a year, on the first full moon after the rice is in, the Lantern Carnival sets itself up in the wide meadow above the shrine at Seven Bends, and for that one night the meadow is the fullest place in the mountains. There are striped tents and a calliope, a great wheel that turns against the stars, stalls smelling of sugar and hot oil, a fortune-teller who is older than the road, and a ring of nine lanterns, each one taller than a house. Then dawn comes, the lanterns go out, and the meadow is a meadow again, with nothing in it but wind and one crooked picnic stone that nobody remembers putting there.

The rest of the year the meadow is empty, which is a problem for the post, because people insist on writing to it. Somebody's fortune came true and they want to say so. Somebody's courage was mislaid on the midway and came back by post a week later, wrapped in a leaf. Somebody's granddaughter rode the wheel to the top with her whole heart and came down holding somebody's hand. They bring their letters to the counter and address them the way you address anything you love, by name and not by place: To the Mender of Horses. To the Reader of the Wheel. Ink goes dark where a person is, and all year these letters' ink points uphill at grass and a crooked stone, which is to say at nobody. Ink does not usually bother with a meadow. But the meadow, one night a year, bothers with everybody.

So the letters go into the drawer, and the drawer waits. On the night of the full moon, the letters lean. The postmaster has never once caught them doing it. He swears every year that this time he will stay and watch, and every year he has customers, and every year he comes back at dusk and finds the whole bundle leaning uphill together, like a row of sleeping birds turning with the weather. He ties the string, and he hands the bundle to whoever the newest courier is. It is considered an honour. It is also considered, by every other courier, somebody else's turn.

This year the newest courier was Kitsu. She is a fox, young for the work, with a red scarf her grandmother knitted too long and a satchel older than both of them, whose faded flap says ALL LETTERS ARRIVE. She carries a paper lantern on a pole across her shoulders. The lantern does not light the road. It lights the addresses, which in the mountain towns is the more useful thing.

There were nine letters that year, which the postmaster said was a record. Eight of them were cream, and behaved like letters. The ninth was grey, and heavier than paper ought to be, and on the front, in careful brushwork that had faded without ever quite fading out, it said: To the Keeper of the Ninth Lantern.


PART TWO

Kitsu reached the meadow at dusk, and the meadow was not empty.

Eight of the nine lanterns were going up, tall as masts, walked up the hill on the shoulders of lantern-bearers who grumbled about their knees. The ninth lantern was the smallest of the ring, and nobody was raising it, because it was already lit. It burned grey, a soft moon-through-paper grey, the colour of a secret told once and never again. Kitsu had carried a lantern for three winters, and she had never seen light do that.

Between the lanterns, the carnival was arriving the way weather arrives, all at once and out of nothing. Striped tents rose out of their own shadows. The great wheel stood up against the last of the daylight and began, unhurriedly, to turn. Somewhere a calliope was tuning itself, over and over, with the patience of an instrument that knows it has all night.

She went to the ticket booth first, because there is always a ticket booth, and put the bundle on the counter, and held the top letter up to her lantern. The ink went dark. All of it, everywhere, the whole address, the way ink does at a party. Everyone in the mountains that any letter could possibly be for was here, inside one lantern-ring, within a mile of her elbow. For the first time in three winters, Kitsu was the least luminous thing on the road.

"Hm," she said.

"They're all like that tonight," said the ticket-taker, a weasel with a pencil behind each ear. "You want the ordinary way. Names and pointing." He read the fronts, one by one, and pointed, briefly and correctly, the way a weasel does everything.

The first letter took her to the ringmaster, a peacock called Toro, who was mid-flourish and folded it politely to accept mail. The letter was from the bathhouse at Hollow Pine, and it said, among other things, that the calliope's tune had lodged in eleven heads for a full year, that two of the bathers hummed it in their sleep, and that the bathhouse would like to know, respectfully, whether it came in a pill. Toro hummed the tune, confirmed it was theirs, and named it. "We call it The One That Stays," he said, and looked proud and sorry at once, which is a peacock thing.

The second letter was for the cranker of the big wheel, a sleepy bat named Koharu who worked nights because the day shift made her squint. It was from an old goat who could no longer climb anything, and whose wife could no longer walk far, and who wrote that the two of them had ridden the wheel to the top the year before and stayed for three turns, and that they were, in a manner of speaking, still up there. Koharu pinned it inside the crank-house door, where the wind could read it to her on slow nights.

The third went to the Mender of Horses, an old boar with a splint on one foreleg, who kept the carousel and all its painted legs. It was from a child, thanking her for the horse with the wobble, which she had mended on the spot with a hairpin. The boar tapped her splint with one hoof. "We hold each other up," she said, and went back to her horses.

The fourth was addressed to the wildcat at the frying stall, and asked, in a spidery and shameless hand, for the batter recipe. The wildcat read it and laughed all the way down her own counter. "The recipe is that you're hungry for it all year," she said. "That part can't be bottled. Everything else can." She sent a paper cone of fried things for the road.

The fifth was for the ticket-taker himself, from a boy who had saved all summer for a ticket and had been let in free, because the queue was long. The weasel read it twice, standing very still between his pencils. "I shortened the queue," he said at last. "That's all I did." He put it in his breast pocket, where it would be checked often.

The sixth was for the Teapot That Rattles, which had a stall of its own, one teapot, for looking at, minded by a young hare. The letter was from the family the teapot used to belong to, and reported that everyone was well, that the old house had been sold, and that they hoped it was happy here. The teapot rattled its lid once. "Good," said the hare.

The seventh was for the sweeper of the midway, a grey old monkey whose broom was taller than the tents. It was from a man whose coat button had come off in the crowd the year before and had arrived by ordinary post a week later, wrapped in a leaf. "Everything's here somewhere," the sweeper said, sweeping. "That's the whole trouble with sweeping."

That left two letters, and both of them said Wheel.


PART THREE

The fortune-teller's tent stood at the quiet end of the midway, striped red and cream, and warm inside as a pocket. The fortune-teller was a tortoise called Madame Kame, so old her shell had gone the colour of tea, with spectacles on a cord and a shawl knitted by somebody who loved her.

Her wheel stood at the back of the tent, tall as a door, and it was painted not with words but with small pictures: a bridge, a kettle, a raincloud wearing a hat, a bird, a boat, a tall door, a closed door, a moth.

The eighth letter was for Madame herself. It was from a farmer whose fortune, nine years ago, had been a tall door, and whose tall door had turned out to be the door of a new barn, and who wished, in future, to be told these things with more pace. Madame read the letter three times, more delighted each time. "Nine years," she said. "That's a fast one. Some of these are still walking."

Kitsu said that nine years seemed slow for a door.

"It walked nine years for one man," said Madame. "Fortunes arrive. It's the arriving that can't be hurried. Sit down, courier. The wheel owes the post."

She spun the wheel with one toe, which is how tortoises spin anything. It went around three times, sighed, and stopped on the moth.

Madame looked at it for a while over her spectacles. "You'll be given something to keep," she said. "Not for ever. That's a good one to get. Most people only get the kettle."

"I keep letters every night," said Kitsu.

"This one will be heavier."

Behind Madame's chair there was a curtain, and over the curtain a board, and on the board, in careful brushwork, it said: THE MOTH KEEPS. Underneath, smaller: NO CHARGE. IT NEVER LEAVES THE TENT.

The moth itself was asleep on the curtain, wings folded, grey as the ninth lantern's light, patterned like spilled ink and pressed flowers, like maps of things people had put down. The arrangement, Madame explained, was simple. A secret is a heavy thing to carry alone, and some people are not ready to burn theirs. So they come here, once a year, and whisper it to the moth, and the moth takes it, and keeps it, and never tells, and never advises, and as far as anyone has ever been able to tell, never dies. Madame did not know a single one of the secrets. "What good," she said, "is a keeper who tells?"


PART FOUR

The ninth letter took the longest, which is usual for grey envelopes.

Nobody claimed the title. Toro pointed at the lantern-ring with his whole tail. The weasel polished both pencils and said he had always assumed somebody lit them, the way one assumes the river is wet. The sweeper swept. Madame Kame said, "Not me, dear. I read the wheel. The lanterns are somebody else's work."

So Kitsu walked out to the edge of the ring, where the ninth lantern stood smallest and alone, and held the grey envelope up to its light, because it was the only light at the carnival that did not shout.

The ink woke.

Under the gold lanterns it had gone dark everywhere at once, like a crowd cheering. Under the grey light it narrowed, the way a crowd goes quiet, and pointed. She turned with it, slowly, with a compass's patience, and the dark ended at the curtain of the fortune-teller's tent. More exactly: at a moth, sitting on the envelope's own corner, weightless, grey as the light it kept, as if it had come out to meet the post halfway.

"Your letter," Kitsu told it, "appears to be carrying its recipient."

The moth did not argue. Moths never do. Couriers do not read letters, and the moth, she was fairly sure, could not. But the lantern could. She held the envelope steady in the grey light, and the ink left the page. It came up off the paper like a breath you can see on a cold night, and folded itself away into the moth's wings, and was kept. The wax seal stayed whole. The envelope, quite suddenly, was the emptiest thing Kitsu had ever held.

The moth rose off the corner the way a person gets up from a chair they have been sitting in for a very long time. For a moment there was one patch on its wings where the pattern looked lighter, as if something old had faded, but she could not have said what it had been a picture of, and did not try.

"Oh, good," said Madame Kame from the tent door. She did not ask what it had been. That was the whole point of the moth. But she listened to the wingbeat all the way up, and said, to nobody in particular, "That was a heavy one."

For the rest of the night, the ninth lantern burned with one thread of gold in its grey, like a lamp that had been paid.


PART FIVE

At dawn the eight gold lanterns went out, one by one, and as each one went, a piece of the carnival went with it, folded back into the dark the way a letter goes into an envelope. The tents went first, then the wheel, then the midway, then the smell of sugar, which stayed in the grass until noon anyway, and argued with anyone who said nothing had been there. The ninth lantern went out last, by itself, politely, the way a guest does who knows the hosts have to work in the morning.

Before the folding, Madame Kame stopped Kitsu at the tent door and paid the post: a strip of paper, folded small, with a picture on the inside. "For the ninth letter," she said. "Couriers get paid. Don't open it till you need it."

"What if I never need it?"

"Then it's yours," said Madame, and the tent folded with her inside it, shawl and spectacles and all, still looking pleased about the farmer.

Kitsu ate her rice ball at the shrine at Seven Bends on the way down, and looked at the meadow, which was a meadow. At Hollow Pine she gave the postmaster back his string, and the one empty envelope. He weighed it in one hand. "Delivered?"

"Completely," said Kitsu.

He did not ask what had been in it. That was good postmanship. He put the empty envelope in the drawer, for the record, and locked it, and by the following spring there was one new letter in the drawer, and by midsummer there were three, all leaning uphill.

The fortune is still at the bottom of Kitsu's satchel, under the flap that says ALL LETTERS ARRIVE, folded small, unopened. She is a courier. She knows better than anyone what happens to things that arrive. Some things, she has decided, are better kept. The moth would approve. It keeps, too.

In the mountain towns, letters travel at night. Once a year, a bundle of them travels to a place that is only there one night a year, and every one of them arrives. The satchel says so, and the satchel has never once been caught lying.
