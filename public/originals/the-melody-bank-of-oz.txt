PART ONE

In the Emerald City there is a bank, and if you have never heard of it, that is because the bank does not advertise. It has no marble lions and no brass rail, only a green door with a bell that rings the first four notes of a waltz, and above the door, in letters the exact green of a leaf held up to the sun, the words: THE MELODY BANK. ESTABLISHED BEFORE YOU WERE BORN, PROBABLY.

Inside, the counters are of polished emerald, and behind the counters sit the tellers, and behind the tellers, in drawers that go back farther than the building does, are the melodies.

A melody, kept in a drawer, looks like a small coiled ribbon of silver thread with a knot in it, and if you are very quiet you can hear the thread humming to itself, the way a cat hums when it is nearly, but not quite, asleep.

The bank takes melodies the way other banks take coin. You bring in a song you know by heart — a lullaby, a market tune, the song your uncle whistles when the cart axle sticks — and the bank winds it onto a spool and puts it in a drawer, and every year, on the anniversary of the day you brought it, the song grows a little harmony, the way coin grows interest, only nicer, because coin cannot hum.

Nobody in the Emerald City thinks any of this is strange. It was going on long before the Wizard came, and it went on after, and it will go on after that, because melodies are the one thing in Oz that everybody has and nobody can spend entirely.

PART TWO

On a Thursday in the spring, a Munchkin girl named Peg Ninebuttons came through the green door, and the bell rang its four notes, and a teller looked up.

"I want to make a withdrawal," said Peg.

The teller was a person of no particular age who wore spectacles on a ribbon, and she had the settled look of someone who has counted things all her life and found the total agreeable.

"Name of the depositor?" said the teller.

"Ninebuttons," said Peg. "Anna Ninebuttons. She's my grandmother. She put in a lullaby fifty years ago, before she moved to the Munchkin country. She's coming to the end of her voice now, and she wants it back, so she can teach it to me before the voice finishes."

The teller wrote the name in a ledger, in ink the color of a held breath, and went away among the drawers, and was gone so long that Peg sat down on the bench by the door and counted the tiles in the floor, which were emerald, and then counted them again to see if they had moved.

The teller came back with nothing.

"I'm sorry," she said. "The lullaby isn't where it should be. It was moved during the reorganization."

"What reorganization?"

"The vault reorganization," said the teller, as if everybody knows about it, and in the Emerald City, nearly everybody does.

PART THREE

The reorganization had been undertaken by a clockwork clerk named Hengist, a cousin of Tik-Tok's, built by the same school of artificers but on a smaller allowance. Hengist was thorough in the way that only a machine can be thorough, which is to say he was more thorough than was ever wanted, and nobody had ever dared to be glad of it until now.

Hengist had found the vault drawers in a state that offended him. Songs were filed by feeling, more or less: all the lullabies on one shelf, all the marches on another, the waltzes by the window where the light was good for them. It had worked for years, in the way that a kitchen works when only one cook knows where anything is.

Hengist had re-filed everything alphabetically.

Lullabies now lived in the L drawers, which held forty thousand songs beginning with L, from "Lament of the Left Boot" to "Lying Awake on a Lumpy Couch," and somewhere in that long silver city of drawers was Anna Ninebuttons' lullaby, and Hengist, who wound himself up each morning with a key kept under the counter, was quite sure he could find it, if he had perhaps a month.

"She doesn't have a month," said Peg. "She has about two weeks, and she'd like to have it sooner."

"Then," said the teller, "you had better speak to Hengist yourself. But not until morning. The vault keeps hours."

Peg did not have the money for an inn, so the teller, who had a grandmother of her own once, and remembered which drawer that was, let her sleep in the waiting room, on the long bench by the green door.

She did not sleep much. The bank at night is not silent, and nobody had warned her, and she lay very still for a long time with her cloak over her head before she understood what she was hearing, and then she took the cloak off her head and lay listening the rest of the night with her eyes wide open.

The vault does not hum. The vault sings, in the way of a great city heard from a hill at night: not one thing, but ten thousand small things, each minding its own business, and all of them together. Somewhere in the south drawers a waltz turned over in its sleep. Two floors of marches slept deeply and dourly and did not dream. A shelf of lullabies breathed, all of them at once, in the particular breathing of mothers who have done it for years. And once, near three in the morning, there came from a high drawer a small ripple of laughter, beginning and ending by itself, and Peg decided not to wonder about it, and mostly managed.

In the morning the teller brought her tea, and said, "You heard them," and Peg said yes, and the teller nodded, the way one veteran nods to another across a field.

"They're louder at night," she said, "because the bank is closed, and the songs know it, and every song in this vault knows exactly one thing better than the people who deposited it. Do you know what that is?"

Peg said she did not.

"It knows who it belongs to," said the teller. "Whatever else happens to it. A coin forgets its owner the moment it's spent. A song never does. That's the difference between our vault and the other banks, and it is why our vault is the only one in the world worth guarding." She unlocked the green door to the vault stairs with a key the shape of a tuning fork. "Hengist is on the third landing. Mind the choir."

PART FOUR

"The choir?" said Peg, on the stairs, and then she came to the third landing, and stopped asking.

The waltzes were kept on the third landing, and the reorganization had been hardest on the waltzes. Waltzes are the most social of melodies; they had lived, before, in one long drawer together, ankle to ankle, and they had been the pride of the bank, and on the last evening of every year the tellers opened the drawer an inch and let them out, and they danced in the vault all night among the files, and it was said that no teller who had heard it ever resigned.

Hengist had filed them by name.

There were eleven thousand of them, and they now lived in eleven thousand separate drawers in eleven alphabets, and they could hear each other through the wood, and every drawer on the landing hummed at once, all day and all night, each waltz trying to keep time with the waltz in the next drawer and getting it wrong by exactly one letter of the alphabet. The sound they made was almost a waltz. It was like a waltz remembered by someone who has been told about waltzes.

"Most of them are called The something," said Hengist, appearing at her elbow with a sound like a music box acknowledging a debt. "The Vienna, The Blue, The Last, The First, The One My Mother Sang. It is a filing challenge. But an honest one."

"Put them back together," said Peg.

"I beg your pardon," said Hengist.

"Put the waltzes in one drawer again. When you're done finding my grandmother's lullaby. Before the year ends. Because it's nearly the end of the year, and they'll want to dance, and you can't dance in alphabetical order, it's been tried."

Hengist stood very still for four whole seconds, which for Hengist was an interior earthquake.

"I was told," he said at last, "that my reorganization was an improvement."

"It's very neat," said Peg kindly, because she was Munchkin, and Munchkins are kind the way other people are tall. "But songs aren't filed things. They're company. You wouldn't file your cousins in alphabetical order and call it an improvement on Christmas."

And somewhere on that landing — she could never afterward say from which drawer — a waltz hummed one bar, softly, twice, the way a room agrees with someone.

PART FIVE

Hengist received her in the vault, which was as big as a church and smelled faintly of cedar and other people's childhoods.

He was small and copper-colored and he bowed with a sound like a music box acknowledging a debt.

"Ninebuttons, Anna," he said. "Lullaby, one, deposited fifty years ago, interest payable in harmony. I have the card." He produced a card from a slot in his chest. "The card says H."

Peg said, "You said the vault was alphabetical."

"It is alphabetical," said Hengist, "and the lullaby is filed under H, for Hush, which is the correct filing, since a lullaby is not for singing so much as for hushing, and the h-word songs are fewer than the l-word songs by a factor of nine, which improves the search."

There was a silence, of the kind that gathers in banks.

"You filed a lullaby under Hush," said Peg, "because of what it's for."

"I file everything by what it's for," said Hengist, with dignity. "It is the only filing that survives the ages. A song's name changes from county to county. Its feeling changes from year to year. But what it is for, that is stitched into it, and cannot be mislaid even by me."

And he went into the H drawers, and was gone eleven minutes exactly, and came back with a small coiled ribbon of silver thread, warm from the drawer, humming to itself the way a cat hums.

PART SIX

Peg held out both hands, and Hengist did not give it to her at once.

"Withdrawals are subject to audit," he said. "Interest accrued over fifty years must be counted in front of the depositor's kin, or the count is not legal. The bank is old-fashioned about this and I am older-fashioned."

So they counted it, there in the vault, Hengist unwinding the lullaby a little at a time. It was a simple tune, the sort a grandmother hums with a child's head against her collarbone, and in fifty years it had grown six harmonies, one for each decade and a little extra for luck.

The seventh harmony was not like the others.

"It's very quiet," said Peg, listening.

"That is the last one," said Hengist. "It grew in the last year. Songs know more than we file, sometimes. A lullaby that has been waiting fifty years for a grandmother's voice grows an ending into itself, so that when the voice finishes, the song can hold the rest."

Peg said nothing, because in Oz there is very little to say to a clockwork clerk who has just told you the truest thing you will hear all year.

PART SEVEN

She carried the lullaby home in a cedar box with holes in the lid, the way you carry a bee, and the box hummed the whole way, quietly, so as not to wake the horses.

Anna Ninebuttons sat up when she heard it, the way a fire sits up when you add a stick, and she learned the lullaby back from her own song, harmony by harmony, in an afternoon, and Peg learned it from her, and when the two of them sang it together that evening, with the grandmother's voice going and the granddaughter's arriving, the seventh harmony fit exactly in the middle, as if it had known all along whose turn it was.

Anna took the box back to the bank the following Thursday, and put the lullaby in again, with the new harmony wound in.

"It will earn interest," said the teller.

"It always did," said Anna.

And the bell above the green door rang its four notes as she went out, and if you stand there on a Thursday evening, when the light is green and low, you can hear the vault behind the wall, forty thousand songs in their drawers, each one humming to itself, each one a little richer than the year before.

Nobody in the Emerald City thinks any of this is strange. But Peg Ninebuttons, who now does the bank's Thursday errands for her grandmother, walks a little slower past the green door than she walks anywhere else, and if you ask her why, she says it is the interest, accumulating, and she is exactly right.
