THE RADIO THAT BROADCAST NEXT WEEK

A Kitsune Original. Text released under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0.


PART ONE

The ridge road between Hollow Pine and the sea crosses itself at a place called Kettle Gap, where the wind comes over the top like water over a step, and in the gap there is a hut, and in the hut there is a schedule, and the schedule is the whole reason for the hut.

Three broadcasts a night. At nine, the hut takes the weather from the coast station and sends it up the line to the mountain towns, because weather in these parts travels uphill faster than a letter and is wanted more. At eleven, the hut takes the mountain weather back down to the shore, where it is mostly ignored, the shore being of the firm opinion that weather is something that happens to the mountains. And at two in the morning, the hut turns itself quietly to a frequency nobody remembers, catches Moth Radio off the water tower on Hill Street, and passes it down the bay road toward Moonwake Bay, where the frequency is even less remembered, but the ferry people are fond of the whales.

The operator of the hut is a bat called Tilda, who is small even for a bat and was hired, after a genuinely exhaustive search of one applicant, on the grounds that she was awake anyway. The equipment is a receiver the size of a bread bin, a kerosene stove, an aerial of salvaged copper that sings in a wind, and a logbook with one ruled line to an hour and a column marked IRREGULARITIES, which has been left blank since the hut was built.

She kept the log faithfully. Weather at nine, weather at eleven, the hum and the whales at two. On Thursdays she wrote WHALES (WITH ALBERT), because Thursdays are when Moth Radio has its guest, the old badger from Ferry Road who explains how to fix things, slowly, in a voice like a warm blanket, and Tilda considered it the best radio in the world.

It was a Thursday, at fourteen minutes past two, when the receiver picked up next week.


PART TWO

She noticed the ident first, because the ident was hers. Her own recorded voice, saying her own words, "This is the ridge relay, Kettle Gap, signing the hour," except that it signed the wrong hour, and then, in the little formal pause where she always put the date, it put next Thursday's date, a full seven days out, as calmly as if a week were a kind of weather that had simply arrived early.

She wrote it in IRREGULARITIES. She underlined it twice, which is a radio operator's way of saying I HAVE THOUGHT ABOUT IT AND IT HAS NOT IMPROVED.

The broadcast under hers was ordinary radio, if you can call a thing ordinary that is dated wrong, and ordinary in the particular way of this coast: it was the week's weather, read a week early, day by day. Mild, mild, fog on the coast road, mild, and then, for the coming Thursday: wind rising through the afternoon, on the bar by four. Tilda checked the almanac, which said mild; and the sky, which said nothing, skies being under no obligation; and then she sat with her wings around the stove and listened to weather that had not happened, in her own relay's good strong signal, until it faded at four exactly, like a tide going out.

She wrote to the postal clerk at Hollow Pine about it, because everyone writes to the postal clerk at Hollow Pine about everything; it saves thinking twice. The reply came back three days later, in green ink: THERE IS NO FORM FOR THIS. ALSO, THE RIDGE RELAY HAS NO SUPERVISOR; THE LAST ONE DIED IN 1931. KINDLY STOP ADDRESSING NEXT WEEK THROUGH THE POST.


PART THREE

It came every Thursday after that, at fourteen minutes past two, seven days ahead, for ninety-odd minutes, and Tilda, who had nobody to supervise her and a whole column for irregularities, began to learn its shape.

The future is mostly small. That was the first thing she learned, and it disappointed her a little, heroically. One week's broadcast contained a slate. Her slate, the loose one over the door, which came down in the wind that Tuesday night and broke a kerosene tin. She heard about it seven days in advance, from next Thursday's small news, in her own ident's placid date, and she stood in the hut with her ears up for a long while, and then she went out and mended the slate.

The following Thursday, at fourteen minutes past two, the broadcast came through without the slate in it. The slate was not broken in next week's radio. The slate had never, now, come down.

Which is how she learned the second thing, the large thing, the thing she sat up all the next day sleeping on, since she is a bat and does her sitting up at night: the future, as broadcast on this frequency, takes amendments.

The Thursday after that, next week's Albert, seven days distant and warm as ever, explained slowly, in a voice like a warm blanket, how to mend a ridge aerial before a winter, for a listener who had not yet written in to say her aerial whistled. Tilda relays that show. Her aerial does not whistle. She had never once written in. She went outside and looked at the copper, and the copper looked back, whistling very quietly, and she understood that she was being mended in advance, by broadcast, out of a voice a week away, and that there was no form for this, and that nobody was ever going to tell her what she was allowed to do with it.


PART FOUR

The letters were the part she could not put down. Her relay passes Moth Radio down the bay road, and Moth Radio reads letters on air, and so next Thursday's broadcast carried, as it always did, the show from a week away, with its letters, from listeners who had not yet written them.

Most of them were the ordinary sort of letter that Moth Radio gets, whenever Moth Radio gets them: a nurse on nights at the cottage hospital asking for more stars; a man on the bay road saving up a true story for the half-moon train out of Platform Zero, asking for something slow to practise to. Tilda wrote none of this in the log. It is one thing to pencil NEXT THURSDAY under IRREGULARITIES; it is another to set down, in an official book, that you have heard a child's letter read out before the child has written it.

The child was called Tess, and she was a vole, and her letter went: "Dear Moth Radio. On Thursday my grandmother is taking me across on the ferry to see the whale, if it is still there. I have saved my pocket money in a paper knot. Please play something patient, for the waiting."

That was the letter. Not yet written, and already read, a week before its Thursday.

And in the small news of that same Thursday, seven days distant, between the weather and the whales, the flat placid voice of her own relay read the bay road's bulletin, as it does when there is small news, and next Thursday's small news was this: ferry turned back at the bar, wind on it by four; crossing not attempted; the whale, if it was still there, eleven miles out, being patient.

And then, at the end of the show, a week ahead, Wren's voice on Moth Radio, mouse-small and steady: "No ferry tonight. Somebody small waited on the quay till dark. If you're waiting somewhere, you're not the only one."

Tilda put the logbook down very carefully, the way you put down something whose tail is still moving, and thought about the shape of the week to come: a child with a paper knot of pocket money; a wind on the bar by four; a ferryman who does not sail in weather; a quay; the dark; and somebody small.

The future, she thought, is a thing you could fix. That was exactly the trouble with it. A slate can be mended. An aerial can be mended in advance. Somewhere in the seven days between her hut and that Thursday there was, she was nearly sure, a screwdriver's worth of difference between the whale seen and the whale not seen, and she had the schedule to herself, and nobody in the world to tell her no.

So she shut the hut at dusk on the Sunday, and flew down the bay road to see the ferryman.


PART FIVE

The ferryman is a sea turtle called Mott, who has kept the crossing to Moonwake Bay since his mother retired to the warm shallows, and whose bell is the brass voice of the whole bay road: one ring when the ferry goes out, one ring when it comes home. Tilda found him at the landing with his back to the last of the day, and she hung on the mooring post at his eye level, which is a courtesy on this coast, and said: "The wind will be on the bar by four, Thursday."

Mott looked at the sky, which said nothing. "Could be," he agreed.

"There's a child on Thursday's passenger list," said Tilda, and then heard herself, and started again, slower, because a bat should know better than most that it matters which way a thing is said. "There will be a child on Thursday's list, with a paper knot of pocket money, going to see the whale. The ferry will be turned back at the bar, and the child will wait on the quay till dark, in the wind, and I know it a week in advance, and I can't decide whether I'm allowed to fix it."

Mott was quiet for the length of time a turtle takes to be sure of something.

"Fix what?" he said.

"The crossing. The waiting. The wind, if I could. The whole Thursday."

"The wind is the sky's," said Mott. "The crossing is mine, and on Thursday, if there's wind on the bar, it stays tied to the quay, and that isn't custom, that's arithmetic. The child is the grandmother's." He considered. "The waiting belongs to whoever's standing in it." Then he turned one slow eye on her, the way he looks at a tide he has known longer than he has known her. "You didn't come down here to fix a Thursday, bat. You came down to ask what you're allowed to carry to it."

"Am I?" said Tilda. "Allowed?"

"The future's not a thing you fix," said Mott. "It's a place. Same as a quay is a place. Nobody fixes a quay. You stand on it in good boots, with tea, and you make the place less lonely, and then whatever comes ashore comes ashore." He began, unhurriedly, to make for the water. "The ferry leaves on the afternoon tide, every day the tide allows, and Thursday's afternoon tide it will not allow; that part will keep." One flipper paused, mid-stroke. "The kettle is in the ferry office. It is a poor kettle, and it has never once been asked to work hard, and I can hear it hoping."

PART SIX

On Thursday the wind came on the bar by four exactly, as forecast, a week in advance, by a broadcast nobody else had heard; and the ferry was turned back, and did not attempt the crossing; and on the quay, in the lee of the ferry office with the tarpaulin over the door, somebody small waited out the wind with a storm lamp, a poor kettle working hard for the first time in its life, and a bat.

They had tea. Tess's grandmother said the wind was a shame and the tea was not. Tess undid the paper knot to count the pocket money, and tied it up again, better, having been shown how by a bat who is good with string; and she asked the question children eventually ask a bat, which is what it is like, hearing everything; and Tilda told her the truth, which is that most of what a bat hears at night is weather and kettles, and that the best thing anybody ever said at two in the morning was a mouse telling the dark it wasn't the only one awake.

At dark they went home, all of them, and the whale, if it was still there, was eleven miles out, being patient.

At fourteen minutes past two, at the hut, next Thursday's broadcast came through on schedule, dated a week ahead, and Tilda sat with the logbook open and the pencil not moving. The week's weather was read, mild and mild and fog on the coast road. The small news of the bay road, seven days distant, went by, and in it there was nothing about waiting at all. No child on a quay; no wind on a dark; nothing. The future, wherever it had been keeping that Thursday, had evidently decided to keep it warm instead.

Tilda wrote in the log, under the day just gone, in the column kept for irregularities, one word: TEA. Then she thought better of it, ruled the line, and left the column blank, because some things are for listening.

The Saturday crossing was calm, exactly as next week's weather had said a week before it said it; and the whale was still there, and rose, by the account of a vole with a paper knot's worth of pocket money, like a library coming up out of the water, whatever that means. And on the Monday after, a neighbour carried a letter up the coast road to the water tower on Hill Street, because the letter was written to the radio, and letters to the radio go under the tower door. Wren read it out at two in the morning, and the relay carried it down the bay road, and Tilda heard it with her wings folded and her ears flat and her heart behaving out of all proportion to the size of a vole.

It said: "Dear Moth Radio. Thank you for the waiting. It was the best part. Play something patient for whoever brings the kettle. I have decided to lose things on purpose for a living. Yours, Tess."

She still keeps the schedule. Weather up at nine, weather down at eleven, the whales at two. Every Thursday, at fourteen minutes past two, the receiver picks up next Thursday, ninety quiet minutes of a week that hasn't happened, taking amendments; and Tilda takes very few, because the future is mostly small, and mostly able to look after itself, and has never once, so far as she can establish, asked to be saved. She mends the slates. She lets Albert mend her aerial in advance. She has sent no warnings up the line, and cancelled no fairs, and argued with no tide.

But under IRREGULARITIES, each Thursday now, at the head of the week to come, she writes the same two words, in her small hand, which are, as far as anyone on this coast has ever been able to establish, the only reliable way to fix a Thursday:

KETTLE ON.
