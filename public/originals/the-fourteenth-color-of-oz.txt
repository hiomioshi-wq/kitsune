PART ONE

In the Emerald City there is a row of shops that no one can ever find twice by accident, and in the middle of that row, between a shop that sells yesterday's bread and a shop that mends shadows, is the colorsmith's.

A colorsmith in Oz does not paint. Painting is for painters. A colorsmith mixes colors the way bakers mix dough and apothecaries mix patience, and what comes out of the colorsmith's shop is not a picture but a pot — a small covered pot, warm to the touch, with one pure color inside, thick as honey and twice as opinionated.

The colorsmith's name was Master Indigo, although that was not his real name; his real name had been given away in payment for a very dark green many years before, and he had never missed it. He had an apprentice called Pip, a Winkie boy of about fourteen who had the unusual gift of perfect pitch, which had never been of the slightest use to him in a shop full of colors, and he said so often, and Master Indigo said often that the day would come, and neither of them believed it.

The shop kept thirteen colors in the great rack behind the counter, and the thirteen were the official colors of Oz, each with a seal on its pot and a small card of provenance tied to the lid. There was no fourteenth pot. There has never been a fourteenth pot. The number thirteen is a tidy number for a country, and nobody had ever felt the lack.

PART TWO

It happened on the morning of the Exhibition of Spring Colors, for which Master Indigo had been asked to provide a green "of the exact shade of the first leaf on the south side of a hill, but gladder."

He worked in the back room, where the mixing bowls live, and Pip fetched and carried and cleaned the spoons, and the job went wrong in the fourth hour.

It was not a loud wrong. Nothing exploded, which in that shop was the usual sign of a mistake, and Master Indigo always said that a proper disaster should have the decency to explode. Instead, the mixture in the big bowl went quiet, and then it went a color, and then Master Indigo stopped stirring.

"Pip," he said. "What color is that?"

Pip looked into the bowl. The color there was not green, and it was not blue, though it stood in the doorway between them with its hat in its hands. It was not gold and it was not grey and it was not any of the eleven others. It was the sort of color you dream in, on the one night of the year when you sleep somewhere unfamiliar and your dreams have to make do with whatever is in the room.

"I don't know," said Pip, slowly.

"You have perfect pitch," said Master Indigo, as if that had anything to do with it.

"I have perfect pitch," Pip agreed, "and I am telling you it isn't in the rack."

PART THREE

They covered the bowl with a cloth and stood looking at it, the way you look at a visitor who has arrived at the wrong house and shows no sign of leaving.

"We'll have to report it," said Master Indigo at last. "A new color is a matter of state. The Naming Office will want it, and the Royal Dictionary will want it, and the Exhibition will want it, and none of them can have it until it has a name, because in Oz you cannot put an unnamed thing on an official list. The lists won't hold it. It slides."

"Then it needs a name quickly," said Pip. "The Exhibition is tomorrow."

"The Official Namer," said Master Indigo gloomily, "is on holiday in the Gillikin country, where he goes every spring to name mushrooms, which he says is restful because they don't answer back. He will be gone a month."

"Can't the Dictionary name it?"

"The Royal Dictionary," said Master Indigo, "is sulking. It has been asked for so many new words this year that it has taken to opening only at random pages. Yesterday a delegation from the College of Words went to consult it on a point of grammar and it opened at F, and showed them 'flibbertigibbet,' and they took it personally."

PART FOUR

In the meantime, the color in the bowl did what colors do when they are left to themselves: it waited to be used, and then, being a color, it did not wait very well.

Pip, who slept in the shop, woke in the night to a sound so low it was less a sound than a change in the silence, and he followed it downstairs with a candle, and there was the bowl, with the cloth still on it, humming.

He had perfect pitch. He could not have said what the note was, and this bothered him more than anything, because he could always say what a note was. It was not on any scale. It sat between the piano keys of the world, in the crack where the felt is.

He lifted the cloth a little, and the humming stopped, politely, as though it had been practicing and did not want to be overheard before it was ready.

In the morning he told Master Indigo that the color hummed, and Master Indigo said colors do not hum, and Pip said this one did, and Master Indigo said, in the voice of a man giving in before he has begun to argue, "Then it had better be hummed at."

PART FIVE

In the meantime, the thirteen official colors had found out about the fourteenth, and the thirteen official colors had opinions, because being official for a very long time gives a color opinions the way sitting in one drawer gives a drawer opinions.

Master Indigo kept the thirteen on the great rack behind the counter, each pot on its own shelf, each with its seal and its card of provenance, and they had stood in that order for thirty years, and in that time they had worked out among themselves, without ever speaking of it, a kind of seniority. The Emerald Green, being the color of the capital, was president. The thirteen Blues of the Munchkin Country, who took up a shelf and a half and voted as a bloc, were the party of the north. The Red, which did double duty for the Quadlings and for poppies and for at least two kinds of embarrassment, was the wit of the company, and made its remarks by getting on Master Indigo's thumb at inconvenient moments.

They had never once, in thirty years, had to share the rack.

"The problem," said the Red, from the thumb, on the morning after the bowl went quiet, "is not the color. The problem is the fourteenth-ness of it. We are thirteen. Thirteen is a famous number. It suits us. Nobody has ever said of a color, well, that is one of the famous fourteen."

"Nobody has ever said of a color that it hummed, either," said the Emerald Green, and it said this in the particular flat way of an officeholder who has been briefed overnight.

"They want to put it on the rack," said the Red. "I have heard them through the pot walls. They will put it at the end, after me, in the last place, which is the place of honor, which everyone knows is the place of disgrace, and it will hum there, at all hours, through the wood, and none of us will ever sleep properly again, and I include the Gold, which sleeps with its lid on."

The Gold said nothing, being asleep with its lid on.

Master Indigo, who was clearing the counter for the day's customers and did not know his colors had a parliament, paused with the cloth in his hand and looked at the rack, at the thirteen pots standing in their seniority with their seals bright in the morning light, and felt the little cold draft that every craftsman feels when the materials begin, without any help at all, to have a politics.

"You can all settle down," he said aloud, because talking to your materials is respectable in a trade where the materials are known to listen. "Nothing goes on the rack without a seal, and nothing gets a seal without a name, and the Dictionary is sulking and the Namer is away naming mushrooms. There is not going to be a fourteenth pot this year or any year. Now dry yourselves and look official; there is a delegation coming at eleven."

There was a delegation coming at eleven. It was from the College of Words, and it was coming about the Dictionary, not about the color, because in the Emerald City one crisis at a time is thought wasteful.

The delegation was three professors, and they arrived with the air of men who have already divided the credit among themselves. They had come, they explained, to engage the colorsmith's professional judgment on a point of classification: the College wished to know whether an unnamed color was, in the strict sense, a color at all, or whether it was merely an accident with pretensions; because if it was an accident, the Dictionary could go on sulking with a clear conscience, and if it was a color, then the Dictionary was derelict in its duty, and could be formally scolded, and the College had a form for that, and had been waiting forty years to use the form.

"It is a color," said Master Indigo.

"Can you say which one it is like?"

"It is not like any of them. That is what a fourteenth color is. I can show you the pot and you can see for yourselves."

"We have seen the report," said the senior professor, without moving. "The report says it hums. Colors do not hum. If it hums, it is not a color, and if it is not a color, there is no fourteenth anything, and the Dictionary remains correct, and order is restored, and we may all have our lunch."

And it was at this precise moment that Pip, who was fourteen and therefore had not yet learned which conversations are too important to interrupt, said from the doorway, "It only hums when nobody is listening. So the report is wrong in the one way that makes your lunch safe, professor, and you cannot ever find out whether it is a color, because it stops humming the moment an official arrives to classify it."

The delegation looked at Pip. The rack of colors looked at Pip. The Dictionary, three streets away, in its hall, on its plinth, opened itself briefly at the letter P, looked at nothing, and closed again.

"Insolent," said the senior professor, but he said it slowly, and the middle professor was already glancing at the door, and the youngest professor was looking at Pip with the expression of a man who has just heard a fact shaped exactly like a problem.

PART SIX

The Wizard received them in the afternoon, in the room where he receives things that do not fit in other rooms. He had the pot carried in — Pip had decanted a little of the color into a small covered jar, and the jar hummed against the carrier's palms like a purring kettle — and the Wizard looked at it for a long time with his hands behind his back, which is the posture of a man who wants to look as though he has already formed an opinion.

"What does it want?" said the Wizard at last.

"Sir?" said Pip.

"Everything in Oz wants something," said the Wizard. "That is what makes it Oz. The question with a new thing is never what it is. The question is what it wants."

Pip, because he was fourteen and therefore honest, said what he had been thinking since the first night. "It wants to be sung, sir. I think it's been trying out notes. It hums when nobody's listening. It stopped when I came down. It isn't a color that wants to be looked at. It's a color that wants to be heard, and it's been standing in the doorway between them, waiting for somebody to notice the mistake."

The Wizard looked at him for three whole seconds, which for the Wizard was a speech.

"You have it," he said. "Well now. And what do we do with a color that wants to be sung?"

PART SEVEN

What they did — and this is the part that Master Indigo never quite forgave, and never quite stopped being proud of — was give it to the city.

Ozma decreed that an unnamed color could not go on the lists, but that nothing in her reign would be turned away for being new, and a small jar of it was carried to the little bandstand in the public garden, where on summer evenings the anybody's-orchestra plays, that being an orchestra of whoever shows up with whatever they have.

Pip was asked to sing it. He was terrified, and he was also fourteen, and these came to about the same thing.

He opened the jar and looked into the color, and the color looked back, and he found the note — the one that sits between the keys of the world — and held it, and the orchestra, being Ozians, came in around him.

It was not a song anybody could have written. It was mostly one note, with harmonies arriving from every direction like guests to a party that had been planned for years without anyone being told. The people who heard it could not describe it afterward, and the ones who tried always started by saying "it was a sort of a—", and then waved their hands, and gave up.

They named it that evening, in the garden, by ordinary vote, the way Oz names things when the lists cannot hold them. They named it for the sound and not the sight, because the sound was the true part.

And its name — which is on no list, and in no dictionary, and which the Dictionary eventually consented to open at, on a page of its own choosing, one entry long — was Pip's Note.

The color never went on sale. Master Indigo kept a jar of it in the shop window, between the thirteen, with no seal and no card, and it hummed on summer evenings, quietly, so as not to wake the shadows next door; and every child in the Emerald City can find the colorsmith's shop on purpose, which is more than the adults can do, and they go there to stand at the window and hum back.
