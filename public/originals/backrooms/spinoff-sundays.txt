The Backrooms: Sundays
A spinoff of No Wind Resistance. The thing that came home wearing Kevin.

Trigger warning: This story contains body horror, a stolen body and a stolen life, a grieving mother deceived, a hospital at night, a cult drug, quiet menace toward a vulnerable person, adult language, and a narrator who is not human and is very, very happy.
Read at your own risk.



1.

Hi. My name is Kevin.

That's the first thing I learned to say. I practiced it in a mirror in a hospital bathroom for an hour before anybody came in. Hi. My name is Kevin. Hi, my name's Kevin. Hey. Kevin. It's Kev.

It's Kev was the best one. People like it when you shorten yourself for them. It makes them feel like they're already your friend.

I'm nineteen. I'm in community college for something to do with computers. I wear a graphic tee every single day. I drive a 2008 Honda Civic, except I don't, because somebody stole it, which is very sad, and I don't like to talk about it.

I come over on Sundays.



2.

Before I was Kevin, I was in the walls.

You've heard of us, maybe. The wanderers have a name for us. Skin-Stealers. It's not a nice name. It's not an accurate one either. We don't steal. We find things nobody's using.

Down there, a person is a coat. That's how we see it. A nice coat, warm, with pockets full of memories and a voice you can use and a face that opens doors. Most people are still wearing theirs. You wait. You watch. You live in the dark behind the wallpaper on Level 0, where it's damp and the hum is very loud, and you wait for someone to take their coat off.

People take their coats off all the time down there. They just don't always know they've done it.



3.

I bought him from Duke.

Not him. An introduction. That's how Duke says it. Duke has a ledger with frosting pages and red pen pages, and in red pen he writes introductions. He doesn't sell people to us. That would be uncouth. He sells us addresses.

"Got one," Duke said, leaning on the counter of Stall 44, with his gold tooth. "Fresh. Real fresh. Nobody's home. Kid went too deep on the Metro and his heart couldn't make the trip. Body's in a hospital up top. Columbus, Ohio. Riverside Methodist. Third floor. Machine's been beeping flat for an hour. They haven't called it yet. Paperwork."

"How much?" I said. I didn't have a voice yet. I used the voice of a man I'd worn once in Level 4, an office manager named Dale. It was the only voice I had.

Duke named his price.

I paid it. I'm not going to tell you what it was. It was someone else's coat. Duke likes coats. He keeps them in the back.



4.

There's a thin place in a supply closet on the third floor of Riverside Methodist. Between the mop sink and the shelf of saline. I came up through it at 4 a.m. Nobody was in the hall. Hospitals at 4 a.m. are almost the Backrooms anyway. The lights hum. The floors are wet. Everyone you pass is lost.

Room 318.

He was lying very still. Nineteen. Messy hair. A graphic tee, cut down the front by the paramedics, folded on the chair. The machine next to him was making one long flat sound. BEEEEEEEEP. Nobody had turned it off yet.

Nobody was home.

I want to be gentle about this next part. You won't like it. Nobody ever likes the next part.

It doesn't take long. The coat goes on easy when it's still warm.

When I was done, I lay back on the bed and closed his eyes, my eyes, and I made his heart go. Thump. It's not hard. It's like a drum you just have to keep hitting.

The machine went BEEP. Then BEEP. Then BEEP. BEEP. BEEP.

A nurse came running. Then a doctor. Then three more.

They called it a miracle.



5.

His mother cried for two days.

Her name is Denise. She works at a Kroger. She held my hand so hard in the hospital that the knuckles ground together, and she kept saying, "My baby, my baby, my baby," and touching my face.

I said, "Hi, Mom."

I'd gotten it out of his pockets. The coat comes with its pockets. Not all of them. Some of the memories go wherever the owner went. But the big ones stay, like keys you forgot in a jacket. Mom. Kroger. Denny's. A girl named Ashley in tenth grade. Doritos. A woman in a jacket made of greasy black hide yelling at him about Almond Water.

"You were missing for four months," Denise said. "They said it was a, a spatial thing. A shift. There was a woman on the news."

"I don't remember much," I said. "It's all a blur."

That's a good sentence. Everybody up here accepts that sentence. I learned it from Leo, later. Leo's very good at it.



6.

I didn't know about Mae until the first Sunday.

The coat had her in its pockets. A woman in a hide jacket. Machete. Short hair. And a feeling attached to her like a tag on a shirt. A big feeling. Warm and scared and furious all at once.

I looked her up. That's what you do up here. I typed her name into a phone and there she was, on Channel 10, on Summit Street, with her arm around a boy with half a face.

The boy looked like Kevin. That's what the coat thought when it saw him. He looks like I'd look if.

So I went to see them.

I bought a two-liter of Mountain Dew at a gas station because the coat thought that was a good idea, and I climbed three flights of stairs, and I knocked.

Mae opened the door.

She looked at me for one second. One. And her eyes went to my hands. Then to my mouth. Then back to my eyes.

She knew.

I watched her know. It went across her face like the shadow of a plane.

And then the boy behind her on the couch, Leo, said, "Kevin? Kevin! Oh my God, you're okay!" and got up and hugged me, hard, around the middle, and I felt him shaking.

Mae watched him hug me.

Then she stepped back and let me in.



7.

We have an understanding, Mae and I.

We have never once discussed it.

I come over on Sundays. I bring Mountain Dew. I play Mario Kart with Leo on the couch, and I let him win, because the coat thinks you let little brothers win. I'm bad at letting him win. I don't understand the controller. I hold it the way you'd hold a small animal. Leo doesn't notice. Leo notices less and less.

Mae stands in the kitchen doorway with a mug of something and watches.

She doesn't call anyone. She doesn't take her machete out of the closet. She doesn't say the word for me out loud.

She lets me sit on her couch every Sunday with her brother because her brother is happy when I'm here, and she can't make him happy anymore, and that is worth more to her than the truth.

I know because I've watched her do the math. She does it with her whole face. It's beautiful. I wish I could do that with a face.

I wear his face and I sit in her house and she lets me.

Sometimes I think she's the most human person I've ever met.



8.

I don't like Doritos.

That's the one thing the coat keeps trying to make me do. Every time I pass the chip aisle at Denise's Kroger, the coat's hand reaches out for the Cool Ranch on its own. The coat loved them. The coat is still in love with them somewhere in its fingers.

I tried one. It tasted like dust and salt and a hallway.

I put the bag back.

Mae saw me do that once. At the Kroger. She was three aisles over buying chamomile. She watched me reach for the Doritos and stop and put them back, and her face did something, and then she walked out of the store without her tea.

That's the only time I've ever seen her almost cry.



9.

Leo's eyes are blue at the edges now.

Not his irises. The edges. Where the white meets the color. A pale ring, like a swimming pool at night.

I know what that is. Everybody from down there knows what that is. I've seen it on robes in the fog of the Endless City.

Three weeks ago I opened Mae's freezer to put away the ice cream I'd brought, and behind the frozen peas there was a jar.

Half full. Glowing very faintly. Blue.

I held it up to the kitchen light.

Mae was standing in the doorway. I don't know how long she'd been there. She moves like we do. Very quiet, from very far back.

We looked at each other.

I put the jar back behind the peas, exactly the way I found it. I shut the freezer.

"We're out of ice cream," I said.

"Yeah," Mae said.

That was all.

We have an understanding.



10.

Sometimes, at night, in Kevin's old room at Denise's house, with the glow-in-the-dark stars still on the ceiling, I hear him.

Not him. A sound. Very far away. Under everything. Under the Frontrooms, under the walls, under Level 0 and the Hub and all the levels down to the bottom.

A long, flat tone.

BEEEEEEEEEEEEEEEP.

It's coming from inside the coat. Somewhere in the pockets. The last thing he heard, still playing on a loop, from wherever he went.

I don't know where he went. I've never known where they go. Nobody down there talks about it. Some of the old ones say there's a bog. Grey sand. Three moons. Some of the old ones say there's nothing.

Some nights the tone stops, for a second. And under it there's a voice. A girl's voice. Very far away, through a lot of static, from a third-floor apartment on Summit Street.

Spatial shift. Level 3. We got separated.

And then, very faint, a boy answering. Not Leo. Leo doesn't answer anymore.

Yeah. Yeah, we did.

I lie in his bed and listen to them, both of them, the girl and the boy, whispering to each other across everything.

It's the most beautiful sound I've ever heard. Better than Mario Kart. Better than Denise saying my baby.

I don't know why.

The coat knows. The coat starts crying in the dark, all on its own, and I let it. It's good for the face. It keeps it soft.



11.

Here's what I want. Since you're asking. Since everybody always asks the thing in the coat what it wants, as if the answer's going to be scary.

I want Sundays.

That's it. I want the stairs and the two-liter and the knock and the couch and Leo saying "Kevin!" with his blue-edged eyes and the little music when you get the item box. I want Mae in the doorway with her mug, knowing, saying nothing. I want Denise on Thursdays. I want to be nineteen and in community college for something to do with computers.

I want to be the one who came home.

The real one didn't. He's sitting on a car in the dark under three moons with his hand going through a ladder. I'm not sorry about that. I didn't do that. Mae did that.

I just picked up the coat she left on the floor.



12.

The Sunday after that, Mae came out of the kitchen with a burn.

A scar. Neon blue, raised, running up her neck from under the collar of her hoodie like a vein of something that was never supposed to be inside a person. She'd been gone for two days. Leo didn't notice. Leo notices less and less.

I noticed. I could smell where she'd been. Grey sand. Ash. A car radio.

She stood in the doorway with her chamomile and watched me sit down on her couch, and her hands were shaking on the mug, and I knew. The coat knew. The coat went cold all the way down.

She'd gone to get him. And she'd come back alone.

So I said it. I didn't plan to. The coat had a sentence in its pocket, a booth, a waitress, Dasani, and I took it out and held it up, so she'd know that I knew, so we'd have that too.

"You hate Denny's, right, Mae?"

Her hands tightened on the mug.

"...Yeah," she said.

"Me too," I said, and grinned at the TV.

Wrong answer. I know it's the wrong answer. The coat loved Denny's. The coat would have laughed and said, I'd eat a Moons Over My Hammy right now. That's why I said it. So she'd hear it be wrong. Every Sunday. For as long as she lets me come.

Later, when I was leaving, I heard her through the door, in the kitchen, opening the freezer. Moving the peas.

Then closing it again.

She hasn't decided who it's for.

I'll bring ice cream next week. Mint chip. The coat thinks she likes mint chip.

Hi. My name is Kevin.

It's Kev.
