The Backrooms: The Flock
A spinoff of No Wind Resistance. Leo, from the ladder to the train.

Trigger warning: This story contains abandonment by a sibling, body horror, a cult, memory loss on purpose, violence against strangers, drug use, adult language, and a narrator who remembers everything and decides to lie about it forever.
Read at your own risk.



1.

The last thing I saw before the Bird was my sister's boots.

Black Converse, the high-tops, with a Sharpie doodle of a frog on the left toe she drew in study hall. Going up the ladder. Rung, rung, rung. Away from my hand.

I was hanging by four fingers from the bottom rung over a tunnel full of noise. Level 3. The Electrical Station, the wiki calls it, if you ever read the wiki, which we did, both of us, for fun, in her dorm room, a month before the mall in Ohio and the service elevator that didn't stop at the basement.

My skateboard was caught on a pipe. I'd spent two summers saving for that board.

I said her name. I said it loud. I said please.

She looked down at me. I want to be fair about this. She did look down. Her fingers brushed mine. And then she looked past me, at the thing coming down the tunnel, and I watched her face do the math.

Then the boots went up. The hatch opened. Yellow light. The hatch closed.

I heard her sit on it.

You can hear that, you know. When someone sits on a metal hatch. It goes boom, low, like a drum. I hear it sometimes still. In the Frontrooms. At Denny's. When somebody sits down too hard in the next booth.



2.

The Clump took me at the bottom of the ladder.

If you've never seen one: imagine a rolling ball made of people. Arms and legs and faces, all fused, all moving, some of them still talking. It doesn't eat you, exactly. It adds you.

I let go of the rung because my fingers gave out. I fell into it. And for a minute, I was in it. I could feel the others. Hands holding my hands. Someone's cheek pressing on my back. A woman somewhere near my ear saying Gary, Gary, Gary, over and over, in a voice like a scratched CD.

It took my left shoe. It took the skateboard. It took a strip of skin off the left side of my face from my temple to my jaw, the way you'd peel tape off a box.

And then, I don't know why, it spat me out.

Maybe I was too small. Maybe I was too sad. Maybe it just didn't want me either. That would be funny, right? Two in one day.

I lay on the grating in the sparks and the roar with half a face, and I didn't get up, because what for.



3.

The people in the robes found me there.

Blue robes. Soft, like bathrobes. White masks, smooth, porcelain, no mouths, just two eyeholes and a little painted beak. They came walking down the tunnel in a line, slowly, humming, holding jars of something that glowed a pale blue.

I thought they were entities. I thought, this is it, this is what kills me.

They knelt down around me in a circle.

One of them put a hand on my forehead. Cool. Gentle. Like my mom when I had strep.

"Oh, little one," she said, behind her mask. "Who left you here?"

And I said it. I said, "My sister." And it was the first time I said it out loud, and it was so much worse out loud.

"Shh," she said. "Shh. We know. We know all about sisters. We know all about being left." She uncapped a jar. "Would you like to stop knowing?"

That's the offer. That's the whole thing. That's what nobody tells you about the Followers of Jerry. They don't make you. They ask.

I drank it.



4.

Blue water tastes like a swimming pool and a birthday.

And then it tastes like nothing, and then you're sitting in a room you don't remember walking into, and someone is washing your face with a cloth, and you can't remember why it hurts.

That's the first gift. Forgetting the bad thing.

They told me about the Bird. They called it Jerry, which is a stupid name for a god, and they knew that, and they didn't care. In the Frontrooms Jerry is a cartoon, they said, a joke, a blue bird on a sticker. But down here every joke is a door, and behind this one is something real. Something kind. Something that watches every wanderer in every level and grieves for them.

The Bird sees every hand that ever let go of another hand, they said. The Bird remembers so you don't have to.

I didn't believe it. I was fifteen. I was from Columbus. I believed in Taco Bell and Mae.

Then I met it.



5.

I can't describe the Bird. Everyone tries. No one gets it right.

It's on Level 11, in a building in the Endless City that doesn't have a door from the outside. You go in from below. Up a stairwell that never ends, and then it does.

It's blue. It's big. It has wings like a ceiling. It has no eyes, or it has a thousand, I can't remember. When it looks at you, you feel seen. Not like at school. Not like a teacher or a cop. Like a mother looking at a sleeping kid in a hospital bed. All of you. Every bit. Forgiven.

It sang to me. Not words. Just a sound, like wind in a bottle.

And I stopped being a kid who was left on a ladder.

I was just one of the flock.

They gave me a robe. They gave me a mask. White, with the little blue beak. When I put it on, the left side of my face stopped hurting for the first time since the Clump.

"We don't need faces," the woman said. Her name was Ruth. She'd been a dental hygienist in Tucson. She didn't remember that. I only know it because later I remembered everything, including the things I wasn't supposed to know. "We have the flock."



6.

I was in the flock for two years.

I need to tell you what we did. Because Mae thinks she knows, and she doesn't, and Kevin thinks he knows, and he really doesn't.

We found fresh ones. Wanderers. Noclippers. Kids, mostly. Kids are easiest. They fall through a wall behind a Starbucks or a Denny's, and they land in Level 0 alone and scared, and if Mae doesn't find them first, or one of the B.N.T.G. scavengers, or a Hound, or a Smiler, sometimes we did.

And we offered.

Would you like to stop knowing?

Most of them said yes. You would too. You fall into an infinite yellow hallway and someone in a soft robe kneels down and puts a cool hand on your forehead. You'd say yes.

The ones who said no.

I'm not going to make this sound better than it was.

The flock calls it cleansing. Ruth would hold their arms, and someone else would hold their head, and I'd pour the jar. Slowly. Into the side of their mouth, while they choked. Until they stopped fighting. Until their eyes went soft and blue at the edges and they said, "Oh. Oh, I see."

I did that maybe thirty times. Maybe forty.

I didn't feel bad. That was the second gift. You don't feel bad. You feel like you're helping. You feel like a nurse.

I put a mask on a girl who couldn't have been older than twelve. She'd been on a school trip. She had a lanyard. I took the lanyard off and put it in a pile with the others.

The pile was big.



7.

I knew about Mae.

That's the thing Mae will never know. That's what the Bird showed me. Everyone says the Bird erases your memories, and it does, but that's not all it does. It takes your memories. It keeps them. It's the Bird. It remembers so you don't have to.

And sometimes, if it loves you, it shows you somebody else's.

It showed me hers.

I stood in the room with no door with my mask on and the Bird spread its wings over me like a ceiling and I saw a hand. A girl's hand. My sister's hand. With a short black fingernail, chipped. Holding a rope.

And on the other end of the rope a young guy in a Starbucks apron with tape over his mouth.

And then another. A woman in scrubs. And another. A guy my age with a Steelers hoodie. Walking in front of her through the yellow hallways to a warehouse on Level 1, where a man in a Hawaiian shirt with a gold tooth counted out flasks.

Three flasks. Two flasks. Five, for a young one.

Hand after hand after hand. All of them let go of.

That's what the Bird sees. Every hand that ever let go of another hand. It had seen my sister's hand do it two hundred times.

The Bird didn't show me that to make me hate her. That's not what it's for. It showed me so I'd understand.

She was buying me back.

With other people.

Every flask. Every Level Key she'd trade the flasks for. Every route. She was building a ladder of people, all the way down, to get to the bottom rung where I was hanging.

I stood in the room with the Bird singing over me and I cried under my mask, and I didn't know why, because I'd forgotten I had a sister. And then the Bird took it back, gently, the way you take scissors from a kid, and I forgot again.



8.

Here's the part with Duke.

He came to the edge of our territory on Level 11 with a ledger under his arm and a smile with one gold tooth in it, and he asked to speak to the one with the scar.

They brought me out. I didn't know him. I didn't know anything.

"Got something for you," Duke said. "Somebody's looking for you. Real hard. Real expensive. And I thought, Duke, you know what's better than one person paying you? Two."

He opened the ledger and showed me a page. A route. In red pen. Levels and stairwells and the times she passed through them.

"She's been coming through the Endless City twice a month," Duke said. "Hub to hub. She'll come again. I'll sell you the times for a jar of the blue stuff. The good stuff. Not cut."

I didn't remember her. That's the truth. But the Bird had shown me her hand, and some part of me under the mask that didn't belong to the flock, some part with half a face, said yes.

I gave him the jar. He gave me the route. He wrote it in his ledger. Frosting pages, he called them. I don't know what that means. I didn't ask.

Then we went and waited for her in the fog.



9.

You know that part. She saw me in the fog of the Endless City. She screamed my name. She begged. I looked at her like a stranger and said Jerry loves you, and then the flock came out of the shadows with knives, and she ran.

Then, months later, the station again. Her and a kid in a graphic tee who looked, God, who looked like me. Who looked like I would have looked if I'd gotten to grow up.

I pointed at him and said he was impure. I told them to cleanse him. I meant it. I had the jar in my hand.

And then the kid held up a cracked datapad and turned something up to max, and the station screamed with static, and the Bird started crying.

I'd never heard it cry. I didn't know it could.

It wasn't a sound. It was a feeling, coming through everyone in the flock all at once, like a phone ringing in every pocket. Every memory it was holding for us. Every hand. All of it. Falling out of its wings like feathers.

I took the mask off because I couldn't breathe.

And I remembered.

Not some of it. All of it. Columbus. The mall. The service elevator. The ladder. The boots going up. The hatch. The boom. The Clump. The shoe. The skin. Ruth. The girl with the lanyard. Thirty or forty jars poured into thirty or forty mouths. Duke's gold tooth.

My sister's hand on a rope, two hundred times.

All of it at once. On the cold floor of a train station on Level 11 with my sister running at me with her arms open.



10.

Here's what I said.

"Mae? Where am I? It's so cold."

That's what's in the script. That's what everybody knows. The poor kid with the scar, waking up from the cult with no idea where he was. Brainwashed. Blank. A victim.

It was a lie.

I knew exactly where I was.

I had about two seconds to decide, while she ran at me, and I've thought about those two seconds every day since. I could have said it. I remember the ladder. I remember you sitting on the hatch. I know about the rope and the warehouse and the man with the gold tooth. I know what you are. I know what I am.

She would have stopped running.

Or she wouldn't. That's the thing. Maybe she would have hugged me anyway. Maybe she'd have said I know, I know, I'm sorry. Maybe we could have gotten on the train and been two monsters together, honest, the way we were in her dorm room reading the wiki.

But I looked at her face. Older. Harder. Hound-skin jacket. Machete. Scars. And underneath all of it, this look I'd never seen on her before, not once, not even on the ladder.

Hope.

So I lied.

And she hugged me. And she said Leo, Leo, Leo, into my hair. And Kevin cried. And the train doors beeped. And we got on.



11.

We're in the Frontrooms now.

I live in Mae's apartment in Columbus. Real sky. A real Denny's, which she says she hates and goes to three times a week. Kevin comes over on Sundays and plays Mario Kart with me and lets me win, badly, so I can tell.

Mae tells people we got separated. Spatial shift. Level 3. She tells it so smoothly. To my mom. To the cops. To the news lady from Channel 10. To me, sometimes, like she's reminding me of the story in case I forgot it.

I nod along. I say I don't remember much. I say it's all a blur. Blue light. Robes.

She believes me. She needs to.

We're the same now. That's what I figured out. Two people in a little apartment in Ohio, both carrying a story about a ladder, both telling the wrong version, both knowing the other one is lying, maybe, deep down, and never saying it.

That's a family. That's what one is. I didn't know that before.



12.

I kept one thing.

When I took the mask off in the station, a feather fell out of the inside of it. Blue. Long. Soft. The Bird must have left it there, the way you'd leave a note in a kid's lunchbox.

It's in the drawer next to my bed, under my socks.

Some nights I take it out. I hold it. I miss the flock. Isn't that sick? I miss Ruth's cool hand. I miss the robe. I miss the mask on the left side of my face that made it stop hurting. I miss not feeling bad.

I miss not knowing.

Sometimes, at three in the morning, I hear Mae through the wall. Whispering to the ceiling. She does it every night. Spatial shift. Level 3. We got separated.

And I lie in the dark in my real bed under my real roof and whisper back, very quietly, so she'll never hear it, the only thing anybody ever said to me down there that I believed.

Jerry loves you.
