The Backrooms: Spatial Shift
A spinoff of No Wind Resistance. Mae, in the Frontrooms, after The Flock.

Trigger warning: This story contains people traded as goods, drugging a family member, a cult, relapse into old crimes, self-hate, adult language, and a lead who finds out the truth and decides she'd rather have the lie.
Read at your own risk.



1.

The apartment is on the third floor of a brick building on Summit Street in Columbus, and it has a real window with a real sky in it, and I hate the sky.

Nobody tells you that. You spend five years in the yellow. Lights humming. Carpet wet. You dream about sky. Real blue. Clouds. Weather. You'd sell anybody for it. I did.

And then you get it, and it's too big. There's no ceiling. Anything could come down out of it. I keep the blinds shut. Leo says it's like living in a submarine. I tell him to drink his Almond Water.

It's not Almond Water. It's just tap water with a drop of almond extract from Kroger. I can't drink anything else. My body doesn't believe in it.

Leo drinks Mountain Dew. Leo's adjusting great. Leo's doing amazing.

That's what I tell the lady from Channel 10, and the cops, and my mom, who cries every time she looks at the left side of his face.

Spatial shift. Level 3. We got separated. He doesn't remember much. It's all a blur.



2.

Three times a week we go to the Denny's on Morse Road.

I hate Denny's. I've said it to Kevin a hundred times. I say it every time we walk in. The lighting. The syrup. That carpet that's almost the right color yellow.

Kevin comes over on Sundays. He's nineteen and he's in community college for something to do with computers and he still wears a graphic tee every single day, and he plays Mario Kart with Leo and lets him win, badly. He thinks I don't notice. I notice everything. I've noticed everything since I was nineteen and my hand opened on a ladder.

Kevin thinks I'm a hero.

Kevin doesn't know what I was doing with my radio the first day I saw him. He doesn't know the number five. He doesn't know Duke's gold tooth, or that he had the face of a sale before he had the face of a friend.

I keep it that way. It's kind. It's the only kind thing I do.



3.

The walls in the apartment are thin.

I sleep three hours a night. Two to five. Then I wake up with my hand held out in front of me in the dark, open, in the shape of his hand, and I whisper it to the ceiling. The thing I say. It keeps the ladder in its box.

Spatial shift. Level 3. We got separated.

I've been doing it for five years. I did it on Level 1 in the warehouse on a cot. I did it in Level 11 in a bank vault. I did it in the Frontrooms in a real bed under a real roof.

And three weeks ago, at 3:04 in the morning, after I whispered it, the wall whispered back.

Very quiet. Almost nothing. From Leo's room.

Jerry loves you.



4.

I didn't move for an hour.

You learn to lie still on Level 0. Something hears you, you lie still. I lay in my real bed with my eyes open and listened to my brother breathing through the wall and told myself I'd heard the radiator.

The next night I didn't sleep. I waited. 3:04. My whisper. Spatial shift. Level 3. We got separated.

Pause.

Jerry loves you.

Three nights. Every night.

On the fourth day, when he was at his GED class, I went into his room.



5.

I'm good at searching rooms. I searched a lot of rooms. Abandoned stalls on Level 1. Dead wanderers' packs. Smilers' nests, once, which I don't recommend.

It took me four minutes.

Sock drawer. Under the socks. Wrapped in a Wendy's napkin.

A feather.

Blue. Long. Soft. The softest thing I have ever touched, softer than anything should be. When I held it, I smelled chlorine and cake, and the left side of my own face went warm, like someone had cupped it.

I know what it was. Everybody down there knows. You see them sometimes in the fog of the Endless City, caught on the rebar. Wanderers who know better walk around them.

It's from the Bird.

He kept a piece of the Bird.

And he whispers back to me at three in the morning in the voice he used in the train station, the calm one, the one that said Jerry misses you, Mae, the Blue Bird forgives you.

Which means he never stopped.

Which means, when he took that mask off, and looked at me with his eyes wide and said, Mae? Where am I? It's so cold?

He knew exactly where he was.

Which means he remembers the ladder.



6.

I sat on his bed with the feather in my lap for a long time.

I could tell you I thought about talking to him. Sitting down at the kitchen table when he got home. Pouring two glasses of fake Almond Water. Saying, I know. I know you know. I know I let go. I'm sorry. Let's say it out loud, once, the real version, and then we can stop whispering through the wall.

That's what a sister does. That's what a person does. Kevin would do it. Kevin would cry and hug him and it would be awful and then it would be better.

I'm not Kevin.

Here's what I actually thought about.

I thought about his face on the ladder. The way he said please. I've had five years of not seeing his face when I close my eyes, because I put it in the box. Spatial shift. Level 3. That's the box. The whisper is the lid.

If he says it out loud, the lid comes off.

If he says it out loud, every Sunday at Denny's he's going to look at me across the table and I'll know that he's seeing the boots going up. Every time I hand him a Mountain Dew. Every time I tell the Channel 10 lady he's adjusting great.

He'll know what I am. And I'll know he knows. In this tiny apartment with the blinds shut.

I can't live in that. I can live in a lot of things. I lived in the yellow. Not that.

I put the feather back under the socks, exactly the way I found it, Wendy's napkin folded the same.

And I went to get my machete.



7.

There's a spot behind the Denny's on Morse Road where the wall is thin.

I'm not saying how I know. Everybody who's been down knows how to find them in the Frontrooms. You feel it in your back teeth. A place where the brick hums at the same note as the lights.

I went at 2 a.m. Hound-skin jacket out of the back of my closet, still a little greasy. Machete. Bandolier. Gas mask. A rope, and a roll of tape.

I leaned on the wall by the dumpster.

The wall wasn't there anymore.

Wet carpet. Yellow. The hum.

I took a breath, and it smelled like home, and I hated that most of all.



8.

Duke's still at Stall 44 on Level 1. Nobody kills Duke. Too useful.

He saw me coming across the warehouse and his gold tooth came out like a sunrise.

"Mae," he said. "Mae, Mae, Mae. Look at you. They said you got out. They said you got on the train with your brother and the Denny's kid and you were living in Ohio, and I said, nah. Not Mae. Mae doesn't stay up."

"I need the blue stuff," I said.

He stopped smiling. Just for a second. Then it came back bigger.

"Huh," he said. He took out his ledger. That ledger. I've seen my name in it a hundred times. "Funny you should ask. Got one jar. Good stuff. Not cut. Came from a boy with a scar on his face, on Level 11, in exchange for a little route information. Your route, actually."

He watched my face.

I didn't give him anything. I've been not giving Duke anything for five years.

"How much," I said.

"Oh, it's not for flasks," Duke said. "You know that. Blue's not a flask thing. Blue's a frosting thing."

I knew that.

I'd brought the rope.



9.

She dropped out of the ceiling on Level 0 about a mile from the stairs to Level 1, landing on her back on the wet carpet. Nineteen, maybe. A Wendy's visor. A name tag that said BRITNEY with a heart over the i. Fries on her shirt.

She sat up and said, "What the fuck? Chad? Is this a prank?"

They always say it's a prank.

I didn't look at her face. That's my rule. That's been my rule since Leo. Face is how you get stuck. I looked at her shoes. White Nikes, cheap, with a grease stain on the right toe.

"Listen to me very carefully," I said.

She did. They always do. I have a voice for it. Calm. Like someone who's going to help.

I walked her for four hours. She held my hand on the stairs. She told me about her little sister, who had a dance recital Saturday. She said I was really nice. She said she was so lucky I found her.

At Stall 44 I took the rope out.

She looked at me. Not angry. Just confused. Like a kid whose mom just took the wrong exit.

"Everybody's got a sister," I said, before she could say it.

Duke gave her a balloon.

He gave me the jar.



10.

It glows when you hold it in the dark. Pale blue. Like a pool at night.

I came up through the wall behind the Denny's at 7 a.m. and a guy taking out the trash looked right at me in my Hound-skin jacket with my machete, and I looked at him, and he went back inside. People in the Frontrooms are good at not seeing things. That's the only thing I like about them.

I put the jar in the freezer behind the frozen peas.

Leo was eating Froot Loops at the counter. He looked up.

"Where were you?"

"Walk," I said. "Couldn't sleep."

He looked at my boots. There was yellow carpet fiber on them. Wet.

He didn't say anything.

That's what we do. We don't say anything.



11.

You don't need much. A capful. That's what Duke said. A capful for a few months. A jar for forever.

I'm not doing forever. I want to be clear. I'm not an animal.

I just want the last five years. I want the ladder gone. I want the flock gone, the robes and the masks and the cleansings, I know about those too, I'm not stupid, I've seen the lanyards in the pile. I want it gone for him. I want him to be a kid who got lost in a mall in Ohio and got scared and doesn't remember much.

The version I tell Channel 10.

I want my story to be true.

Sunday morning I made tea. Real tea, for once. Chamomile, with honey, the way Mom used to make it when we were sick. Two mugs. The blue looks green in chamomile. You can't see it at all if you stir.

I set his mug down in front of him at the table.

He looked at it. Then at me.

He's not stupid either. He was in the flock for two years. He poured that jar into thirty or forty mouths. He knows what chlorine and birthday cake smells like.

He held the mug in both hands for a long time.

"Mae," he said.

"Drink your tea, Leo."

He looked at me. Half a face. My brother. And I thought, here it comes. He's going to say it. He's going to set the mug down and say, I remember the ladder. I remember the boots. I remember you sitting on the hatch.

He didn't.

He picked up the mug and drank the whole thing.

"Tastes like a swimming pool," he said.

And then his eyes went soft and blue at the edges, and he smiled at me, a little kid's smile, and said, "Hi, Mae. Is Kevin coming over?"



12.

Kevin came over. They played Mario Kart. Kevin let Leo win, badly. Leo didn't notice.

That night, at 3:04, I woke up with my hand out in front of me in the dark.

I whispered it to the ceiling.

Spatial shift. Level 3. We got separated.

And I waited.

Nothing came through the wall. Not a whisper. Just my brother's breathing, slow and even and happy.

I lay there for a long time listening to nobody answer.

It should have felt like the lid going back on the box. Like the ladder going away. That's what I paid for. That's what Britney with the heart over the i was for.

But it turns out the lid isn't the whisper.

The lid was him whispering back.

Because now I'm the only one in the apartment on Summit Street who knows. And there's nobody on the other side of the wall to know it with me. Just me. In the dark. With my hand held out, open, in the shape of a hand that isn't there.

There's half a jar left in the freezer behind the peas.

I think about it every night at 3:04.

I haven't decided who it's for.
