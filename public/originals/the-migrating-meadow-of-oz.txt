PART ONE

In the Quadling Country, which is the southern part of Oz and is red the way the Munchkin Country is blue, there is a meadow that walks.

It goes south. That is the only direction it has ever gone, which the Quadlings say shows good sense, since south in that country is toward the strawberries. Every full moon, when the light lies flat on the fields like a dropped ribbon, the meadow takes a step. It is not a long step. A meadow is not built for striding, and knows it. But over the course of a year, which is twelve or thirteen steps depending on the moon's arithmetic, the meadow has moved a good half mile from where it started, and it takes its flowers with it, and its bees, and its three birch trees, and one extremely loyal birdbath that has never once asked to be let off.

The birdbath came with the meadow from the beginning, or near enough, and nobody in the nearby village of Tickle Down knows why. It is a stone birdbath, of the ordinary sort, wide and shallow, with a chipped rim where a cart wheel caught it in a year nobody remembers. The meadow keeps it the way a ship keeps a figurehead, and the birds of three counties have arranged their affairs around it, since a birdbath that changes its address every full moon is, for a bird, the most interesting thing in the world.

The village of Tickle Down had never minded the walking. Farms mind; villages, which are more philosophical, do not. The meadow's path crossed nobody's fence, for the simple reason that the meadow had been walking longer than the fences, and the fences, in a spirit of neighborliness, had put themselves where the meadow was not going to be.

But Oz, in the last few years, had been getting organized, and organized countries have registries.

PART TWO

The Land Registry of the Quadling Country was a new building in Tickle Down, red brick, with a map of the country on the wall that was the pride of the office, and a head registrar whose name was Mr. Pike and who believed, with the whole of his heart, that a map is a promise.

Mr. Pike had drawn the map himself. Every farm was on it, every fence, every bench, every named tree, and in the northeast corner, drawn in good faith from the reports of honest surveyors, was the meadow, with its flowers and its three birches and its birdbath, drawn where it had stood one Tuesday in the spring.

The following June, the map was wrong.

Mr. Pike took this personally. He was not a bad man; he was a careful one, which is different, and often worse. He sent a surveyor, and the surveyor came back and reported that the meadow had moved eight inches since the spring, and Mr. Pike wrote a memorandum, and the memorandum said: REGISTRY OBJECTS TO MOVING, and he sent it to the meadow, because in Oz you can send things to meadows, and the meadow received it, and the bees carried the paper off and lost it in a thistle, and that was the end of that for a season.

The next full moon the meadow took another step, and this time it stepped off the edge of the map, and Mr. Pike came to the village inn that evening with the look of a man whose house has been rearranged in his sleep.

"It is not the walking," he said, to the inn at large. "I could stand the walking. It is that it will not tell anyone where it is going."

PART THREE

The Registry had forms for everything, and among the forms was one for this exact situation, or near enough: Form Eleven, NOTICE TO QUIT, VACATE, OR OTHERWISE DEPART, which had been written for tenants and had never once been served on a meadow.

Mr. Pike filled it out in his best hand. And then, because the Registry's charter obliged him to serve notice in person, and because no one in Tickle Down would do it for any money — for the village liked the meadow, and a village that likes a thing will do anything with it except serve it papers — he hired the only person willing, who was a shepherd girl named Betony Marsh.

Betony kept sheep on the southern common with a clockwork dog named Tick, who had been built by a Gillikin tinker as a present for a duke and had been given away at once, dukes not wanting dogs that can be wound. He was a very good dog. He kept time, which for a sheepdog is nearly everything.

Betony took the job because it was a job. Her mother needed a new axle, and the Registry paid well for unusual service, and serving a paper on a meadow sounded, on the face of it, like the easiest day's work in the world.

"You have to find it first," said the innkeeper, when she told him.

"It's a meadow," said Betony. "It's green and it's in a field. How far can it go?"

The innkeeper looked at her with the patience of a man who has lived beside a moving meadow all his life.

"Full moon tonight," he said.

PART FOUR

Mr. Pike deserves a moment, before the moon rises, because the story has been unkind to him and he was not the villain of it. No one in Tickle Down was the villain of it. That is the trouble with stories that are only about a map and a meadow: there is no room in them for a careful man who is right about nearly everything.

He was right about the map. The map was a promise; ask any surveyor. A promise cannot keep sliding southwest by eight inches a month without somebody eventually having to say so, and the somebody is never the map.

He was right about the paper. Form Eleven, whatever its mercy on the reverse, had been drawn up for tenants, and a meadow was not a tenant, and serving papers on a thing that has never once had an address is a legal impossibility that the Registry's charter could not actually permit, and Mr. Pike had signed it anyway, in his best ink, with his eyes open, which was the most honest cowardice in the Quadling Country that spring.

And he was right, most of all, about the birdbath, though he never knew it. When the surveyors walked the ground with their strings, in June, on the common, and the spiral came out of the clover line by line, Mr. Pike stood at the edge of the party with the map under his arm and watched Betony Marsh stake out the meadow's four-hundred-year sentence, going home at the speed of a thoughtful cow, and he took out his pen, the good one, and he drew the spiral exactly, and he was, for eleven minutes, the happiest registrar in the history of the Quadling Country; because the promise was mended; because the map was now truer than any map of a fixed field had ever been, being the only map in Oz with a future on it.

He wrote the line under the seal — THE MEADOW IS GOING HOME. IT WILL ARRIVE IN TIME. THIS IS KNOWN — and the wording is his, and the Registry keeps the original framed above the counter, and Mr. Pike, when he retired, which he did at the end of that summer, citing the unusual hours, gave as his reason, in the notice the office printed and the village read aloud at the inn, the only sentence of his whole career that said more than the ink did:

A REGISTRY CAN HOLD ANYTHING, IT TURNS OUT, IF IT LEARNS TO WRITE BIGGER.
PART FIVE

She found it walking.

It was after moonrise, on the common, and Tick gave one of his low clockwork growls, and Betony looked up from the sheep and saw the meadow going past, at about the speed of a thoughtful cow. The grass moved like the flank of an animal breathing. The three birch trees rocked very slightly, the way passengers rock on a boat. And the birdbath rode in the middle of everything, upright and serene, on what looked like a raft of roots, and the moon lay in the water of it, and a nightjar was having a drink.

Betony stood in its path, and the meadow stopped.

It did not stop the way a thing stops that has run into something. It stopped the way a person stops who has been called by name — all at once, and attentively. The grass around her boots stilled, and the bees, who work the night shift when the meadow is moving, hummed down to a listening note.

"Form Eleven," said Betony, feeling extremely silly, and she read it out, all the whereas's and herebys, to a meadow, in the moonlight, with a clockwork dog at her heel. And at the end of it the meadow did the most extraordinary thing it had ever done, which was to sit down — the whole of it, at once, in place, folding itself small the way a tired animal folds — and the birdbath rocked, and the water in it slopped over the chipped rim, and the nightjar flew up swearing in nightjar.

Tick, being a clockwork dog, went and stood under the birdbath, which was dripping, and caught what he could of the moonwater in his keyhole, which is not recommended for clockwork, and he did not care.

PART SIX

The meadow sat still for nine days, which is the Quadling way of thinking, and Betony came every evening with the sheep, and on the ninth evening she noticed what everyone had missed who had not spent nine evenings with it: the birdbath was reading.

It read the Form Eleven, which she had left tucked under its chipped rim, because she had not known where else a meadow would keep its correspondence. It read the front, which was all whereas's. And it read the back.

The back of Form Eleven is blank in most copies. But the Registry of the Quadling Country was thorough, and its founder had been a woman of unusual mercy, and so the back of every Form Eleven carried a paragraph, printed very small, that no one in the Registry had ever read out loud, and Mr. Pike had certainly never read it, or he would have served the paper differently. The paragraph said, in the founder's own words:

THIS NOTICE IS ALSO, IF THE RECIPIENT CHOOSES, AN INVITATION. THE REGISTRY WILL HOLD A HEARING TO DECIDE WHERE THE RECIPIENT IS GOING, AND A HEARING IN THIS COUNTRY IS A PARTY, BECAUSE WE COULD NOT BEAR TO MAKE IT ANYTHING ELSE. THE RECIPIENT MAY BRING THE MEADOW.

And the birdbath had read it, the only literate object in the meadow, and had been waiting nine days to see whether anyone would come and ask what it said.

Betony asked. The birdbath showed her the paragraph, by tipping its water so the moonlight lay along the lines of the small print, which is how birdbaths point, and Betony read it, and then she sat down in the grass, and the meadow sat around her, thinking its long slow thought, and Tick wound down beside her with his nose against the birdbath's stone foot, and the nightjar came back and said what it had to say about interruption.

PART SEVEN

The hearing was held in June, on the common, and it was, as promised, a party. The Registry brought the map, rolled up like a carpet, and unrolled it on trestles beside the strawberries, and Mr. Pike came with his best ink, prepared to argue, and there was no argument to be had, because the birdbath, who came on its root-raft, had a solution, and had been holding it all along.

You could see the answer in the clover, the birdbath indicated, if anyone had thought to look. The meadow's path, step by step, year by year, written in the way its flowers grew thickest, was not a path away at all. It was a spiral. A very wide one, walked so slowly that no one had ever noticed the curve, but a spiral still, with the birdbath at the outside edge riding along, and the center of the spiral, the meadow's whole destination, this whole time, was a dell on the southern slope where — the surveyors confirmed it, walking the ground with strings — the meadow had started, four hundred years ago, before Tickle Down was Tickle Down, and had been walking ever since, at the speed of a thoughtful cow, around and around, home.

Mr. Pike drew it that night, in his best ink, on the corner of the map: a wide red spiral, with the Registry's seal on it, and under the seal, one line: THE MEADOW IS GOING HOME. IT WILL ARRIVE IN TIME. THIS IS KNOWN.

And the Registry made one more mark, at Betony's request, by the birdbath: a small red circle, and inside it, in letters so small you have to know they are there, the words READ THE BACK.

The meadow, at the next full moon, took its step, unhurried, on schedule, home in four hundred more years at the speed it has chosen; and it has never since been served with papers, though it is invited to everything, and the birdbath reads its mail aloud now, to the bees, who tell the flowers, who do not care for news but are unfailingly polite about it.
