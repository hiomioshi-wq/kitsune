The org chart of Dreadco was drawn, by charter, by hand, once a year, on vellum, by the Undermanager, and hung in the lobby, and it was, everyone agreed, the most accurate document the firm produced, because the charter required the chart to be drawn not as the firm WAS, but as the firm FEARED it was, and no two years had ever matched, and the discrepancies were the firm's whole system of prophecy.

The boxes at the top never changed. THE CHAIRMAN, alone, in a box drawn with the kind of ink that gets up afterward. Below him, the divisions — CATCH, STAFF, SALES, and the fourth one, the one whose name nobody could read because the ink on its box changed daily, which was by design, and which everyone called, for payroll purposes, CONTINGENCIES.

The problem, that autumn, was that the boxes at the bottom of the chart had begun to go missing.

Not the people. The people were all present, at their desks, drinking their coffee in the sips of attention the coffee allowed. The BOXES were going — the ink ones, the names of the junior staff, the little entries at the bottom of the chart where the intake vessels went, and the vellum, on each successive redraw, simply had fewer of them, and the edges of the missing places were not smudged, which ruled out the usual weather, and were not crossed out, which ruled out the Chairman, who never crossed out, having built his entire theology on the difference between a deletion and a bill.

Undermanager Quill counted, on the third redraw, and came up four names short, and went to the files, and the files — which were her files, which she had personally strapped to the building's foundations for eleven years — contained, for each of the four, the same single entry, in a hand that was not hers:

TRANSFERRED. SEE CONTINGENCIES.

There was no transfer form. There is a form for everything at Dreadco, and Quill, who had spent eleven years becoming the building's memory of them, knew every form in the tower's possession, and there was no transfer form, because transfer, as a concept, had been abolished by the firm in 1938, on the sensible grounds that it generated hope.

She took it to the Catch Division, at the morning briefing, as a staffing matter, and the Division, which could smell a restructuring the way ordinary offices smell rain, went very quiet around their coffee, and looked at the fourth box at the top of the chart, whose ink said, this morning, something that had never been legible, ever, in the whole history of the vellum, and which was, this morning, perfectly, unmistakably clear.

It said: HIRING.

Basket put down his sandwiches. Mr. Lament, whose hair had been professionally blown back since the retreat, leaned forward with the expression of a man watching a rival fall off the brass plate. The fear of phone calls after midnight put down the phone.

"Something," said Quill, "is interviewing our juniors. Off the books. In the tower. And whatever it is, it has CONTINGENCIES' handwriting."

It was Colly Vetch — who else, by then; it is the whole function of a Colly in any organization, in any tower, in any vellum-drawn century, to be the one who goes and looks through the door that everyone else has professionally agreed is not on the floor plan — who found the interviews, that evening, because Colly did his rounds at night, in the cold, in the draft, door by door, and the tower had doors too, and he knew all of theirs, and one of them, on the ninth floor, which had been a supply closet since Quill's first year, was standing open.

Into dark hallways. The oldest fact of his industry, in the one building in Gloam that was supposed to be above it.

"this is a trap," said Draft, in the grass of him, lying very flat. "this is the most obvious trap in the history of the firm, and the firm once trapped itself in 1902 as a training exercise."

"I know," said Colly, delighted, and went in.

The ninth floor was hiring. The supply closet opened onto a corridor that the tower did not have, with an interview room at the end of it that the tower would have billed for, and in the room there was a chair, a desk, and a jar — an arrangement Colly recognized from the best day of his life — and behind the desk sat the interviewer.

It was not the grey arrangement. It was not the Chairman. It was, when it looked up, and it did look up, with a sound like a filing cabinet being opened in an empty house, a thing that Quill would file, in the end, under EXECUTIVE, FORMER, RETAINED? in handwriting that shook for the only recorded time — the department heads' department head. The first Undermanager. The one who, firm legend said, had invented the jar, and the intake, and the assessment, and the whole baseline grammar of the grey arrangements, and had then, in the year of the charter's writing, when the Chairman's grandfather was still bolting plates to lobby walls, gone into the basement of the industry's oldest question — what does the firm do with the fears nobody will ever be afraid of again? — and never come back up.

"I have been down here," said the first Undermanager, "since 1871. I have watched the wheel turn through the lobbies from below, through the elevator shafts, at the speed of a career. And I have kept the census that the firm does not keep. The census of the retired fears. The ones your plate cannot bill. The fear of the dark in a city with new streetlights. The fear of the deep in a city that drained its lake. The fear of open doors in a district that has, this quarter, thanks to a boy and his badge of cold, stopped being afraid of open doors." The filing-cabinet voice went through the interview room like a draft under a door. "Every fear you make brave, Mr. Vetch. Every one your rounds un-sell. Where do you suppose they go?"

"They go," said Draft, from Colly's teeth, very quietly, in the voice of a tenant who has just read his own name on a lease — "into a jar."

"They come to ME," said the first Undermanager. "I keep them. I feed them the one food that keeps a fear alive without its district, which is PURPOSE, which this firm stopped providing in 1938, when it abolished transfer, and I have been hiring them back — off your plates, out of your files, box by box, vellum by vellum, into the only department that has never once lied on an org chart. The census. CONTINGENCIES. The fear of what this firm becomes when it has nothing left to sell, which is, Mr. Vetch, and you may check the ink, the one fear this firm has never dared to staff."

The room held the weather of it. The jar on the desk held something small and old that had been the fear of the deep in a drained city, paddling, retired, retained.

And Colly Vetch — who had by then stood in the Chairman's purple and been measured at zero and kept on, and who had exactly one professional competency, which the whole tower could recite — looked at the interviewer, and at the jar, and did the thing that would, in the fullness of the vellum, turn out to be the single most consequential act of policy in the modern history of the firm, and the least profitable, and the most.

He asked the interviewer a question that nobody, in a hundred and fifty-one years of the tower's interviews, on either side of any of its desks, had ever asked.

"What are YOU for?" said Colly.

The filing-cabinet voice stopped.

The corridor that the tower did not have stood open into the dark, and in the dark, somewhere below the lobby, below the foundations, below the brass plate and the plate's whole religion, the census held its breath, and the first Undermanager looked at the boy standing in its doorway — the only interviewee it had ever had who had come in through the OPEN side — and the answer, when it came, was one word, and it was the truth, and it was, for the first time in the firm's records, filed voluntarily:

"...keeping," it said. "i am for keeping."

Undermanager Quill's report, the following Monday, ran to four pages and one recommendation, and the Chairman read it standing, before the window, in the purple, for the length of a whole weather system, and the vellum was redrawn that year with a fifth division at the bottom of the chart, below the boxes, in ink that everyone could read, in a hand that was not the first Undermanager's and was not Quill's and was never, despite eleven years of requests, identified:

KEEPING. SEE ALSO: THE OPEN DOOR POLICY.

The four names came back. The census kept its basement, and its jars, and its purpose, and the transfer form was reintroduced for the first time since 1938, printed on vellum, in triplicate, generating, as everyone had warned, hope — measured, on the plate, at its first nonzero quarter in a century and a half, and billed, by the Chairman's own pen, at a loss, on purpose, which the firm called, after that year, a vetch.

Colly's door on the ninth floor stayed open. It had a jar beside it now, and a bell, and the department's retired fears came up it, on Tuesdays, to be staffed with the only purpose the tower had ever found that fed them without a scream: the census of what the firm would not look at, and the minding of it.
