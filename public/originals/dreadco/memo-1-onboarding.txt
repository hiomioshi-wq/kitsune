The city of Gloam kept its monsters the way other cities keep pigeons: as a fact of civic life, dealt with by a department, budgeted for annually, and complained about chiefly when the budget was cut.

The department was Dreadco.

Dreadco occupied a tower at the unfashionable end of the skyline, black as a burnt match, with a lobby you could smell from the street — a smell like a lightning strike deciding to be permanent — and a sign over the revolving doors that said, in letters of excellent provenance:

DREADCO.
A NECESSARY EVIL SINCE 1871.
NO APPOINTMENTS. APPOINTMENTS ARE, THEMSELVES, A KIND OF FEAR.

The towers of Gloam had grown up around the old understanding, which ran as follows: fears are born from people (they are, in the strictest sense, a crop), and fears, once born, need managing, because an unmanaged fear gets ambitious, and an ambitious fear eats a neighborhood. And so the city had long ago done the cynical, brilliant thing it does with every unpleasant necessity, which was to give the necessity a company car and an org chart and somebody to blame, and Dreadco had been the somebody ever since, in the way that the mugger and the insurance industry are, historically speaking, the same firm with two letterheads.

Dreadco did three things. It caught fears. It staffed fears. And it sold protection from the fears it caught, staffed, and occasionally lost, which completed a circle so perfect that the Chairman kept a diagram of it in his office and called it The Wheel, and laughed at it, alone, which was the sort of thing the Chairman did, and one of the gentler ones.

Into this firm, on a wet Tuesday, came a young man named Colly Vetch.

Colly was twenty-two, entirely unqualified, and in possession of the two things Dreadco's recruiters were trained to pounce on: he was broke, and he was desperate, in roughly that order, with a third thing — a kind of relentless, unkillable good cheer — that the recruiters had no metric for and would spend the next year failing to file.

The interview was on the eleventh floor. There was a chair, a desk, and a jar, and the jar was the interview.

"Fear assessment," said the interviewer, who was a person-shaped arrangement of grey suit with a voice like a filing cabinet apologizing. "Standard intake. State your name and place your hand on the jar."

"Is it going to hurt?" said Colly, placing his hand on the jar, which was a promising instinct, since the answer to that question at Dreadco, for every question, at every level of the org chart, up to and including the Chairman's own private washroom, was yes.

The jar contained a fear. It was small, as fears went, and it had been in the jar for a long time, and it was, on the day of the interview, extremely bored, which is the single most dangerous condition a fear can be in, more dangerous than hunger, more dangerous than rage, because a bored fear will attach itself to anything just to have somewhere to be.

The fear in the jar was the fear of open doors at night.

Not the fear of what comes through them. That was a different department, a senior fear, with its own parking. This was the simple, ancient, animal one: the door is open, the room is dark, the hallway beyond is darker, and some part of you that predates language is lying flat in the grass of your mind going still, still, still. Everybody has it. It is one of Gloam's most reliable crops. It came in standard, it shipped in jars, and the eleventh floor had eleven thousand of them, and the jar that Colly Vetch put his hand on had been listening to interviews for six years and had formed opinions.

Colly's hand went on the jar. The jar went warm. The interviewer looked at a gauge, and the gauge did something gauges do not do, and the fear of open doors at night, bored beyond endurance by six years of hands and interview questions, did the one thing no intake fear is ever supposed to do, which is make a decision.

It went up his arm.

There was a moment — everyone on the eleventh floor agreed about the moment, and the moment became the first entry in Colly Vetch's personnel file, under INCIDENTS, INGRAFTING, ACCIDENTAL — when the young man stood up very straight with his eyes gone completely dark and said, in a voice with a note in it like a door swinging wide in an empty house:

"oh," it said. "oh, this is much better than the jar."

And then the eyes came back, and they were Colly's, and Colly said, "Sorry — sorry — did I say something?" because he had, and it had not been him, and the interviewer wrote one word on the intake form and underlined it twice, and the word was RETAIN.

This is the part of the story where a different firm would have called an exorcist. Dreadco called HR.

"You're hired," said the grey-suited arrangement, "in the capacity for which you are now, technically speaking, qualified, which is: vessel. Welcome to Dreadco. Your fear will be along shortly. It is, at present, acquainting itself with your left arm."

And that was the onboarding. There was paperwork — there is always paperwork at Dreadco, the firm having discovered in 1874 that forms are the most reliably fear-generating artifact ever devised by man, and the firm having been living off the discovery ever since — and the paperwork had a line for everything: next of kin (none), blood type (ask the arm), and, at the bottom, in the small print that nobody read and everybody at Dreadco knew by heart, the sentence that would define the next six memos of Colly Vetch's life:

THE EMPLOYEE IS EXPECTED TO MAINTAIN THEIR FEAR IN GOOD WORKING ORDER. THE FEAR IS EXPECTED TO MAINTAIN THE EMPLOYEE. DREADCO ACCEPTS NO LIABILITY FOR DISAGREEMENTS BETWEEN THE TWO PARTIES, WHO ARE HEREBY REferred TO, FOR ALL PAYROLL PURPOSES, AS ONE (1) EMPLOYEE.

The elevator down was long, and Colly rode it with one ear turned toward the inside of his own head, where something new was settling in with the proprietary calm of a tenant who has read the lease and found it favorable.

"Hello?" said Colly, aloud, to himself.

"hello," said himself, from the inside, with a note in it like a hallway at night, like a draft under a door, like six years of a jar, and then, with the tone of somebody deciding, on balance, to be civil to the new landlord: "you may call me draft. i have decided we are going to get along."

Colly Vetch, twenty-two, broke, cheerful, and as of that elevator the sole registered employee of a two-party corporation, grinned at his own reflection in the doors.

"Excellent," he said. "I've never gotten along with anybody for free before."

The doors opened on the lobby, and the lightning-strike smell, and the revolving doors, and the street, and Gloam beyond it, all wet and lit and open, every door in the city, waiting.
