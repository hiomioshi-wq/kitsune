PAGE ZERO

A Kitsune Original. Text released under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0.


PART ONE

Haru had drawn a page a day for six years, and on the first morning of the seventh year, the page drew back.

It was not dramatic. He sat down at the desk by the window at six, as always, with his coffee going cold in the mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST UNCLE, and he taped down a fresh sheet of Bristol board, and he picked up his pencil to start the first panel of chapter two hundred and eleven of a manga that four thousand people read every week and that had never once paid his rent. And in the top left corner of the blank page there was already a line.

A small, careful line, about the length of his thumb. Slightly curved. As if someone had begun to draw an eyebrow and stopped.

He had not drawn it. He was sure. He looked at the pencil in his hand, at the sheet, at the window. The cat was asleep on the radiator. The cat could not draw.

He erased the line. He began his first panel. By lunchtime he had forgotten.


PART TWO

The next morning there were two lines.

The morning after that, there was an eye.

It was a good eye. That was the thing that bothered him most, later. It was not his style; his style was loose and quick and a little ugly, which his readers said was the charm of it. This eye was patient. Every lash was there. It looked up and to the left, toward the corner of the page where, if this were one of his own layouts, the next panel would begin.

He did not erase it. He drew his page around it, working the eye into a crowd scene, a face in the background of a festival. Nobody commented. Four thousand people read it and not one of them noticed that there was, in the corner of page two hundred and thirteen, an eye that did not belong to him.

On the fourth day there was a face.

On the fifth day there was a fox.


PART THREE

She took up the whole first panel: a young fox in a long red scarf, standing on a mountain road at night, holding up a paper lantern on a pole. She looked out of the page at him with that same patient eye. Behind her, very faintly, someone had sketched the outline of a lighthouse, leaning slightly to the left.

Under the panel, in tidy lettering that was not his, it said: Page zero.

Haru put his coffee down very carefully.

"I don't do fantasy," he said out loud. The cat opened one eye. "I do a slice-of-life comedy about a man who runs a failing bicycle shop. There are no foxes in it. There have never been foxes in it."

He went for a walk. He bought a new pencil he did not need. When he came back, the second panel was drawn. The fox was walking now, the lantern swinging, and the road ran out of the panel's border and down the page, into the white space where he was supposed to be putting his bicycle shop.


PART FOUR

He could have thrown the page away. He thought about it, all that afternoon, sitting at the desk not drawing. He had a deadline. He had four thousand readers who wanted to know whether the bicycle shop would survive the opening of the new superstore across the road. He had six years of pages and a rule, the only rule that had kept him going: a page a day, his page, no matter what.

But he looked at the fox, and he thought: somebody else has a rule too.

So that night he picked up his pencil and, very carefully, at the bottom of the page, where the fox's road ran out of white space, he drew a bicycle shop. His bicycle shop, loose and quick and a little ugly, with its hand-painted sign and the broken bell over the door. And standing in the doorway, his main character, the tired man with the moustache, looking up the road as if he had heard something.

He went to bed.

In the morning the fox had reached the door.


PART FIVE

Chapter two hundred and eleven of the bicycle shop manga was the strangest chapter he had ever published. Half of it was not in his style. A mountain courier arrived at a failing shop with a letter that had no name on it. The man with the moustache read it, and laughed, and wiped his eyes. Nobody in the comments could agree on what had happened, and for the first time in six years there were more than four thousand of them.

The fox stayed three chapters. Then one morning Haru sat down at six and taped down a fresh sheet, and the page was blank, and stayed blank, and he understood that she had other letters to deliver.

He still draws a page a day. But now, before he starts, he leaves the top left corner empty for a minute or two, just in case. And sometimes, not often, when he looks back, there is a small careful line there, about the length of his thumb, slightly curved, as if someone has begun to say hello.
