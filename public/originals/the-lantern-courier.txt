THE LANTERN COURIER

A Kitsune Original. Text released under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0.


PART ONE

In the mountain towns, letters travel at night.

Nobody remembers who decided this. The postmaster at Hollow Pine says it was a matter of cost: the day roads belong to the ox carts and the tea merchants, and a courier who walks them must pay a toll at every bridge. The night roads belong to no one. The old women in the bathhouse say it is because words written by daylight need the dark to settle, the way tea leaves need still water. Kitsu, who carries the letters, has never had an opinion about it. She was born in the dark and she prefers it.

She is a fox, and young for a courier, with a red scarf her grandmother knitted too long and a satchel that is older than both of them. On the satchel's flap someone long ago stitched the words ALL LETTERS ARRIVE, and the thread has faded to the colour of dry grass. Kitsu carries a paper lantern on a pole across her shoulders. The lantern does not light the road. It lights the addresses.

That is the first thing they teach you, when you are a courier in the mountain towns. A letter is not delivered to a place. It is delivered to a person, and people move. They marry into other valleys. They take work at the charcoal kilns. They grow old and go to live with a daughter on the coast. So the couriers carry a lantern made from the paper of a returned letter, and when you hold an envelope up to its light, the ink of the address goes faint where the person used to be and dark where the person is now. You follow the dark ink. It is not difficult. It only takes walking.

On the night this story begins, Kitsu had forty-one letters in her satchel, and forty of them behaved.

The forty-first had no address at all.


PART TWO

She found it at the bottom of the satchel when she reached the shrine at Seven Bends, which was where she always stopped to eat her rice ball and look at the stars. The envelope was heavy, cream-coloured, the kind of paper the stationer in Hollow Pine sells only in spring. It was sealed with plain wax. On the front, where the name should be, there was nothing.

Kitsu turned it over. Nothing on the back either, except a small drawing in the corner, done in brown ink: a lighthouse.

There are no lighthouses in the mountains. Everybody knows that.

She held the envelope up to the lantern. Usually the light made the ink swim and settle; this time the paper simply glowed, and in the glow she saw, very faintly, a line. Not writing. A line, like a thread pulled through the paper, running from the lighthouse in the corner off the edge of the envelope and pointing east, down the mountain, toward the sea.

"That is a long way," Kitsu told the envelope.

The envelope did not argue.

She could have taken it back to Hollow Pine. There is a shelf in the post office for letters that cannot be delivered, and once a year the postmaster burns them in a small brass stove and says a few words. It is a respectful end. Nobody would have blamed her.

But the satchel said ALL LETTERS ARRIVE, and Kitsu had carried it for three winters, and she had never once put a letter on that shelf.

She finished her rice ball. She delivered the other forty letters before dawn, very quickly, slipping each one under its proper door. Then she slept through the day in the hollow of a cedar, and when the sun went down she picked up her lantern and walked east.


PART THREE

It took her six nights to reach the coast.

On the second night, a heron who ran a ferry across the Kagami River asked what was so urgent that a mountain courier would come so far downstream. Kitsu showed him the envelope.

"No name," said the heron. "Then how do you know it isn't for me?"

She held it up to the lantern for him. The thread of light pointed past him, past the river, east.

"Ah," said the heron, a little disappointed. He did not charge her for the crossing.

On the fourth night it rained, and she shared a rice-straw shelter with an old badger who sold umbrellas, and who told her that in his experience letters without names were usually apologies. "People can't bring themselves to write the name," he said, "because then it is real. So they write everything else and leave the front empty and hope the post will be clever."

"The post is not clever," said Kitsu. "The post is just stubborn."

"Same thing, at the end of a long road," said the badger.

On the sixth night she smelled salt for the first time in her life, and she came over a ridge and saw the sea lying under the moon like a sheet of hammered tin, and on a black rock at the end of a long spit of sand, a lighthouse.

The thread in the envelope pulled so hard she nearly dropped it.


PART FOUR

The lighthouse keeper was a tall old crane named Oyuki, with grey feathers and a cardigan buttoned wrong. She opened the door before Kitsu could knock.

"You're not the usual post," she said.

"No," Kitsu said. "I'm from the mountains." She held out the envelope.

Oyuki did not take it at first. She looked at the drawing in the corner for a long time, and then she said, very quietly, "My brother drew lighthouses like that. Always leaning a little to the left. He said it was the wind."

She let Kitsu in. The kitchen was warm and smelled of kelp broth. Above the stove hung a dozen drawings of lighthouses, all leaning a little to the left.

"We argued," Oyuki said, "forty years ago. About the lamp. Our father left the light to both of us, and I said one keeper was enough, and he said fine, then, and went up into the mountains to be a stationer. I never wrote. He never wrote. I thought he must have died. I suppose he must have, now."

"The paper is from Hollow Pine," Kitsu said. "From the stationer's. The one in spring."

Oyuki broke the seal.

Kitsu did not read the letter. Couriers do not. But she saw that it was short, and that the old crane read it three times, and that on the third time she laughed, a little cracked bark of a laugh, and wiped her eyes on the sleeve of her wrong-buttoned cardigan.

"He says the light was always mine," Oyuki said. "He says he only wanted to be asked." She folded the letter very carefully. "Forty years. He couldn't write my name on the front because he couldn't decide whether to write sister or keeper."

"The post is stubborn," Kitsu said. "It worked out the rest."


PART FIVE

She stayed one day at the lighthouse, sleeping in a coil of rope while Oyuki polished the great lens, and when evening came the old crane gave her a parcel wrapped in oilcloth.

"For the stationer at Hollow Pine," she said. "If he is still there. If he isn't, for whoever sells the spring paper now. Tell them a lighthouse keeper would like to buy some."

On the front of the parcel, in large, careful letters, she had written a name.

Kitsu put it in her satchel, under the flap that says ALL LETTERS ARRIVE, and lit her lantern, and walked west into the dark, toward the mountains, where the letters travel at night.
