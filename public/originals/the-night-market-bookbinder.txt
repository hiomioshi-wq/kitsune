THE NIGHT MARKET BOOKBINDER

A Kitsune Original. Text released under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0.


1. The Stall Under the Bridge

The night market in Low Fennel opens when the last tram goes home, and it closes when the first gull complains about the light. In between, it sells everything a town forgets it needs: lamp wicks, mended kettles, buttons in jars sorted by mood, and, under the stone bridge where the river makes its quietest sound, books.

The bookbinder's name was Pell. She was a hedgehog, which meant two things. She was very good with needles, and nobody ever tried to steal from her twice.

Her stall was a table, a stool, a lantern with a green shade, and a sign painted on the back of an old breadboard:

BOOKS MENDED.
SPINES STRAIGHTENED.
NO QUESTIONS ASKED ABOUT WHAT YOU SPILLED ON THEM.

People came with books that had been dropped in baths and books that had been read so many times the pages had gone soft as cloth. A badger brought a cookbook with a whole chapter glued shut by jam. A heron brought a dictionary that had lost the letter Q, which he said he had never needed but missed all the same.

Pell mended them all. She worked with linen thread waxed against the damp, with paste she cooked herself from rice flour, and with a bone folder that had belonged to her grandmother and was the colour of old tea.

And when she finished each book, before she handed it back, she held it shut between her paws for a moment and listened.

She never told anyone why.


2. What Books Remember

The truth was simple and a little strange: books remember their readers.

Not the words. The words are the book's own. What a book remembers is the weight of hands, the warmth of a lap, the angle of a lamp, the place where someone stopped one evening and never came back to. Pell had heard these memories since she was small, as a faint rustle, like a page turning in another room.

A book that had been loved hummed. A book that had been forgotten on a shelf for thirty years made almost no sound at all, only a dry little tick, like a clock somebody had stopped winding.

So when a young fox came to the stall one night with a book wrapped in a scarf, and set it on the table, and Pell heard nothing from it at all, she put down her needle.

"That's not a book that's been forgotten," she said. "That's a book that's been waiting."

The fox looked up. Her ears were the colour of embers. She wore a courier's satchel, the kind with the brass buckle stamped with a little lantern, and she had the tired look of someone who walked for a living.

"How can you tell?" she asked.

"Forgotten books tick," said Pell. "Waiting books hold their breath."


3. The Book in the Scarf

The fox's name was Kitsu. She delivered letters in the mountain towns, she said, and sometimes things that were not letters, and this was one of those.

"It came to the post office with no address," she said. "Only a note: For whoever can finish it."

Pell unwrapped the scarf. Inside was a small book bound in faded blue cloth. The corners were worn round. The title on the spine had rubbed away to a silver ghost.

She opened it. The first page was handwritten in a careful, slanted hand:

This is the story of the year the river froze, and what we found under the ice.

The next pages told the story. A village, a winter, two children skating further than they were allowed. The handwriting grew quicker as it went, as if the writer had been hurrying to keep up with their own memory. Then, a third of the way through, it stopped. The rest of the pages were blank.

"Someone stopped writing," said Kitsu.

"Someone stopped," Pell agreed. She turned the book over. The spine was cracked at exactly the last written page, cracked deep, the way a spine cracks when a book is left open and face-down for a very long time.

"It's not torn," she said slowly. "It's not damaged. There's nothing here for me to mend."

Kitsu's ears drooped. "Then I've walked all the way down from Hollow Pine for nothing."

"I didn't say that."


4. Listening Harder

Pell held the book shut between her paws, and closed her eyes, and listened harder than she had listened to anything in years.

At first: nothing. The held breath.

Then, under it, so faint she had to lean her whole small body over the table: the scratch of a pen. A lamp. A kitchen that smelled of oranges and woodsmoke. A voice humming a song with no words. The pen stopping. A chair scraping back. A door.

And then the long, long quiet of a book left open on a table, waiting for the person who had stood up to come back and sit down again.

Pell opened her eyes. Her nose was wet.

"They meant to come back," she said. "Whoever wrote this. They stood up to answer the door, and they meant to come back."

Kitsu was quiet for a while. The river under the bridge went on making its quietest sound.

"Can you tell who it was?" she asked.

"Books don't remember names. They remember hands." Pell turned the book so the lantern caught the cloth. "Left-handed. See how the ink is smudged along the bottom of each line? And a baker, I think. There's flour in the binding."

"There's only one left-handed baker in the mountain towns who'd write a book," said Kitsu, standing up so fast the stool fell over. "Old Mrs Tamsin, in Fold. But she stopped baking years ago. She stopped everything years ago."


5. The Road to Fold

Pell did not, as a rule, leave her stall. The night market was where people knew to find her, and a bookbinder who wanders is a bookbinder whose customers wander off too.

But she packed her bone folder, a spool of linen thread, a pot of paste, and the blue book, and she put a second sign on the breadboard that said BACK BY THE NEXT NEW MOON, and she climbed into the cart that Kitsu borrowed from the miller.

It took two nights to reach Fold. The village sat in a high valley beside a river that, even in late autumn, ran fast and cold and loud.

Mrs Tamsin's bakery had its shutters closed. The sign over the door had faded to a pale suggestion of a loaf. But there was a light in the back window, and when Kitsu knocked, a voice called, "It's open. It's always open. Nobody ever comes."

The kitchen smelled, very faintly, of oranges.

Mrs Tamsin was a stoat, very old and very small, sitting in a chair by a stove with a blanket over her knees. She looked at Kitsu's satchel, and then at the hedgehog behind her, and then at the blue book in Pell's paws, and every part of her went still.

"Where did you find that?"


6. The Year the River Froze

It came out slowly, the way true things do.

She had been writing it for her brother, Mrs Tamsin said. The story of the winter they were children, the year the river froze for the only time anyone could remember, and they skated all the way to the bend where the old mill wheel was trapped in the ice, and saw, under the ice, something neither of them could ever agree on afterwards.

"He said it was a lantern," she said. "Lit. Under the ice. I said it was the moon's reflection. We argued about it for sixty years."

She had been writing it down so that at least the argument would last. Then one evening, a third of the way through, there had been a knock at the door. A letter. Her brother had been taken ill, far away, over the pass.

She had stood up to answer the door, and gone, and by the time she came home it was spring and he was gone and she could not bear to sit back down at that table. So she had given the book to the post office with no address and a note, hoping that someone, somewhere, could finish what she couldn't.

"But nobody else knows what happened," said Kitsu gently. "Only you."

Mrs Tamsin looked at the book for a long time.

"I suppose that's true," she said. "I suppose it was always going to have to be me."


7. Mending

Pell did not mend the book that night. There was nothing broken in it.

Instead she sat at the kitchen table, in the chair that had been pushed back sixty years ago, and she re-sewed the cracked spine so that the book would lie open flat at the blank page without fighting. She cut fresh endpapers from a sheet of good cream paper she had brought for the purpose. She worked slowly, so that there would be time.

Kitsu made tea. Mrs Tamsin watched.

Somewhere around midnight, the old stoat got up out of her chair by the stove and came and sat down at the table, and took the pen that Pell had laid beside the open book, and held it in her left paw.

She did not write anything for a long while.

Then she wrote: The thing under the ice was a lantern. My brother was right. I always knew he was right. I just liked arguing with him.

And then she kept going.


8. Waiting Books

Pell went back to the night market at the next new moon. Her customers had not wandered off after all. The heron was waiting with the dictionary, which had somehow lost the letter Z as well.

She mended it. She mended everything.

And now, sometimes, when someone brings her a book that makes no sound at all, that holds its breath under her paws, she does not hand it back right away. She asks, very gently, whether they know who stopped reading it. Or writing it. Or where the door was that they stood up to answer.

Most people don't. But some do. And a few, when they hear the question, sit down very slowly on her stool under the bridge, by the river's quietest sound, and tell her.

The blue book lives on a shelf in a bakery in Fold now. The bakery is open again, a little, on Sundays. Mrs Tamsin bakes orange loaves, and if you ask nicely, she will let you read the story of the year the river froze.

It has an ending. And the book, Pell says, hums.


THE END
