A FIELD GUIDE TO EXTINCT COLORS

A Kitsune Original. Text released under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0.


PART ONE

The Colour Register at the Marchmont Institute is kept in a room with no windows, because sunlight is a liar. It has been kept there since 1841, in a cabinet of drawers, and each drawer holds a single color on a single card, and each card has a name, a description, the date of its last confirmed sighting, and the names of two witnesses.

There are one thousand and forty-two colors in the Register. Of those, three hundred and eleven are extinct. That is not a sad number, my grandmother used to say. That is a number of things the world was kind enough to show us before they went.

My grandmother was Etta Sorrel, Keeper of the Register for forty-four years. She was seventy-nine the summer I came to stay with her, and she had hair the color of the inside of a chestnut, which is to say the color the Register calls Sorrel, which is to say the color she was named for. She had a way of holding a card up to the lamp and squinting at it that made her look like a jeweler, and a way of saying "Ah" when a color was exactly right that made you feel you had personally done something clever.

I was nine. My mother was working on a fire-recovery team in another province and could not take me, so I was sent to the Institute for the summer with a suitcase and a book about whales. I had been told my grandmother was a scientist. I had not been told what kind.

The first thing she did was sit me down at the long table under the cabinet and put a card in front of me.

"What color is that?" she said.

I looked. It was blue. Not sky blue, not sea blue, not any blue I had a word for. It was the blue of the inside of a shadow on snow, if the shadow were warm. It made the back of my eyes feel clean.

"Blue," I said.

"Which blue?"

"Just blue."

She made a small disappointed noise and took the card away. "That's Bellwether Blue," she said. "Named for a sheepdog. Last confirmed sighting 1968, in the eyes of the dog itself, by two shepherds who agreed on it to the end of their lives. The dog is dead. The breed is gone. The color is extinct. If you had said 'the blue of a dog's eyes in snow,' I would have given you a point."

I did not know there were points. I sat up straighter.

"Can a color come back?" I asked.

"Sometimes." She put the card back in its drawer. "A color is not like a species. It doesn't need a body. It needs a witness who remembers it accurately enough to point at it again. If two people can see it, it comes back. That's why the Register exists. We are not collectors. We are witnesses."

She showed me the rules. A color may be entered only if it has been seen by two people, separately, and their descriptions agree. A color may be re-entered if it has gone extinct and two people see it again. A color may be marked *fading* if only one witness remains, and *gone* if there are none. The cards are stored in the dark. The only lamp allowed is a low, warm one, because the wrong light will change a color the way the wrong word will change a memory.

I loved it immediately. It was the most serious thing I had ever been allowed to do.

That summer we entered a color called Harbor Fog, which is the gray of the sea when it has come up onto the land and forgotten why. We entered a color called Tin Roof After Rain, which my grandmother said had been extinct since 1974 and which I found, on a walk, on the roof of the boathouse. We entered a color called Grandmother's Kitchen, which was not one color but a way of describing six, and which the Register rejected on the grounds that a color must be one thing. My grandmother argued with the Register for three days and then entered it as a *palette*, which is allowed, and which she said was a loophole she had been saving for forty years.

I did not know then that she was teaching me because she was going blind.

She told me in July, on the bench outside the Institute, with a cup of tea in her hands and the evening coming down.

"There's a thing happening in my eyes," she said. "The center is going. It'll take a year, maybe two. I can still see the edges. I can still see the lamp and the cards if I hold them to the side. But the middle is going, and the middle is where the colors are."

I did not say anything. I was nine, and I thought that if I did not say anything, it might not be true.

"The Register needs a Keeper who can see," she said. "I've been putting off finding one. It turns out I was waiting for you."

"I'm nine."

"You're nine," she agreed. "That's old enough to see. It's old enough to be a witness. It's not old enough to sign the cards alone, but it's old enough to be the second pair of eyes." She looked at me sideways, which is how she looked at everything now. "Do you want the job?"

I said yes so fast that she laughed.

PART TWO

The work changed after that. In the mornings we did the cards: my grandmother held each one up to the lamp and squinted at the edges, and I told her what I saw in the middle. In the afternoons we went out with a notebook and a pencil and looked for colors that were fading or gone.

The Register's most wanted list was pinned to the wall. There was Glacier Milk, lost when the Voss Glacier melted in 1961. There was Passenger Rose, the color of the breast of a passenger pigeon, last seen 1914. There was Lamp Yellow, the color of a whale-oil lighthouse lamp before the electric conversion in 1936. And there was Kettle Black, which was my grandmother's own entry, made when she was seven, on the card that started everything.

*Kettle Black. The black of the iron kettle in my mother's kitchen, under the window, in the morning. Last confirmed sighting: 1954. Witnesses: Etta Sorrel; Margaret Sorrel.*

Margaret Sorrel was my great-grandmother. The kettle had been lost when the house burned in 1962. The Register had marked the color gone in 1979, when Margaret died and Etta was the only witness left, and gone again in 1991, when Etta decided she could no longer trust her own memory of it.

"It's a foolish thing to want back," she said, when I asked why she kept the card at the top of the list. "It's a black. There are a thousand blacks. But it's the first one I ever named, and it was my mother's, and I would like to see it once more before the middle goes."

So we went looking for Kettle Black.

We started at the Institute's storeroom, where the household objects from the burned house had been kept: a clock with no hands, a pair of scissors, a tin of buttons, and a kettle. The kettle was black. It sat on a shelf under a sheet of plastic, and when I lifted the plastic and held the lamp to it, the black came up out of the iron like something alive.

"Is that it?" I said.

My grandmother tilted her head. She could see the edges of it, the shine along the spout, the curve of the handle. "It's close," she said. "But it's not the color. The color was the black under the window, in the morning, with the light coming through the steam. This is storeroom black. It's been in the dark too long."

We tried other blacks. We tried the black of the stove, and the black of the coal scuttle, and the black of a raven's wing. Each time she said no, and each time she explained why, and each time I wrote the reason down, because that was the work: not just to find the color but to say what it was not.

On the third day we were caught in a rainstorm on the way back from the boathouse. We stood under the eaves of the boat shed and watched the rain come across the harbor in gray sheets. A crow landed on the fence post in front of us, and shook itself, and its feathers went from matte to shining and back again as the water moved through them.

My grandmother was looking at the edges of it. I was looking at the middle.

"That's it," I said. "That's the black. Not the kettle. The black of the crow when it's wet, right before it shakes."

She was quiet for a long time. The rain went on. The crow looked at us with its head on one side and then flew away.

"Say it again," she said. "Describe it."

I described it. I said it was a black with blue in it and a little green, and that it wasn't flat, and that the light did not sit on top of it but went into it and came back changed. I said it was the black of a thing that was about to move.

When I was done, my grandmother took out a pencil and wrote it down on the back of an admission ticket, in the careful hand she used for the Register.

"That's not Kettle Black," she said. "That's a new one. We'll need a name."

We called it Rain Crow. We made a card that afternoon. My grandmother signed as first witness, because she had seen a crow shake itself dry on a fence post when she was a girl, in a different rain, and had never given the black a name. I signed as second witness, because I had seen it that day. The Register accepted it. The drawer for R smells now of wet feathers.

She was very pleased. She said it was the best day she'd had in years.

Then she sat down heavily on the bench outside the Institute and said, "Wren, I need you to do something for me, and I need you to do it properly, because it is the last thing I will ask you."

PART THREE

The last thing she asked me was to give her back her own color.

Sorrel had been in the Register since 1841, under a different name, and had been re-entered twice: once in 1902, when the second witness died and a new one was found, and once in 1954, when the color was confirmed on the head of a girl named Etta who was standing in a kitchen with her mother's kettle. My grandmother had been the second witness to her own name-color when she was seven. She had never expected to be the one who needed re-entering.

"The middle is going faster than I thought," she said. "I can't see it anymore. I can see the shape of my hands and the edge of the lamp, and the cards are just white. I need you to look at Sorrel and describe it back to me. If I can recognize it from your description, that's a witness. That's how the Register works. The second witness doesn't have to see it with their eyes. They have to see it with their memory."

So I took the card out of the drawer. It was a small square of silk, faded at the edges, the color of the inside of a chestnut, of a fox in late light, of tea when the sun goes through it.

My grandmother sat across the table with her hands folded. Her hair was white now, but the color was still there in her memory, and my job was to find it.

I described it for an hour. I described it as the color of a chestnut shell split open in October. I described it as the color of the light on the bricks of the Institute at five o'clock in summer. I described it as the color of the tea in her cup when she held it up to the lamp. I described it as the color of the inside of a fox's ear, if the fox were asleep.

At each description she said no, or close, or a little more red. She was not being difficult. She was being exact. It was the most exact I had ever seen anyone be about anything.

Then I said, "It's the color of a voice saying your name in a kitchen, when you were small, and the kettle was on."

She was quiet for a long moment.

"Yes," she said. "That's it. That's the one."

We re-entered Sorrel that afternoon. My grandmother signed as first witness, by memory. I signed as second witness, by sight. The date of last confirmed sighting is the day I described it to her, which is the same as the day she heard it, which is the same as the day it came back.

She died the following spring. She left me the keys to the cabinet and the low warm lamp and a note that said: *The Register is not about the past. It is about paying attention. Colors do not go extinct because the light goes. They go extinct because nobody looks. Look, Wren. That is the whole job.*

I am the Keeper now. I am much older than nine. The middle of my eyes is still good, and I still keep the cards in the dark, and I still hold them up to the lamp and squint, and the work is exactly what she said it was.

There is one new card in the Register that I made myself, on the day of her funeral, and it is the only card with a color I could not see with my eyes and could not describe with a swatch. It is filed under B, for Being Looked At. The description says: *Warm. A little like the inside of a shell. Mostly like being known.* The witnesses are Etta Sorrel and Wren Sorrel. My grandmother signed it before she died, blind, on the strength of my description, because she said she recognized it.

The Register accepted it. It is not extinct. It is the only color I know that cannot go gone, as long as somebody keeps looking at somebody else.

That is what my grandmother taught me, and it is the whole of the field guide, and it is why I keep the lamp low.
