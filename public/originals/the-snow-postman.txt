THE SNOW POSTMAN

A Kitsune Original. Text released under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0.


ONE

High in the mountains, above the last village, above the last tree, above the place where the road gives up and becomes a goat path and the goat path gives up and becomes a rumour, there was a post office.

It was a stone hut with a chimney and a red box nailed to the door. The postman was a stoat called Fennick, and in winter his coat turned white, so that when he walked out across the snowfields with his satchel he vanished completely, and all anybody saw was a small brown satchel bobbing along by itself across the mountain, delivering letters.

There were only nine houses on Fennick's round. They were spread across forty miles of rock and ice, and it took him a week to visit them all, and then he turned around and walked home and started again.

He did not mind. He liked the cold. He liked the quiet. He liked the way the snow squeaked under his paws in the early morning and the way the stars came down so close at night that he felt he could post a letter to one.


TWO

The ninth house was the furthest. It belonged to an old ibex named Mrs. Orla, who lived alone at the very top of the pass in a cottage built into the cliff like a swallow's nest.

Mrs. Orla received one letter every week. It came in a pale blue envelope, always the same, addressed in tidy, slanting writing. It came from somewhere far away, from a city by a warm sea; the stamps had palm trees on them.

And every week, when Fennick knocked and handed it over, Mrs. Orla held it to her nose and smiled, and said, "From my son," and gave Fennick a cup of hot goat's milk with honey, and they sat by her fire for exactly as long as it took to drink it.

She never opened the letters in front of him. That was fine. Some things are private.

Then, one January, the blue letters stopped.


THREE

The first week, Fennick told her it must be the weather. The trains were slow. The ships were late.

The second week, he said the same, less certainly.

The third week, he did not know what to say. He stood on her doorstep with his satchel full of nothing for her and his ears flat against his head, and Mrs. Orla looked at him for a long time, and then she said, very gently, "Come in anyway. The milk's already warm."

They sat by the fire. She did not mention the letters. Neither did he. When he left, she stood in the doorway and watched the satchel bob away across the snow until she couldn't see it any more.

On the long walk home, Fennick thought about it.

Something had happened in the warm city by the sea. He did not know what. It might be nothing: a busy month, a lost pen, a son who had simply forgotten, the way sons sometimes do. Or it might be something worse. Either way, an old ibex at the top of a mountain was waiting by a fire for a blue envelope that did not come.

And Fennick was a postman. The whole of his job, the whole of his life, was the space between a letter being written and a letter being read. That space was now empty, and he could not stand it.


FOUR

The post office had a ledger.

Every letter that had ever come up the mountain was written down in it, in Fennick's careful hand: the date, the house, and the address it came from, copied off the back of the envelope. He turned back through the pages by lamplight until he found it. Mrs. Orla's son. A street, a number, a city by a warm sea.

He took down a sheet of paper and a pen.

He was not a writer. He was a postman. He sat for a long time chewing the end of the pen before he wrote anything at all.

Dear Sir, he wrote at last.

I am the postman for your mother. I have brought her your letters every week for six years. She keeps them in a tin on her mantelpiece; I have seen it. She has not had one for three weeks. She has not said anything, but she leaves the door unlatched now, in case I come in the night.

I do not know why you have stopped writing. It is none of my business. But if you are able, please write, even if it is only to say the weather. She would like to know the weather.

Yours faithfully,
Fennick (Stoat), Postmaster, Upper Pass Office.

He folded it, sealed it, stamped it with a stamp that had a mountain on it, and put it in the red box on his own door. Then, because the red box was only collected once a week, and that was by him, he took it out again and walked it down the mountain himself, two days down, to the village with the railway.


FIVE

He did not know if it would work. He went back up the mountain and walked his round, and at the ninth house he sat with Mrs. Orla by the fire, and drank the milk, and talked about the snow, and did not mention it.

Week four: nothing.

Week five: nothing.

On the sixth week, at the village post office at the bottom of the mountain, the postmistress, a cheerful marmot, handed him the bundle for the pass and said, "There's a parcel for you, Fennick. You. Personally. I didn't know you got post."

"I don't," said Fennick.

It was a small, flat package, wrapped in brown paper, with palm-tree stamps.

Inside was a letter, in tidy, slanting writing, and a bundle of five pale blue envelopes, tied with string.

Dear Postmaster, the letter said.

Thank you for writing to me. I am sorry. I have been very ill, in hospital, for two months, and could not write and did not want anyone to tell her, because she would try to come down the mountain, and she is too old for the mountain. I am better now. I am home.

I have written the five letters I missed. They are dated. Would you give them to her one a week, as if nothing had happened? Then she will never have to worry about the weeks she worried. And I will write again on Sunday, the way I always do.

Thank you for noticing.


SIX

So Fennick did.

He carried the first blue envelope up the mountain, seven days across the snowfields, and knocked on the ninth door, and when Mrs. Orla opened it he held it out without a word.

She looked at it. She looked at him.

She took it very carefully, the way you would take a bird, and held it to her nose, and closed her eyes.

"From my son," she said.

"Late," said Fennick. "The weather."

"Yes," said Mrs. Orla softly. "Yes, I expect it was the weather."

She made the milk with extra honey that day. They sat by the fire for a long time, much longer than it took to drink it.

And when he got up to leave, she said, without looking at him, very lightly, "You're a good postman, Fennick."

"It's only letters," said Fennick.

"No," said Mrs. Orla. "It isn't."


SEVEN

He delivered the other four, one a week, until he caught up with Sunday, and the letters came again in the ordinary way, with palm trees on the stamps.

Mrs. Orla never asked. He never told. Up there at the top of the pass, where the stars come down close enough to post a letter to, some things go unsaid because saying them would make them smaller.

But on the mantelpiece, beside the tin of blue envelopes, there is now a second, smaller tin. Inside it is a single sheet of paper with a mountain stamp in the corner, which arrived, the following spring, from a warm city by the sea, with a note clipped to it in tidy slanting writing:

I thought you should have this. He wrote it.

And sometimes, in the long winter evenings, when the fire is low and the snow is falling, the old ibex takes it out and reads it, the letter she was never meant to see, about an unlatched door, and a tin on a mantelpiece, and a postman who noticed.


THE END
