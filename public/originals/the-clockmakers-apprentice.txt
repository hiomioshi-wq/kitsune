THE CLOCKMAKER'S APPRENTICE

A Kitsune Original. Text released under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0.


ONE

In the town of Brask, every clock was made by one person, and that person was an owl called Master Hollin, and he was very old, and he was dying.

He did not make a fuss about it. He mentioned it once, over breakfast, the way you might mention that it looked like rain.

"I'll be gone by the spring," he said, buttering toast. "So you'd better learn the Great Clock."

His apprentice, a young squirrel named Tibb, dropped her spoon in her porridge.

The Great Clock stood in the tower above the square. It was the size of a house, and it had been running for three hundred years, and every clock in Brask (every kitchen clock and pocket watch and grandfather clock in every hallway) was set by it. When it struck noon, the whole town stopped for lunch. When it struck six, the whole town went home. It was, as Master Hollin liked to say, the heart of the place, and hearts need looking after.

"I can't," said Tibb. "I've only been here a year. I can do pocket watches. Small ones. Badly."

"You did the baker's cuckoo clock," said Master Hollin.

"The cuckoo comes out backwards!"

"Yes," said the owl, with deep satisfaction. "People come from three towns away to see it. Eat your porridge."


TWO

So all that winter, Tibb learned the Great Clock.

She climbed the tower every morning in the dark, up four hundred and twelve steps, with Master Hollin coming behind her more slowly every week. She learned the names of the wheels: the great wheel, the centre wheel, the third and fourth wheels, the escape wheel with its teeth like a row of tiny shark fins. She learned to wind the weights, which were stones as big as she was, on chains that groaned. She learned to oil the pivots with a feather, and to listen to the ticking (tock... tock... tock...) and to hear, inside it, whether the clock was well.

"It's too slow," she said one morning, frowning. "By... a second? Two?"

Master Hollin, sitting on the top step to get his breath back, opened one eye.

"Three," he said. "And it's been three slow for a hundred years. Leave it."

"Why?"

"Because," said the owl, "a hundred years ago, the clockmaker before me, old Mother Wicke, set it three seconds slow on purpose, and she told me, and now I'm telling you, and one day you'll tell someone. That's the job."

"But why did she..."

"Wind the weights," said Master Hollin, and closed his eye.


THREE

He grew thinner. He climbed more slowly. In February, he stopped climbing altogether, and Tibb went up the four hundred and twelve steps alone, and came down, and sat by his bed and told him what she'd heard in the ticking.

"There's a catch on the third wheel," she said. "Every hour, at about twenty past. Just a little stutter."

"Hm," said the owl, his eyes closed. "Worn tooth. Twelfth from the mark. Don't replace it. File it. Gently. Half a breath."

"How do you know which tooth? You haven't seen it in a month."

"I've known that clock for seventy years," said Master Hollin. "You know someone for seventy years, you know where they limp."

She filed the twelfth tooth, gently, half a breath. The stutter stopped.

She came down and told him, and he smiled with his eyes closed, and said nothing, and that was the best thing anyone had ever said to her.


FOUR

He died on the first warm day of March, in the afternoon, with the window open and the sound of the Great Clock coming across the rooftops.

Tibb was with him. Near the end, he opened his eyes and looked at her, very clear, the way he used to look at a watch he was about to fix.

"The three seconds," he said.

"You never told me why."

"No." He was quiet for so long that she thought he had gone. Then: "When Mother Wicke was a girl, there was a fire. In the square. A cart of lamp oil. Her little brother was... there." He took a breath that rattled. "The clock struck noon, and everyone came out of the shops for lunch, into the square, all at once. Into it. If they'd come out three seconds later..."

He did not finish. He did not need to.

"She couldn't change what happened," he said. "But she could make sure noon came a little late, forever. A little gap. A breath. Just in case." His eyes were closing. "Silly. Doesn't do anything. Never did."

"It's not silly," said Tibb.

"No," the owl agreed, very softly. "It's the job."

The Great Clock struck four. He listened to every stroke, and after the fourth one he didn't listen any more.


FIVE

The town of Brask had a funeral for him, and everybody came, because everybody's clock had been made by him, and you could not walk through Brask without hearing his work ticking in every window.

Afterwards, Tibb climbed the four hundred and twelve steps.

She sat on the top step where he had sat, and listened to the clock (tock... tock... tock...) and for the first time in her life she felt how enormous it was. Not the clock. The time. Three hundred years of it, passed from paw to paw. Mother Wicke, who lost her brother. Master Hollin, who knew where it limped. And now her, a squirrel who could do small pocket watches badly and who had made a cuckoo come out backwards.

She thought about setting the clock right. Three seconds. It would be easy. A turn of a screw.

She thought about the fire in the square, two hundred years ago, and a girl who couldn't change anything and changed it anyway.

She left it three seconds slow.


SIX

Tibb is the clockmaker of Brask now.

She is good at it; better than she believes. She makes pocket watches that are not bad at all, and kitchen clocks, and grandfather clocks, and she has made, by request, eleven more backwards cuckoos, and one sideways one, and a clock for the school that plays a little tune at the end of the day that sounds like a sigh of relief.

Every morning she climbs the four hundred and twelve steps and listens to the heart of the town. She knows the twelfth tooth on the third wheel. She knows where it limps.

And at noon, every day, in the square at Brask, everybody comes out of the shops for lunch, three seconds late, and nobody knows why, and nobody needs to.

Next year she is taking an apprentice. A small, nervous hedgehog who can barely hold a screwdriver. Tibb has already decided that on the first day, she will make the hedgehog a pocket watch, a small one, set three seconds slow.

And when the hedgehog says, "It's wrong," she'll say, "Yes. Wind it. I'll tell you why one day."

That's the job.


THE END
