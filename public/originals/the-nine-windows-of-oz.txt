PART ONE

In the Gillikin Country, in the town of Purple Bench, which is named for a bench that is no longer purple but has kept the name out of politeness, there is a house with an attic, and in the attic there are nine windows.

The windows were blown sixty years ago by a glassblower named Master Ollie, who was famous in three counties for being able to put anything into glass if you could describe it honestly enough, and who had come to Purple Bench one autumn to convalesce from a bad cold, and stayed three winters, and made the windows to pay for his lodgings, because he had run out of money and never ran out of honesty.

They were not ordinary windows. Master Ollie had run short of sand that first winter — the roads were closed and the sand-carts did not come — and he had made up the difference with whatever the household could put in the melting pot, and the household, being Gillikin and thorough, had put in a great many things that were not sand at all: three letters that could not be delivered because the people they were for had moved away and left no address; a tablespoon of water from the summer millpond, kept in a jar since August by the youngest daughter for reasons she could not explain; a pinch of ash from the first fire of each of the last six years; and a handful of small objects from under the sofa cushions, which are, as everyone knows, the most honest objects in any house.

The glass that came out of that pot was clear to look at, like any good window glass, and it was not until the ninth window had cooled and been set in its frame, on the Tuesday before the thaw, that anyone looked through one and saw yesterday.

PART TWO

Not their own yesterday. That was the first thing the family checked, and the first thing Master Ollie checked too, when they fetched him up the stairs with his hat still in his hand.

Each window looked into somebody's yesterday, and it was always the same somebody per window, and none of the somebodies were anybody the family knew. The fourth window, for instance, looked into the kitchen of a Winkie farmhouse on a summer afternoon, and a woman was lifting a pie out of an oven, and out of the window behind her you could see a yellow road, and no matter what time of day you climbed to the attic, the pie was just coming out, just at the moment of coming out, held in two striped mitts, forever.

Master Ollie studied the nine windows for a week, and at the end of the week he wrote, in the plain way he wrote everything:

THE GLASS REMEMBERS. IT CANNOT HELP IT. THESE NINE ARE MADE MOSTLY OF MEMORY, AND SO THEY LOOK BACKWARD. DO NOT ASK THEM TO LOOK FORWARD. THEY HAVE NOT GOT ANY.

And under that, because he was an honest man and charged accordingly:

CHARGE A BUTTON A LOOK. THE BUTTONS WILL KEEP BETTER THAN THE YESTERDAYS.

PART THREE

This is what was in the glass, and it is worth the telling, because nobody in Purple Bench ever did tell it properly, and the town has been guessing for sixty years.

The letters went in first. There were three of them, brought by the postmaster's own hand, which in a town of one shelf and one strong pigeon is the highest solemnity the office has. They were good letters — well meant, warm, addressed to people who had once lived in Purple Bench and now did not, and who had gone away in the spring of a bad year, in three different directions, without leaving forwarding addresses, which is a thing that happens in small towns more than towns like to admit. The law of the post is that a letter belongs to its address, and an undeliverable letter belongs, in the end, to nobody; and the postmaster had kept them eleven years in the drawer where the office keeps its conscience, and when Master Ollie asked what honest things the household had for the melting pot, the postmaster brought the three letters and said, in the voice of a man paying a debt, "They were meant. That is honest enough for anything."

The millpond water went in next, the tablespoon of it, kept in a jar by the youngest daughter — who was Senna's grandmother, and was nine that winter, and had kept the jar since August for a reason she could not explain then and explained all her life afterward, which was that the millpond in August was the best thing she knew, and she had wanted to be sure of keeping some.

And the ash went in from six autumns, one pinch to a year, because the household kept the first fire's ash in a tin as a matter of tradition whose origin nobody remembered, which is the best kind of tradition, being load-bearing.

All of which is to say: the nine windows were made of love with nowhere to go, summer in a jar, and the remembered warmth of six winters. Nobody who knew the recipe has ever been surprised by where the windows look. The surprise, if there is one, is that glass so honest should have picked other people's yesterdays to hold instead of the household's own. But the Tabors, being Gillikin and thorough, have a family saying about it, which Senna's grandmother used to recite while dusting, and which runs: A LETTER THAT CANNOT BE DELIVERED GOES ON WANTING TO ARRIVE. THE GLASS IS ONLY DOING WHAT THE LETTERS WOULD HAVE DONE. IT IS LOOKING IN AT SOMEBODY ELSE'S HOUSE, TO SEE IF THEY ARE HOME.

PART FOUR

The family — whose name was Tabor, and who ran the post office, which in Purple Bench meant one shelf and a strong pigeon — took his advice, and the nine windows became the town's second attraction after the bench.

For a button, anyone could climb to the attic on a Sunday, when the Tabor house was open to visitors, and look through whichever window had nobody waiting at it, and see a yesterday that was not their own. This was the rule of the glass, and nobody ever found a way around it: the windows never once showed a visitor their own past, not by accident, not by wishing, not by standing at the right angle. The glass, being made of other people's undeliverable things, had other people's yesterdays in it, and was firm about the fact, the way glass is firm about everything.

The town came to know the nine yesterdays well. There was the pie window, and there was a window that looked into a schoolroom where a lesson was always just ending, and one that looked into a garden where it had just stopped raining, and the ninth window, which was the smallest, and which looked into a room where somebody was always just leaving, so that you never saw their face, only the door, and one hand on the latch.

People liked the ninth window least and came back to it most.

PART FIVE

Sixty years is a long time for a family to keep an attic, and the Tabors kept it well, in the Gillikin way, which is to keep a thing long past its usefulness because it has become a relative. The last Tabor daughter, whose name was Senna, cleaned the nine windows every Thursday, and knew all the yesterdays by heart, and had never paid the button, being family, and had never needed to, for the same reason.

And then, in the spring of this story, Master Ollie's grandson came to Purple Bench.

He was a glassblower too, from the southern cities, and he had a letter of introduction and a professional eye, and he looked at the nine windows not the way the town looked at them, which was fondly, but the way a glassblower looks at old work, which is the way a doctor looks at an old friend's knees. And the ninth window he looked at longest, and then he offered to buy it.

He offered a great deal of money, and he was honest about why: the glass was his grandfather's best, he said, and there were perhaps nine panes of it left in the world, and he had four, and the family had eight, and if he had the ninth pane he would have what his grandfather never had, which was all of it.

Senna, who had cleaned that pane every Thursday for eleven years, and had come to think of the person always just leaving as somebody she knew, said no.

The town, which loved the nine windows the way it loved the bench, said no.

The offer went to the town council, which was swayed by the great deal of money and by the argument that one window of nine was hardly the loss of the whole attic, and the council was not made of bad people, but it was made of people who had each looked through the ninth window exactly once and had gone away discomfited and had not gone back.

The council said yes, in the spring, with the best intentions in the world, which is how most things are said yes.

PART SIX

The glassblower's men came on a Tuesday, which in Purple Bench is the day for taking things apart, and they lifted the ninth pane out of its frame with great care, and wrapped it, and carried it down the seventeen stairs, and Senna stood at the bottom and did not watch them do it, because watching would have been a kind of agreement.

The pane rode away in a wagon with straw under it and a tarpaulin over, and Senna went back up to the attic and looked at the hole where it had been, which now showed the ordinary present, which was a grey Tuesday, and she found she had nothing to say to it.

The leaks began that evening.

Small ones. A fish on the doorstep of the smithy, wet, swimming-looking, eleven miles from any water. A verse of a song in the schoolroom that no one had taught anybody, which all the children knew by the next morning. A snowfall, one minute long, out of a clear June sky, on the bench — and the snow was old snow, an older year's snow, for it lay in the cracks of the wood the way it had once lain in the cracks of another wood entirely, and it did not melt until Thursday, which is not snow's way.

The town held a meeting about the leaks, and the meeting did what meetings do, which was to look at the person best able to explain, and Senna stood up and said that the ninth pane had been yesterday's, and yesterday had come out of its frame, and yesterday was getting everywhere.

"Then we shall put the pane back," said the council, relieved, and sent a fast rider south with the town's money and its apologies.

The rider came back on the ninth day. The glassblower's shop had the pane, he reported, wrapped and honored, on a shelf in the sun; and the glassblower had, that very week, been offered more than a council's ransom for a single pane of his grandfather's memory-glass by a collector from the Emerald City; and the glassblower was an honest man, and had refused; and the honest man's apprentice had not, and the pane was gone.

PART SEVEN

Senna went to the Emerald City herself, in June, with the fish in a covered jar (it had appeared again, in the schoolroom inkwell, and seemed content) and eleven buttons in her pocket, one for each year of Thursdays.

It took her four days to find the collector, which she did by following the snow, which leaked out of his townhouse in little fits whenever the front door opened, so that the doorstep was always swept and the neighbors were always puzzled.

He was not a bad man. He was a man who had paid a fortune for the one thing money could not verify, and he had it in a room by itself on a stand of purple wood, and he looked through it, he told her, every evening at sundown: at the room where somebody was always just leaving, at the hand on the latch.

"I could sell it," he admitted, "at ten times what I paid. The glassblower's people say memory-glass is the only glass that appreciates."

"It's leaking," said Senna.

"Everything old leaks," said the collector.

"Yesterday," said Senna, "is getting into your neighbors' shoes."

And she told him about the fish, and the verse, and the minute of snow on the bench, and the collector, who had bought the window because he had once, in his own house, in his own yesterday, been the one always just leaving, and had wanted very much to see the other side of the door — sat very still for a while, and then stood up and wrapped the pane with his own hands.

He did not sell it to her. He gave it back, on the condition, laid down in his own handwriting on the wagon-way bill, that the window keep its charge of a button a look, forever, for anyone, and that one button of every year be sent to him, which Senna has done every June since, a small parcel, a single button, and he writes back every time, one line, about the weather.

The pane went home in the wagon, and back up the seventeen stairs, and into its frame, which Senna had oiled every Thursday of its absence, and the leaks stopped that same evening, the way a held breath is let out.

The fish was returned to the millpond water it had come from, by way of the melting pot. And the ninth window shows what it always showed: a room, and a door, and a hand on the latch — and once, and once only, on the first Thursday after it came home, the hand, for just a moment, unclasped, as if whoever it was had heard the attic settle, and was pleased.
