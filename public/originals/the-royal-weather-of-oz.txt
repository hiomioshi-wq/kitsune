PART ONE

The Royal Weather Office stands behind the palace in the Emerald City, in a building with a barometer on the roof the size of a cartwheel and a weathervane that points, very slowly, at whatever it likes. It has three clerks, and the three clerks are the whole of the Royal Weather, because Oz is a well-organized country and does not need weather the way other countries need it. Rain in Oz is an appointment. Snow is a delivery. Nobody in Oz has ever been rained on by accident, and if you have, you may take it up with the Office, which is exactly what happened in the spring of this story.

The three clerks were these:

The first was a Rain Fairy named Mrs. Cumulus, who had been retired for two hundred years and still came in on Thursdays, because the Office was the only place where anybody talked about the rain with proper respect. She wore a grey shawl and carried a little umbrella that had never once been opened, since opening it would have been an insult.

The second was a retired thunder named Roland, who had been a very good thunder in his day — the kind that arrives a polite distance behind its lightning and makes up for lateness with warmth — and who now did the paperwork. He had a low, comfortable voice, and when he spoke, the inkwells trembled, and the filing cabinet learned to stop rattling by holding its breath.

The third was a very literal Sandman, of the sort that delivers sleep in sacks, who had been seconded to the Office during a staff shortage forty years before and had never been seconded back. He delivered weather the only way he knew how: in sacks, with labels. A sack labeled DRIZZLE. A sack labeled LIGHT BREEZE, HUMID. Nobody had ever asked him to change, because the system worked, and in Oz, if a system works, you guard it with your life.

PART TWO

The Office's pride — the thing that Mrs. Cumulus would show you before she would offer you tea — was the Schedule. The Schedule was a ledger as long as a fence, and in it was written every piece of weather that Oz would ever need, and where it would go, and when.

The Schedule's masterpiece was a small rain.

It was a very good small rain, the kind that sits politely in the middle distance while a wedding happens, so that the wedding has something picturesque to be married under, and it had been booked, by a Winkie family of foresight and patience, for the wedding of a daughter who was not yet born at the time of the booking. The rain was scheduled for one hundred and forty years in advance, on a Thursday in June, at three o'clock, in the village of Dug-Out Bottom, which is in the Winkie Country, west of here, and which is very fond of weddings and will hold them under anything.

The booking had been initialed by three successive Directors of Weather, and had survived two palace renovations and one brief vogue for indoor weddings, and the whole Office regarded it the way a family regards a photograph of an ancestor who once did something extraordinary: not with love exactly, but with an unbroken, nodding respect.

And then, one market day in May, it arrived.

PART THREE

It did not arrive in Dug-Out Bottom, where it belonged, and it did not arrive in June, a hundred and forty years from now, when it was welcome, and it did not arrive at three o'clock, which would at least have been punctual.

It arrived over the Emerald City market at ten in the morning, in the middle of the Annual Display of Strawberries, and it was a very good small rain, and it rained exactly as well as it always had, on the strawberries, and on the awnings, and on the parade.

Now, it must be said that a rain cannot help where it falls. A rain goes where it is sent. Somewhere between its drawer in the vault and the sky above the market, this one had been sent to the wrong place at the wrong time by a hundred and forty years and a few hundred miles, and it did the only thing a well-brought-up rain could do under the circumstances, which was to be brief, and gentle, and to leave everything a little greener than it found it.

The market recovered. Markets do. But the Office had been publicly wrong, and in the Emerald City, where everything is organized, being publicly wrong is a weather event of its own.

PART FOUR

"An apology will have to be issued," said the Director of Weather, who had come out of retirement for the emergency and stood in the Office with his coat still traveling toward the hook.

"What form?" said Roland, and the inkwells trembled.

"What do you mean, what form?"

"In Oz," said Roland, "an apology must be paid in kind. It's the law of exchanges. You can't say sorry in words to a village, it isn't currency. We are the Royal Weather. When we apologize, we apologize in weather."

There was a silence while everyone considered the accounts.

"The wedding family," said Mrs. Cumulus at last, and her voice had the grey thickness of a sky before it decides. "The family in Dug-Out Bottom. They've been waiting a hundred and forty years for their wedding rain, and it's spent itself over a strawberry parade. What are we going to send them instead?"

"Nothing," said the Sandman. "The vault is empty of Wednesday."

"What?"

"Every rain in the vault is scheduled," said the Sandman, slowly, because he was a very literal Sandman and things took the time they took. "There are no spare rains. There never have been spare rains. The Schedule does not allow for spares, because a spare is a rain that expects to be wasted, and no rain of ours expects to be wasted."

PART FIVE

While Mrs. Cumulus went looking through the Schedule, the Office did what offices do in a crisis, which is to keep its appointments, because the weather does not stop being wanted just because the weather is in trouble.

Roland took the counter. It was the work he liked best, and the work he was worst at, and the two facts were connected: a retired thunder cannot hear a request for weather without taking it personally, and there is nothing in the world so unlike a form as a personal favor.

A farmer came in at eleven for the breeze he had booked for Tuesday, to dry his hay.

"You will have it," said Roland, and the inkwells trembled. "Eleven o'clock, the east field, and it will be a good breeze, an honest breeze — I used to know it well. It has cousins."

"Eleven o'clock is when we turn the hay," said the farmer. "If it comes at noon it is of no use to me and it knows it."

"Then it will come at eleven," said Roland, in the low voice that had once arrived a polite distance behind lightning, and the farmer went away comforted in a way he could not have explained to his wife, and the breeze, which had been filed for Tuesday since the farmer's grandfather's time, came at eleven exactly, and was an honest breeze, and the hay was dry by noon, and the farmer told the whole of it at the inn that evening as a story about the weather, which are the best stories, because everyone already believes half of them.

The Sandman took the deliveries. He went out with his sacks at midday, which is not when a Sandman goes out, and he went out with the wrong sack twice, so that the village of Yellow Gorse, which had asked the Office for a light mist for its ferns, received instead a small sack of sleep, forty worts' worth, and the entire fern society of Yellow Gorse lay down in the afternoon in the fern fashion, which is to say invisibly, and was so refreshed by it that the village wrote in to complain that the Office had never done anything so good and could it be arranged for the same mistake weekly.

The Sandman filed the letter under Compliments, and showed it to no one, and felt, for the first time in forty years, that his secondment had been a career.

And Mrs. Cumulus read the Schedule, page by page, from the front, which is the honest way, and the front of the Schedule is mostly wedding weather, because the Schedule's founders were romantics with good ink; and she read of a drizzle booked for a proposal in 1942 that had gone off perfectly, and of a snow booked for a children's morning that had arrived flake for flake as written, and of one hundred and forty years of weather kept faithfully by three clerks and their predecessors, all of it sound, all of it pure, all of it proud — and she came at last to the wedding page, and read the entry, and the entry was sound, and the ink was good, and the booking was whole; and she took off her spectacles, and put them back on, and read the entry with her nose nearly on the paper, and saw, at the corner of the writing, under the small neat curve of a hundred and forty years of pressure, the faintest brown shadow of a stain, spreading.

PART SIX

It was the paperweight that did it, in the end, as these things so often are.

Mrs. Cumulus went to the Schedule itself to look for the error, page by page, with the grey shawl pulled close, and on the page for the wedding year she found the booking, and the booking was sound, and pressed against the booking, holding it open at exactly the wrong moment of a very long afternoon, was the Office's only paperweight, which was made of a hailstone kept from the great storm of long ago, and which had been sitting on the corner of that page in perfect contentment for a hundred and forty years.

A paperweight made of hailstone, left on one page of a weather ledger for a century, does what any ice does. It melts slowly. And a drop of storm-water, falling very slowly, over a hundred and forty years, can wear a very small hole through one entry of a very long schedule, until the entry is not quite an entry any more, and the rain it holds is not quite booked, and one market morning in May the rain simply falls out of the hole, and goes, and being a good rain, goes where rain goes.

"Under the paperweight," said Mrs. Cumulus. "The error is under the paperweight. We have been keeping our mistakes pressed flat for a hundred and forty years, and they have been quietly dripping."

PART SEVEN

The apology took all afternoon to assemble, and it was, in the opinion of the market, the finest the city had ever seen — though the wedding family of Dug-Out Bottom, it must be owned, received the better half of it.

To the market: a rainbow, issued at four o'clock, with a formal card of regret from the Office, signed by all three clerks, the card being scented lightly with rain. The rainbow stood over the strawberry stalls until supper, and the strawberries, which had been rained on for eleven minutes, sold at a premium, being the sweetest in forty years, which the Office did not claim credit for, but did not exactly deny.

To Dug-Out Bottom, for the following June, and every June after for as long as the family should keep having weddings: weather by request, on deposit, in advance, drawn from the one source that cannot run out, which is the Schedule's own reserve of Thursdays, re-audited by Mrs. Cumulus personally, every Thursday, forever.

And the paperweight was retired. It sits on a shelf in the Office now, in a small glass dish, where it can drip if it wants to, and does no harm to anyone's plans; and under the dish is a little card in Roland's tidy thunder-hand, which the schoolchildren of the Emerald City are brought to read on wet afternoons, and which says:

THIS IS THE WEATHER THAT GOT AWAY. IT WAS NOT THE RAIN'S FAULT. IT IS NEVER THE RAIN'S FAULT. CHECK YOUR PAPERWEIGHTS.
