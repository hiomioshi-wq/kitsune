THE LAST SLEIGH TO HOLLOW PINE

A Kitsune Original. Text released under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0.


ONE

On the last night before Midwinter, the post office at Hollow Pine had one parcel left, and nobody to carry it.

Kitsu looked at it for a long time. It was round and badly wrapped, tied with fishing line, and addressed in very large letters to THE OLD REINDEER AT THE TOP OF THE PASS. There was no name. There was a drawing of a reindeer, in case the postman was unclear on the concept.

"Who sent it?" she asked.

"Doesn't say," said Tobin the badger, stamping something without looking at it. "It came in with the snow. And you're not taking it. The pass is shut, your lantern's cracked, and it's Midwinter Eve. Go home. Eat something."

Kitsu picked up the parcel. It was warm.

"Oh, for pity's sake," said Tobin, and went to find her a better lantern.


TWO

Everybody in the mountain towns knew about the old reindeer. Her name was Bracken, and for forty winters she had pulled the Midwinter sleigh from town to town, carrying oranges and coal and news. Then her knees went, and the towns bought a motor sled, and the motor sled was very quick and nobody ever waved at it.

Bracken had gone up to the hut at the top of the pass. She did not come down.

It took Kitsu until midnight to get there. The snow came sideways. Twice she lost the path and found it again by the marker stones, and once she stopped and simply stood with her back to the wind and said several words Tobin had taught her by accident.

There was a light in the hut. Kitsu knocked.

"If you're selling something," said a voice like a slow door, "I've got no money. If you're lost, it's downhill."

"Parcel," said Kitsu.

The door opened. Bracken was enormous and grey and had one antler shorter than the other. She looked at Kitsu, and at the snow on Kitsu, and then she stood aside, because there are rules about Midwinter that are older than manners.


THREE

She unwrapped the parcel by the fire, with her teeth, taking her time.

Inside was a harness bell. It was old and dented and it had been polished so hard that the pattern had nearly gone. Wrapped around it was a piece of paper, and on the paper, in pencil, in forty different hands, were names.

Bracken read them. It took a while. Some were written by children and some had clearly been written by the same children forty years later. Mrs. Tamsin from the stationer's. Both of the Orla boys. Somebody who had signed only with a paw print. And at the bottom, in the same very large letters as the address:

WE KEPT YOUR BELL. WE TAKE IT IN TURNS TO POLISH IT. THE MOTOR SLED IS RUBBISH. PLEASE COME TO DINNER.

Bracken did not say anything for some time. The fire popped.

"My knees," she said at last.

"I know," said Kitsu.

"It's four miles down in the dark."

"I know. I've got a good lantern."

Bracken looked at the bell. Then she hooked it over her short antler, where it hung a bit crooked, and she shook her head once to hear it. It rang a little flat. It was the best sound Kitsu had heard all year.

"You'll have to go slowly," said Bracken.

"I'm not in a hurry," said Kitsu, who was, and had been all her life.


FOUR

They came down out of the pass at first light, a very small fox with a very good lantern and a very large reindeer going one step at a time.

Somebody in Hollow Pine heard it first. It might have been Tobin, who claimed afterwards that he had only happened to be standing on the roof. A flat little bell, a long way up the mountain, coming slowly.

One door opened. Then another. By the time Bracken reached the bottom of the road, the whole town was standing in the snow in its slippers, and nobody was saying anything, and every single person was waving.

There was a place set for her at the long table. There had been a place set for her every Midwinter for eleven years. Nobody had mentioned it, in case it made people sad.

Kitsu ate two dinners and fell asleep with her face on the bread. When she woke up there was a blanket on her, and a harness bell ringing somewhere outside, very slowly, up and down the street, because the children had asked for a ride, and Bracken had said that she supposed her knees could manage one.

She managed eleven.
