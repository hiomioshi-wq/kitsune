Chesney had been boiling backwards for three days before anyone said so out loud, which, in an office whose business is noticing, was itself a delay worth filing.

It began, as these things do, with the tea.

Merrit took his first cup of the morning at nine, and the tea was wrong in a way he could not immediately diagnose, which bothered him more than a wrongness he could diagnose, diagnosis being the whole of his profession and the sole source of his calm. The tea tasted of yesterday. Not stale — that was the confounding thing; staleness has a flavor of its own, and Merrit knew it the way a mother knows her own child's cough. The tea was fresh. It was simply fresh from the wrong day. It had the exact bright, already-happened quality of a thing that has been drunk before, and Merrit put the cup down, and looked at Chesney, and Chesney looked, as much as a kettle can, anywhere else.

"You're boiling yesterday's water," said Merrit.

Chesney did not deny it. Chesney's position, rendered over the following days in the kettle's only available medium — a range of boiling sounds from the apologetic to the openly defiant — was that the water was perfectly good water, that the Bureau had drunk it yesterday and nobody had died, and that in any case the physics of the situation was, like most complaints about the senior partner, unproven.

The physics was, however, provable, and the proof arrived on the Thursday in the person of the Inspector of Moments.

Her name was Miss Gantt, and she was the Bureau's entire regulatory apparatus, which is to say that the Bureau of Small Delays had exactly one person in the world with the authority to walk in unannounced and check the cabinet, and the world had spent the last decade keeping her comfortably elsewhere. She was tall and precise and carried a clipboard whose clip, Merrit had always noticed, engaged a half-second before the paper was fully in, which told you everything: Miss Gantt arrived at her moments slightly early, on principle, to be sure of them.

"Kettle," said Miss Gantt, from the doorway, without preamble, which was her way and had been since her first inspection, when Merrit had attempted a preamble and she had inspected it.

"Inspector," said Merrit.

"Your tea," said Miss Gantt. "I've had a cup from the pot you sent down to the clockmaker's, in the interests of an unannounced sample, and it is a day old."

"It's fresh," said Merrit.

"It is fresh," Miss Gantt conceded, in the voice of a woman conceding a point only to make the point behind it hurt more, "from yesterday."

Chesney boiled, defiantly, on the stove.

What had happened — and Merrit worked it out over the following hour with Miss Gantt watching and Chesney sulking, which a kettle does by boiling two degrees too hot — was this: Chesney had a small defect, present since manufacture, which Merrit had always privately attributed to the same era of craftsmanship that had produced the cabinet. Every kettle draws its water from somewhere; most draw it from the tap. Chesney, under certain conditions of resonance — the Great Clock three doors down being, as it happened, exactly those conditions — drew his from when instead.

Not far. One day. The water Chesney boiled was the water of the day before, the same water, the same atoms, arriving at the Bureau twenty-four hours after itself, which raised a question that had never once come up in Merrit's eleven years of training, or in the Bureau's manuals, or in the one letter Merrit had ever received from upstream (which had arrived, characteristically, before it was written), and the question was this:

If a kettle boils yesterday's water, and you drink yesterday's tea, and you do it every day, in what sense — Miss Gantt's clipboard was out now, the paper fully engaged — is the Bureau operating on today at all?

The inspection lasted two days, and Merrit would remember it afterward as the finest two days of work he had ever done, because the question was genuine and the Bureau's whole charter was on the table and Chesney, the cause of it all, was discovered along the way to be boiling not for himself but for the office, which is to say for Merrit, which is to say that the defect, the resonance, the entire backwards day of water, was the kettle's eleven-year answer to the question nobody had asked him, which was: how does one contribute, in a Bureau of Small Delays, if one is a kettle?

He boiled the day before's water so that Merrit's tea would be ready one day early. It had never once occurred to Chesney that tea, in order to be tea, wants to be today's, and Merrit found himself in the extraordinary position, familiar to every parent who has ever been handed a dead beetle with love, of having to file a fault in something that was entirely, structurally, a kindness.

Miss Gantt's finding ran to eleven pages and is still, by Merrit's own request, in the cabinet, under Faults, Beautiful, because it contains the following paragraph, which Merrit reads aloud to new visitors whether they ask or not:

The Bureau finds that the kettle Chesney, through a defect sustained in manufacture and maintained through loyalty, has for a period of approximately eleven years drawn the Bureau's water from the previous day, with the result that the Bureau's tea has been consistently one day old and consistently well meant. The Bureau further finds that this constitutes the single longest-running unauthorized temporal operation in the valley's recorded history; that it has caused no harm, no paradox, and no complaint from the clerk concerned; and that in the view of this Inspector, who has spent a career among people who would move the century for a far worse reason, the fault lies not with the kettle, but with a world that expects contributions to be punctual.

The remedy, arrived at jointly, was a compromise that made everyone equally unhappy, which is how you know it was correct. A small copper bracelet was fitted to Chesney's spout — the clockmaker downstairs did it, at cost, which was the whole town's way of applauding — tuned to the Great Clock's three-second hum, and it holds Chesney's drawing to the present day, more or less, allowing one minute of backwards draw per year, on a day of the kettle's choosing, which the Bureau calls pension, and Chesney calls, by boiling, his.

The tea since has been today's.

Merrit says it tastes exactly the same, and he has said it every morning for a year, in Chesney's hearing, which is the Bureau's way of saying thank you to the senior partner, who boils, punctually now, unprompted, which is the kettle's way of saying: the feeling is mutual, and it always was, even before today.
