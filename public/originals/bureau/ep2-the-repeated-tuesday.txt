The trouble with Tuesdays, Merrit Loop often said, is that there are so many of them and they are all so similar. It was a professional observation, not a complaint, and it is the reason the Bureau keeps a full set of Tuesdays in the cabinet, filed under When, Rear, in case a spare is ever needed.

The spare was needed in Hollow Pine on the third Tuesday of the month, when a Tuesday happened twice.

The first sign was the pigeons. The Hollow Pine pigeon post runs on a schedule of iron nerve, and the pigeons are the only civil servants in the valley who have never once lost a letter, a feat they attribute to rigorous resentment. On the Tuesday in question, the pigeons arrived at their destinations, delivered their letters, and were halfway home before discovering that they had not, in fact, delivered them yet — the letters were back in their little bags, undelivered, patient, and a pigeon who has flown nine miles for a delivery that has un-happened is not a pigeon you want in your airspace.

The second sign was the Night Market. Half the market is only open on Tuesdays, and a market that closes at the end of a Tuesday and then discovers the Tuesday is still going — stalls re-lit, awnings re-rolled, the whole apparatus of an ending disassembled by the fact that the ending did not take — is a market with strong views about the sanctity of closing time.

The third sign, which was the one that got the Bureau involved, was that a grandmother in a cottage on the north slope was delighted, and would not stop being delighted, and her delight was audible across three fields.

Merrit arrived by the two o'clock cart, with Chesney in a padded basket, because Chesney refused to be left behind and Merrit had long ago stopped pretending the kettle wasn't the senior partner. He climbed the north slope and found the grandmother — Mrs. Bettel, by the mail, which was pinned to the door with a delighted hand — sitting in her garden, drinking tea she had already drunk once that day, and greeting the same afternoon the way you greet an old friend who turns up at the door a second time with the same bottle of wine and no memory of the first visit.

"It came back," said Mrs. Bettel, by way of explanation, introduction, and biography. "The whole day. Tuesday's come round twice."

"I can see that," said Merrit, who could. The light had the particular amber of a day that knows it is repeating and is trying to act casual.

"And I want to keep it," said Mrs. Bettel.

There it was. The Bureau's whole trade in one sentence. Merrit had a form for that, too — it was the most-used form in the cabinet, and the cabinet kept it under Greedy, but Kindly, which was where Merrit filed everything that was technically an infraction and actually a human being.

Understand what a repeated day is, because the town never quite did, and Merrit's reports were the only documents that ever got it right. The second Tuesday was not a copy. Time does not copy; it has never once in its life plagiarized itself; there is nothing in the universe so unborrowed as an hour. The second Tuesday was a surplus — an actual, additional Tuesday, manufactured at the Tuesday factory (wherever that is; the Bureau's official position is "upstream") and delivered to Hollow Pine by accident, and an accident, once it has happened, is a fact, and facts, Merrit always said, are just things that haven't been filed yet.

Which left the Bureau with two duties in tension, which was how Merrit liked his duties, because tension is what holds a clock together.

Duty the first: the surplus Tuesday had to be reconciled. You cannot have a town that has had one more Tuesday than the rest of the valley; the pigeons' schedules would never forgive it; the market would be open on a Wednesday that was, in local terms, a Thursday; and the Great Clock, whose business is the whole valley's time and not just Brask's, was already showing symptoms — Merrit had seen it that morning, sulking, which a clock does by being two seconds slow instead of three, and everyone in Brask could feel the wrongness in their teeth.

Duty the second: Mrs. Bettel had drunk tea in her garden twice on the same afternoon, and she had liked it, and the Bureau of Small Delays had been founded — Merrit was reasonably sure; the founding documents were filed somewhere in the cabinet and the cabinet would not say where — on the principle that small delays are the ones worth keeping.

He spent the afternoon in the garden, with tea, working it out aloud, which was how he did his best work, and Chesney in the middle of the lawn, boiling occasionally, which was how Chesney contributed.

"The day can't stay," said Merrit. "The valley needs its Tuesdays back in the right number. Tuesday is a public material, Mrs. Bettel. It's like the river. You can't keep a bucket of river just because you like the noise it makes."

"I caught this Tuesday fair," said Mrs. Bettel.

"You caught it because it leaked," said Merrit, "and leaks are my whole department."

"Then what," said Mrs. Bettel, and here she looked, for one moment, like the girl she had been forty years of Tuesdays ago, "am I supposed to do with a day I have already spent once, and spent well, and been given again?"

And Merrit, who had been a clerk long enough to know that the answer to that question was already sitting in the garden wearing a cardigan, put down his cup and told her the truth, which is the Bureau's last resort and its best instrument.

"You spend it differently," he said. "A day that comes round twice is a day with its permission slip signed. Whatever you did this morning, you did. Nobody can take that. What you do this afternoon is nobody's but you. That's not a repetition, Mrs. Bettel. That's a second draft. Most people never get one."

The afternoon lasted four hours and was, by every account, the finest single afternoon in Hollow Pine's civic memory, including the market's, which extended its Tuesday a third time to cover it, and the pigeons', who flew one extra round entirely unbidden and delivered every letter with a slightly better attitude, and even including Mrs. Bettel's, who spent the four hours doing all the things she had spent the first Tuesday not doing: the letter to her sister she had put off for two years, the walk up the ridge she had put off for six, the small unforgivable selfishness of drinking tea in her own garden in the middle of a Tuesday with the door shut and the beds unmade.

At dusk, Merrit stood at the edge of the garden with his pocket watch, and the watch was showing the second Tuesday wearing out, as surplus days do, gracefully, like a guest who knows the hour, and the amber went out of the light the way it goes out of a story when the story is ready, and the ordinary Wednesday arrived on time, escorted, Merrit always suspected, the whole way.

The Form took him twenty minutes. REPEATED DAY, SURPLUS, ONE (1) TUESDAY, LOCAL. RECONCILED BY EVENING. RETAINED IN PART: the letter, the ridge, the tea. He signed it, stamped it with the little smiling clock, and filed it under the cabinet's own judgment, and the cabinet put it, without being asked, under Days, Well Spent, of which the Bureau's files now held one.

He was half down the slope before he remembered the pigeons, who had one thing left to say, and said it, in the time-honored manner of the postal service, from a height.
