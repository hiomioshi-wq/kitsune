The Bureau of Small Delays occupied one room above the clockmaker's shop in Brask, which everyone agreed was the right address for it and nobody could have said why.

You came up a stair that creaked in the key of C, past a landing where the light was always at four in the afternoon regardless of the actual hour, and through a door marked BUREAU OF SMALL DELAYS, AT YOUR EVENTUAL SERVICE. Inside there was a desk, a chair, a window with a view of the Great Clock's back, a filing cabinet, and a kettle.

The desk belonged to Merrit Loop, Clerk (First Class, and only class), who had worked for the Bureau for eleven years and had been late twice, both times with excellent reasons that he had written up in triplicate. Merrit was a compact man with the calm of someone who has seen the same Tuesday from several directions and found all of them survivable. He drank tea at a steady rate of one cup per crisis, whatever the century.

The kettle was called Chesney. Chesney was brass and prepotent and had opinions about the water. Chesney had come with the Bureau, in the sense that no one had ever hired Chesney and no one could remember the Bureau without him, and the clockmaker downstairs maintained that Chesney was technically the senior partner, since kettles predate clerks by some four thousand years and seniority is seniority.

The filing cabinet filed things by when, not what, which took getting used to. Merrit had once spent a whole afternoon looking for the stapler, which had turned out to be filed under Next Tuesday, where it had been waiting with what Merrit felt was unearned smugness.

On the morning this account begins, the post arrived, which was in itself unremarkable, and the post was a jar, which was.

The jar was about the size of a pickle jar and it was full of a minute.

You will have to take the jar's word for part of this, but you already do that every day with less reputable witnesses. The minute in the jar looked like a very slow honey, the color of noon light through a dusty window, and it sat in the glass without moving, the way a minute does when it has nowhere to be. Somebody had written on the label, in pencil, in the careful hand of a person who has recently had a shock: FOUND IN THE SQUARE. IS THIS YOURS?

Merrit held the jar up to the light. The minute held itself up to the light back, with the patient insolence of the temporarily detained.

"Well," said Merrit, to the office at large, "somebody's missing this."

At noon that day, somebody was. At noon that day, everybody was.

The minute in the jar was the minute that should have passed over the town of Brask at 12:04, and its absence had gone out across the square like a small hole in a sock. Nobody noticed the hole, at first. What they noticed was the effects, which were Brask's whole stock in trade.

The Great Clock struck twelve and then struck a moment that was not any moment in particular, a kind of twelve-and-a-little, and the crowd in the square looked up at it with the betrayed expression of people who have synchronized their lives to a machine for forty years and been repaid with an alibi.

Two shopkeepers shook hands at the exact same time and each believed the other had initiated it. A dog barked one second before the thing it barked at. The clockmaker downstairs stopped mid-repair, holding a mainspring that had, very briefly, been somewhere else, and looked at it with the loathing of a man whose entire profession has been personally insulted.

By half past twelve the town had worked out that a minute was missing, because Brask is a clock town and a clock town can lose a war without noticing but not a minute. By one o'clock there was a queue at the Bureau's door, and the queue was doing what queues do, which is agreeing with each other, loudly, about whom to blame.

"The clock's wrong," said the first person in the queue.

"The clock's fine," said Merrit, who had spent eleven years defending the Great Clock's honor and intended to spend eleven more. "The clock is never wrong. It's three seconds slow, which is a policy, and everything else it does is between the tick and the tock. What's wrong is that there's a minute gone, and it isn't the clock's minute. It's ours. All of ours. It's a civic minute."

"Then find it," said the queue.

"It's been found," said Merrit, and he held up the jar, and the queue went quiet in the way that crowds go quiet when a thing they were ready to be furious about turns out to be sitting in a jar at eye level.

The minute looked out at them. It had the decency to look embarrassed, which a minute can do, since a minute is mostly expression anyway.

The rest of the day was paperwork, as the best days are.

There is a Form for the return of a civic minute — Merrit kept it in the cabinet under Today, Soon — and the Form has requirements. A missing minute cannot simply be poured back into the square, because a minute, once detached, is very slightly not your minute anymore; it has been out in the world, it has seen things, chiefly the inside of a jar, and time is careful about what it lets back in. The return had to be witnessed, by a clock; it had to be received, by the town; and it had to be forgiven, which is the part nobody ever believes until they see the line on the form.

Merrit did the first part at one minute to one. The Great Clock's face had been watching the square all its life and was the only witness in Brask with an unimpeachable sense of timing, and it watched now, with its back to the Bureau window and its attention all the way round, as Merrit stood on the Bureau's little balcony with the jar and took off the lid.

The minute came out slowly, the way honey would if honey were pleased. It hung above the square for a moment — a small, noon-colored, briefly independent creature, taking its last look at the world from the outside, which is all any of us get of it — and then it settled down over the crowd and into the street and the clocks and the dog and the shaken hands, and became 12:04, forty-nine minutes late, to a reception that no minute in the history of minutes has had before or since: the town of Brask applauded.

The forgiveness clause Merrit handled himself, on the Form, in the space marked FORGIVEN BY, where he wrote the town of Brask, all of it, and dated it, and signed it with the Bureau's stamp, which is a small clock with a smile in it, which is also the Bureau's entire philosophy, stamped.

That evening, when the square was empty and the lamplighter was doing the lamps in the wrong order because he felt like it, Merrit sat at the desk with a cup of Chesney's best and looked at the label from the jar, which he had kept, because the Bureau keeps labels; labels are the honest part of a story.

FOUND IN THE SQUARE, the label said. IS THIS YOURS?

"No," said Merrit, to the label, to the square, to the minute that was out there somewhere being forty-nine minutes late inside everybody's afternoon. "But I know who keeps it."

He filed the label under Today, which was technically wrong, and the cabinet accepted it anyway, and Chesney boiled unprompted, which was his way of applauding, and the Great Clock struck nine, three seconds late, on purpose, as it had every evening since 1840, for reasons that were nobody's business yet, and would not be Merrit's business for four more episodes.
