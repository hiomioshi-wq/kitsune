Merrit Loop's day off was a Thursday, and it had been a Thursday for eleven years, and it had never once gone wrong, which was, by the standards of the Bureau of Small Delays, a record of almost suspicious quality, and Merrit had said so, out loud, once, in Chesney's hearing, in the tone of a man complimenting a bridge on its load-bearing capacity.

The office had a saying, pinned nowhere but believed everywhere: never compliment a day.

It went wrong at eleven minutes past nine on a fine Thursday morning, when Merrit, on his day off, in his own garden, two streets from anything temporal, in his second-best coat, with a cold drink and a nowhere to be, noticed that the morning was on its second attempt.

The light did the amber. The birds did the first verse again, a little louder, in the manner of a singer who suspects the audience has dozed. His cold drink went from half-drunk to full, un-drunk, refilled by no one, which is the exact moment a clerk of eleven years' standing stops drinking and starts observing.

The Thursday was repeating.

Not a surplus, this time; Merrit knew surplus to the ounce; he had filed one personally, and it had smelled of Mrs. Bettel's tea. This was a different animal: a loop. The Thursday had reached eleven minutes past nine, found something it disliked about eleven minutes past nine, and gone back for another try, and it was showing no inclination whatsoever to accept defeat. Nine-eleven arrived. Nine-eleven arrived again. The morning stood at the doorway of quarter past nine like a cat at a door it has decided is not a door.

Merrit put down the drink, put on his best coat, his working coat, and went in to the office, on his day off, at nine-thirteen, for the ninth time.

Chesney was already boiling.

"That's the spirit," said Merrit, and made the tea, which was the wrong tea for the loop to be his own, since the tea tasted of now, which meant the loop was not attached to Merrit's morning; it was attached to something in the Bureau's care, which meant — Merrit's hand stopped halfway to the second cup, which is the Bureau's version of a siren — that something in the building was due to happen at nine-eleven and had decided not to.

The filing cabinet stood at the back of the room, filing, which is to say: not filing. The cabinet, Merrit saw, was holding a drawer.

You will not find this in any manual, and Merrit had read every manual the Bureau owned, twice, once for the plot. A cabinet files by when. It is the most obedient machine in the building, after Chesney, and before Chesney's bracelet. When a cabinet holds a drawer — grips it, at the handle, in the particular rigid way a hand grips a rope that something at the other end is pulling on — it means the cabinet has a filing that cannot be filed: a when that is refusing to arrive.

Merrit crossed the room, in the amber, with the birds doing their third verse.

He opened the drawer, over the cabinet's equivalent of a deadlift.

Inside, there was a Form. Merrit knew it before he read it, the way you know a face you've been writing to for years. It was the Bureau's own — the oldest kind, the founder's stationery, the kind that did not come in a pad but was issued, singly, at need — and it was blank but for one field, the field at the top, which had been filled in, in the founder's hand, in 1840, and which read: WHEN: THURSDAY, THE LAST.

The founder of the Bureau of Small Delays — clockmaker, maker of Chesney, architect of the cellar, author of the margins — had died on a Thursday.

And he had left the paperwork.

The Bureau of Small Delays, Merrit understood, all at once, over the worst cup of tea of his career, was not the founder's business. It was the founder's filing. The building, the cabinet, the kettle, the charter, the cellar, the whole apparatus of jars and grace and eleven years of Merrit's excellent calm — it was the founder's answer to the one delay the founder had known he could not fix, which was the delay at his own end, and the Bureau was where he had filed it, and the cabinet had been holding it, unfiled, unfile-able, since 1840, because a man who spent his life putting time in jars was a man who could not, at the last, put his own Thursday in one, and could not bear to have it filed under Finished, and so had invented a drawer that would hold, forever, the single hardest when the Bureau had ever contained.

And what the loop was — nine-eleven, again, the birds again, the amber again — Merrit finally, finally understood, holding the drawer against the pull, was the town's own clockwork keeping its promise. The Great Clock had been the founder's firstborn. It had run three seconds slow since the man died, and everyone thought it was grace, and it was grace, and it was grief, and it had been grieving in a very literal, very Braskian way all along: the whole apparatus of the missing seconds, the daily tithe, the cellar, the noon-colored jars — the town's famous three-second lag was the clock refusing to let its maker's last Thursday fully arrive, and the Bureau's founder, who had known himself too well, had put the mechanism's brake in the one place it could hold forever: behind a drawer, in a filing that said THE LAST, and the cabinet had held it for a hundred and eighty-six years, and the drawer was now, for the first time since 1840, creaking.

Merrit did what a clerk does, which is the thing the manuals say and the thing the founder, in all his margins, had never written down: he asked the filing what it wanted.

This is not mystical. It is the Bureau's core competence, the same competence as Mrs. Bettel's second Tuesday and Marla Quen's fifty-nine minutes: you open the drawer, and you read the when, and you let the when tell you the terms.

The when wanted an ending. Not a reversal; the Bureau has no such form. An ending: a last Thursday that was allowed, at last, to be over.

Merrit filed the founder's death at nine minutes past nine, on his own day off, in his working coat, with the drawer's permission and the cabinet's unspeakable relief and Chesney boiling through all four attempts like a kettle at a graveside — filed it, stamped it with the little smiling clock, and wrote in the space marked FORGIVEN BY the only true thing in the whole valley: THE CLOCK.

The amber went out at quarter past nine like a lamp being carried to another room.

The Great Clock struck nine-thirty, three seconds late, and the three seconds were — Merrit stood very still in the empty office and felt it happen — for the first time since 1840, ordinary seconds, and not a holding pattern; and the Brask Second-Hand reported, the following week, that the Long Noon had felt "somehow longer this year," and the town agreed, and nobody could say why, and the why was that the town's oldest held breath had finally, after a hundred and eighty-six years, been let out.

Merrit took the next Thursday off, and it went wrong in the ordinary ways, which is to say that it rained, and the clockmaker's dog was sick on his coat, and the tea was only fair.

He said, in Chesney's hearing, that it was the finest Thursday of his career.

The office's other saying — the one that goes nowhere pinned, the one that lives in the work — is this: ENDINGS ARE JUST DELAYS THAT HAVE BEEN FILED. And under the desk, in the founder's old cigarette tin, where Merrit had never once looked, there was a small brass key, and the key fit the cellar's oldest shelf, and on the shelf there was one jar left from 1840 — the founder's own three seconds, drawn off the clock on the day of the death, by the mechanism, unbidden — and Merrit took it up to the square at the next Long Noon, and opened it, in front of the whole town, and let the founder's three seconds out, at last, into the morning air, where they turned, briefly, noon-colored, and went, and everyone in the square applauded, and nobody knew why they were crying.

The cabinet filed the applause under Today.

Which is where the Bureau keeps everything now, and always has, and always will, and where, Merrit is fairly sure, it belongs.
