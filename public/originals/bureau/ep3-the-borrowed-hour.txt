Every trade has a case it dreads. Doctors dread the one where the symptoms are polite. Clockmakers dread the watch that is fast AND slow at once, and claims to enjoy it. The Bureau of Small Delays dreads loans.

Time can be borrowed. That is the first thing they do not tell you, and the reason the Bureau exists, and the fact that Merrit Loop had written on a card and pinned above the desk, in Chesney's opinion at exactly the right angle to be read while drinking tea: TIME CAN BE BORROWED. IT CANNOT BE STOLEN. THE DIFFERENCE IS THE PAPERWORK.

The woman who came to the Bureau on a wet Thursday was named Marla Quen, and she was seventy-one, and she had, in the pocket of her coat, the last hour of her life's great letter, and the letter was forty-one years old.

She had started it in the Night Market when she was thirty. It was to a man who had gone away on the sleeper train with a promise that he would be back before the letter was finished, which had seemed, at the time, like a joke about her handwriting. She had written it across four decades in the margins of everything: a line between customers at her stall, a line while the kettle thought about boiling, a line in the dark when the words were the wrong ones and had to be undone. The letter had survived two fires and one wedding, hers, to a good man, now gone, who had known about the letter and had kept its corner of the desk sacred, which is the kind of thing you only find out about a person afterward, from the shape they leave in your habits.

Forty-one years, and the letter needed one hour more. She had run out of the ordinary kind, and she knew about the Bureau, because everyone in the valley knows about the Bureau, the way they know about the dentist and the weather: with a plan never to need them, and a private fear of the day they might.

"I want to borrow an hour," said Marla Quen, at the desk, in the voice of a woman asking for something she has already decided to deserve. "For a letter. It's late."

"How late?" said Merrit, reaching for the Form without seeming to, which is the Bureau's version of a doctor's reflex hammer.

"Forty-one years," said Marla Quen.

Merrit kept writing. The Bureau does not blink at figures; figures are the Bureau's native language, and anyway Chesney had already boiled, which was the kettle's way of saying that the interview had his support.

"Interest is one minute per hour," said Merrit. "You borrow one hour, you repay one hour and one minute. Repayment is due within your lifetime, at a moment of the Bureau's choosing, and the Bureau chooses kindly, but it does choose. There is no forgiveness clause on loans. That's the difference between a minute lost and a minute promised. Are you certain you have the repayment in you?"

Marla Quen looked at him for a long moment, and Merrit, who had conducted this interview in both directions many times over eleven years, watched her do the arithmetic that every borrower does, the only arithmetic that matters: what remains, against what is owed.

"I have the hour," she said. "And I'll find the minute somewhere. I always have."

He signed it out to her at four o'clock. An hour, withdrawn from the cabinet, from under When, Spare, where the Bureau keeps the valley's small change — there is such a drawer, and it is fuller than anyone would believe, because time, like money, like affection, like the ordinary kind of minutes, is mostly hoarded by people who were saving it for something — and the hour went into her hands like a length of good ribbon, and she carried it out of the Bureau the way you carry a cup of something hot through a crowded room, with an attention that made the whole street part for her politely, though the street could not have said why.

She finished the letter in fifty-one minutes.

The extra nine she spent reading it, which Merrit, when he heard, regarded as the soundest use of borrowed capital he had encountered in his career. The letter went out that evening on the sleeper train, addressed to a man forty-one years gone and nineteen years dead, to an address that was now a bakery, in a town that had since been renamed, and it was delivered — this is the part the Bureau knows and the post office does not — to the right place anyway, because a letter with forty-one years of intent in it has a momentum of its own, and the postal service's legendary stubbornness is, at its root, a sympathy.

The repayment came due eleven days later.

The Bureau chooses kindly. Merrit had told her that, and it was true, and it was also true that the Bureau had, on this occasion, very little choosing to do, because the hour presented itself.

Marla Quen was at the Night Market, at her stall, on a Tuesday, at dusk, doing nothing in particular — the letter was gone, the good man was gone, the man on the train had been gone so long that "gone" had stopped being about him at all — and it came to her, the way the repayment always comes, which is the way the borrowed thing's twin comes: quietly, and in the same shape as the taking.

She felt the hour arrive at her shoulder like a debt she had not, in the event, minded owing. Sixty minutes, plus one for the interest. It did not take her death or her health or anything the dreadful stories promise; the Bureau has never taken a minute off the end of anyone, and considers the rumor a slander put about by the postal service, with whom relations are professional and cold. It took an hour, is all. It came, and she knew it had come, and she looked at her stall, at the dusk, at the ordinary beautiful unrepeatable Tuesday around her, and she paid it the way you pay any honest debt: with the hour it asked for, given over to nothing at all — no stall, no letter, no task, nothing owed to anyone living or dead — one hour of her own life, spent sitting on a crate behind her own awning in the good dark of the market, drinking tea, watching the lamplighter do the lamps, in debt to no one, owed by no one, for the first time in forty-one years.

Chesney, who had insisted on coming, boiled at the end of it, out in the square, unprompted.

Merrit, at the desk, filed the closed loan under Paid, In Full, Kindly, and made the tea, and pinned a new card above the desk, because eleven years of this work teaches you one sentence at a time, and the sentence this case had taught was one he thought the cabinet would want:

THE INTEREST IS NEVER THE PROBLEM. THE INTEREST IS ONE MINUTE. WHAT PEOPLE BORROW AGAINST IS THE OTHER FIFTY-NINE.
