Every clock town keeps one secret, and Brask's was three seconds long.

The Great Clock of Brask ran three seconds slow. Everyone knew this. It was on the postcards. Tibb, the clockmaker's apprentice, told visitors that it was deliberate, three seconds of grace, the town's founding gift to itself, and visitors went away moved by it, and Tibb never told them the second half, which was: deliberate, yes; grace, certainly; but the three seconds had to come from somewhere, and they had been going somewhere, every day, since 1840.

They had been coming here.

The cellar under the Bureau of Small Delays was not on any floor plan, which was reasonable, since the Bureau's floor plan was filed by the cabinet, and the cabinet had its own ideas about which parts of the building were load-bearing paperwork. There was a door at the back of the filing room that Merrit had opened twice in eleven years, both times to check, both times closing it again with the very specific quietness of a man who has confirmed that the accounts are all still there, still accruing, still fine, and would very much like to be somewhere else.

Behind the door was the Bank of Brask, and the Bank of Brask kept time.

Shelves. Thousands of them, banked back into the dark further than the little lamp Merrit carried had opinions about reaching, and on the shelves, in jars of the same noon-colored glass as the minute from the square, were seconds. Three a day, every day, drawn off the Great Clock at the stroke of midnight by a mechanism nobody living had built and nobody living serviced, because it serviced itself, because it had been built by the founding clockmaker with exactly the self-maintaining stubbornness he had put into everything else, and it was, in Merrit's professional judgment, the only machine in the valley that had never once been late.

Three seconds a day since 1840. Merrit had done the arithmetic once, early in his clerkship, on the Bureau's best paper, and had then filed the arithmetic under Later, and the cabinet had re-filed it under When You Are Ready, which was the cabinet's whole personality.

The number, for the record, was this: the town of Brask had, in the cellar under the Bureau, a little over one hundred and eighty-six years of surplus delay, and the surplus was growing at the rate of three seconds every midnight, and the shelves, which were ordinary pine, had begun — Merrit had heard it, on his second visit, standing very still with the lamp — to creak.

The creaking was the problem that brought the Bureau, for the first time in its history, to the question it had been founded, Merrit was now fairly sure, to avoid: what was the Bank for?

You could not spend it. That was the first rule, and it was not the Bureau's rule; it was arithmetic's. Delay hoarded is not delay held in trust. A second saved in 1840 was not a second that could be returned to anybody now; time, unlike money, is issued fresh every morning and the old issue is never honored. There was nobody in the valley who could be given back one single second of 1841, because there was nobody left from 1841 to give it to.

Or nearly nobody.

The meeting was Merrit's idea, and it took him four months to arrange, and the arrangement consisted of one sentence in the valley's smallest newspaper, the Brask Second-Hand (motto: "Free With This Newspaper"), which read:

THE BUREAU OF SMALL DELAYS SOLICITS THE VIEWS OF ANY RESIDENT AGED NINETY OR OVER ON A MATTER OF LOCAL TIME.

Eleven people wrote back. Nine wrote jokes. One wrote, at dictation, from a granddaughter, that she had been meaning to complain about 1911 for some years now and was glad somebody was finally taking an interest.

And one, the eleventh, wrote on a postcard, in a hand that shook but had never once in its ninety-six years misspelled anything, a single line, which Merrit pinned above the desk next to the other cards, where it stayed:

YOU ARE SITTING ON A CENTURY OF US. LET SOME OF IT OUT.

The founding clockmaker's mechanism could not be stopped — stopping it would mean stopping the Great Clock, which would mean stopping Brask, which everyone agreed was not on — but it could, Merrit discovered, over one long winter of reading the founder's manuals in the cellar with the lamp and the jars and the sound of 186 years settling on pine, be tuned. The three seconds a day were not a fixed levy. They were a tithe, set deliberately, at three, in 1840, by a man who had put the number in brackets in his own hand in the margin of the mechanism's specification, with a note, which Merrit read aloud to the empty cellar four times before he trusted his eyes:

"(THREE IS FOR THE SHELF. TWO WOULD DO. START AT THREE, AND WHEN THE SHELF SPEAKS, TUNE TO TWO, AND LET SOMEBODY LATER DECIDE ABOUT ONE. — T.H., 1840)"

The shelf had spoken. Merrit tuned it to two.

And he did the other thing, the thing the postcard had asked for, in the only way the arithmetic permitted, which was the way that had been in front of the Bureau the whole eleven years, hidden in the one place Merrit had never thought to file it: in the streets.

You cannot give anybody back their 1841. But a town, the eleventh postcard-writer had understood, and Merrit now understood, is not made of people only. A town is made of afternoons, and the afternoons were still arriving, fresh, daily, unclaimed, and a town with two hundred years of stored grace in its cellar was a town that could afford to be slow on purpose in public.

The Bureau of Small Delays, from that spring, took over the maintenance of the town's one unbreakable tradition: the three-second grace itself. Brask stayed three seconds slow — that was sacred, that was the postcards, that was the town — but now, at the Bureau's insistence and the Great Clock's cooperation, the three seconds rotated. They moved. Every noon, the town's three seconds of grace were drawn from the cellar's accounts, and released into the square, as three seconds of no clock at all: a daily, civic, scheduled pause, at twelve o'clock precisely, when everybody in Brask, on the great clock's authority, had three seconds that were nobody's, off the books, out of the jars, given back from the shelf to the street.

Brask calls it the Long Noon, and visitors think it is charming, and Tibb tells them it is tradition, and it is now, because Merrit filed it that way, under Gifts, Civic, Recurring.

The cellar still grows. Two seconds a day, banked in the dark, into a town that has learned, at noon, to spend them.

Merrit visits twice a year now. The jars glow the same noon color, all the way back, and the shelves have stopped creaking, which is either the carpentry settling or the arithmetic forgiving him, and on his last visit, checking the accounts, he found the founder's specification open on the stand at the same page, and a new pencil mark beside the old one, which was impossible, because no one else had been down there since the clockmaker himself, in 1840; and Merrit read it with the lamp held high, and copied it out exactly, and filed it under the only heading the cabinet had left empty all these years:

"(GOOD. — T.H.)"
