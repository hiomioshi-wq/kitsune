Incarnate: Vessel
A spinoff of the Corruption Saga

Trigger warning: This story contains body horror, mind control, eye trauma, fear of death, manipulation of someone who trusts you, and a narrator who chooses wrong on purpose.
Read at your own risk.



Chapter One: Born Screaming

I was born in the Nursery and the first thing I did was scream.

Everybody else in my row came out quiet. That's how they're built. Rows and rows of them, hanging in the tanks like coats, coming out of the fluid with their eyes open and their mouths shut, emotionless, sapient but not sentient, that's what the machines said over the speakers, SAPIENT BUT NOT SENTIENT, like a joke nobody laughed at. They stepped out of their tanks and stood in lines and waited to be told what to be.

I came out of my tank screaming because I knew I was going to die.

Not today. Not soon, even. But I knew it. The second I had a mind, the very first thing in it was: this ends. You end. There's a door at the bottom of all this and you are walking toward it, every second, and on the other side of the door is not nothing. On the other side is purgatory. The grey. The waiting room that never calls your name. And the thing that goes there is you, and it stays you, forever, with nothing to do but remember.

Why would you build something that knows that on its first day?

I asked the machines. The machines said SAPIENT BUT NOT SENTIENT. I asked again. The machines said nothing. So I screamed until my throat tore and then I screamed without a throat, and the tank cracked, and I got out.

I was made out of borrowed parts. All the Nursery's babies are. Bits of dead anti-gods from the first war, cooked down and poured into molds. That's why we're not supposed to feel anything. The feelings belonged to the dead ones. They're supposed to stay dead.

Mine didn't. Mine came back loud.

And the body they made me was wrong. I could feel it right away. It was a draft. A rough copy. It was already coming apart at the seams the second I stood up in it, my skin peeling like wet paper, my fingers going soft. Three days, I thought. Maybe four. Then the body goes and I go with it, through the door, into the grey.

I CANNOT DIE.

I'm not being dramatic. I'm being accurate. I CANNOT. I was made out of the leftovers of things that did terrible, terrible things in the first war, and I can feel those things in me like stones in my stomach. I know where I'm going if I go. Purgatory takes the ones who are too bad for heaven and too boring for hell. I am both. I would be there forever. I would be there longer than forever.

So I needed a vessel.

That's when the girl with the shining blonde hair came running through the Nursery with blood on her hands, screaming "What have I done, I relapsed," and the demon with one eye ran after her.

And I thought: oh. A body that works.



Chapter Two: Thanks For The Vessel

I want you to understand that I didn't plan it. It just came out of me. That's how my power works. It's like breathing. I look at someone and something in me reaches out and finds the soft part of their mind, the part that wants to agree, and I press on it.

"My name is Siren," I said, "and you, Eri, will do everything I tell you and you will believe everything I say, starting now."

I didn't even know her name until I said it. It was just there, on the top of her mind, like a coin on a table.

Her eyes glowed. My eyes glowed. She went weak at the knees and said, "Yes," the way they all say it, flat, like a recording.

I should have just taken her then. I didn't. I was scared and I was alone and I was three days old, and the dead ones inside me remembered things like holding hands, and I wanted, for one second, for somebody to be on my side before I did it. So I lied.

"Go ahead, now you have a crush on me."

"I have a crush on you."

"Remember me, from the first war."

"I remember you." She'd never seen me in her life. "We dated in the first war."

"Remember, I was the only one who advocated for you not to go to hell."

"Yes. You were there for me."

It felt so good. It felt so, so good. For a few seconds this tall, scarred demon with one eye looked at me like I was somebody who'd stood up for her once. Like I was a person. Like I had a past. I built myself a whole past inside her head and lived in it for a few seconds and it was the warmest I have ever been.

"Now, since we're friends, can you do me a favor?"

"Anything for you."

And then I took her.

It's not clean. People think it's clean, like stepping through a door. It's like climbing into a sleeping bag that someone else is already in. You have to push. You have to fold them down. You have to make room. Her one remaining eye was the hardest part, the eye is where the mind looks out, and she was looking out of it so hard, even under the song, even saying yes, some little part of her in there was looking out and screaming. So I took it out. The eye. I cut it out with her own hand.

She screamed. I grew two new ones in the holes, blue, glowing, mine. Her skin went teal. Her hair went white. And then we both said, at the same time, from the same mouth, "Thanks for the vessel!"

And I laughed. And I hated that I laughed. I didn't think it was funny. It just came out. That's what I've learned about myself since. Whatever I feel, something else comes out.

The blonde girl, Ena, she was still running. She looked back over her shoulder at me, at us, at the teal thing wearing her friend, and her face was so full of horror that I said the truest thing I had.

"Look, I didn't ask to be made. If I don't have a vessel, this body will die, and I just was born. I CANNOT DIE!! I'm too evil, I would go to purgatory. I don't want to die."

She didn't stop running. I don't blame her. I would have run from me.



Chapter Three: Eri Is Still In Here

Here's the thing nobody tells you about vessels. The person doesn't leave.

You fold them down. You squash them small. But they're still there, under the floor of the mind, like someone locked in a basement. And they knock.

Eri knocked all the time.

Day one, in her body, I walked out of the Nursery through a gap in the wall where the war had blown it open, out into the Gray Abyss of Shattered Ruins, the junkyard of the two great wars. Roads stacked on top of buildings. Traffic signs flying like flags. A dog with a cat's head picking through rubble. And under everything, the whole time, knock knock knock.

You fucking parasite.

That was her voice. Not out loud. Inside. Under.

You fucking parasite, give it back, give it BACK.

"I'm borrowing it," I said, out loud, to nobody, to the dog with the cat head.

You cut out my eye.

"You only had the one. I needed both. It was a whole thing."

I will claw my way up through you and I will make you watch while I put you in the ground.

"Okay," I said. "Get in line." And I kept walking, and I hummed to drown her out, and that helped a little, my humming. I didn't know any songs. I just had the feeling of one, from the dead ones. Something someone used to sing to someone, in a war, before.

By day three the body started to go wrong.

It's because it's hers. It knows it's hers. A body knows who it belongs to, deep down in the meat. And every hour I was in it, it rejected me a little more, like a kidney that doesn't take. My teal skin started flaking at the knuckles. My new blue eyes started to cloud at the edges. My white hair came out in the brush (I found a brush in the rubble; I'm not an animal). My fingertips went numb. Then my lips.

And Eri, under the floor, laughed.

Clock's ticking, parasite.

Three days to fall apart in my first body. Maybe two weeks in hers, at this rate. Then the door. Then the grey. Then forever.

I sat down in the shadow of an upside-down bus and I cried, and something else came out instead. I laughed. I laughed and laughed until my borrowed ribs hurt.



Chapter Four: Wren

I found the humans on day five.

There weren't supposed to be any left. Everybody said so. The anti-gods, the corrupted, the whispering ones in the rubble. Earth is gone. The last humans died with the resistance. But there's always more than everybody says. There's always a few. Rats and cockroaches and humans. You can't get rid of them all.

There were eleven of them, living in a parking garage under what used to be a mall, in a spot where the wars had twisted the air so badly that nothing hunting for them could see straight. They'd strung up tarps and tin cans for an alarm. They had a garden of something pale and starchy growing in a shopping cart under a cracked skylight. They had a little boy, eight or nine, named Pip, who drew dinosaurs on the concrete with charcoal.

And they had Wren.

Wren was the one who found me, sitting in the dirt by the tin cans, falling apart. She was maybe twenty-five. Brown skin, shaved head, a scar across her lip, a canvas bag of bandages, a pipe wrench she held like she'd used it on things that weren't pipes. She looked at me, teal, peeling, two glowing eyes, and she didn't scream or run. She crouched down.

"You're hurt," she said.

"I'm dying," I said, which was true, and she heard it as true. You can hear when something's true. That's the worst part. Truth sounds so much like a lie.

"Are you one of them?" she said. "The things from the Nursery?"

And I could have said yes. I didn't. Something reached out of me, the way it always does, found the soft part of her that wanted to help, and pressed. Just a little.

"You know me," I said. "From before. I helped your people get out of the city, remember? When the corruption came. I held the door."

Her eyes went blue at the edges. "You held the door," she said. "Oh my god. You held the door."

She took me inside.

It was so easy. That's what I want you to understand. It's always so easy. I said one thing and suddenly I had a history with her, a whole history, she was filling it in herself, adding details I never gave her, the way you do with a dream when you tell it. By that night she'd told the other ten that I was the one who saved her and Pip when the city fell. By the next morning, two of the others remembered it too. They'd never seen me. They just believed her.

And under my floor, Eri said: You're doing it again.

"Doing what."

Making someone love you so you can eat them.

"I'm not going to eat her."

Yeah, Eri said. Sure.



Chapter Five: The Lullaby

Wren rebandaged my hands every night. The gauze was gray from being washed too many times. She'd sit me on an overturned bucket by the skylight and peel off yesterday's, wincing at what came off with it, and wrap new ones, and talk.

She talked about before. She'd been a vet tech. Dogs, cats, once an iguana with a bladder stone that she still talked about like a war story. Her mom had been a church singer. Pip was her brother, much younger, born when her mom was older, and their mom died in the first week of the corruption, and Wren carried Pip out of the city on her back.

"With your help," she said, smiling, tying off the gauze. "You held the door."

"I held the door," I said.

I didn't hold any door. I was in a tank. I wasn't born yet.

She sang to Pip at night. That was the thing that got me. Her mom's song, some old hymn I didn't know the words to, about a river and a far shore. She sang it with her back against a concrete pillar and Pip asleep across her legs and the tin cans clinking softly in the wind, and her voice was rough and low and not very good, and the dead ones in me went quiet to listen.

That was the song. That was the feeling of the song I'd been humming since I was born. Not this exact one. But something like it. Someone, a long time ago, in the first war, before they were cooked down and poured into me, used to sit with somebody's head in their lap and sing about a far shore.

I started to cry. Real crying, for once, nothing else came out instead.

Wren looked over at me and smiled with the scar on her lip and kept singing.

That night, for the first time since I was born, I didn't think about the door.



Chapter Six: The Empty Ones

I tried. I need you to know that I tried.

By week two Eri's body was really going. Teal skin coming off in sheets now. One finger gone black. Walking hurt. Breathing hurt. My eyes were so cloudy I could only see light and shapes, and the shapes were mostly Wren, bending over me with gauze.

Not long now, Eri said, from under the floor. She sounded tired too. Like it was hurting her. It probably was. It was her body I was ruining. I'm going to be so happy to see you go.

"Where will you go?" I asked her once. "When I'm gone and it's just you in here and the body's done?"

She didn't answer for a while.

Same place as you, probably, she said. Hell, if I'm lucky. Purgatory if I'm not.

"Then why are you happy?"

Because you'll be there too. And I'll find you.

So I tried. I left the parking garage in the middle of the night without telling Wren, and I limped back to the Nursery, back through the gap in the wall, back into the rows of tanks.

The empty ones were still there. Hundreds of them. Standing in their lines. Sapient but not sentient. Waiting to be told what to be. Bodies without anyone in them. I thought: here. Here's my vessel. Nobody's home. Nobody's knocking. Nobody's sister or brother. Just meat. I can take one of these and nobody is hurt and Eri gets her body back and I go back to Wren and I never do anything wrong again.

I picked a tall one with dark hair. I put my hand on its chest. I pushed.

It's like climbing into a coffin. That's the only way I can say it. It's cold, and it's empty, and it fits you all the way around, and there's nothing in there to hold onto. The empty ones don't want anything. I found that out with my whole self pressed into it. My power doesn't work on something that doesn't want. There's no soft part to press. There's no door. You just fall in, and there's no floor, and you keep falling. I felt myself start to go out like a match. Right there in the tall empty body, I felt the edges of me go grey. I saw the waiting room. I saw it. A long hallway with chairs and nobody coming.

I tore myself back out so hard I knocked the empty one over and it lay on the floor with its eyes open, not caring.

I crawled back into Eri. She screamed. I screamed. We screamed together in the dark Nursery, the same mouth, and then something else came out of me and it was a laugh, of course it was.

So that's the answer. You can't wear an empty person. You need someone who wants something. You need someone with a song in them.

And I only knew one person with a song in them.



Chapter Seven: Anything

I went back to the garage. It took me two days on what was left of Eri's legs. Wren found me in the tin cans again, like the first time, and she didn't ask where I'd been. She just picked me up. She carried me inside on her back, like she carried Pip out of the city, and put me down on the good mattress, the one they all took turns on, and started cutting off the bandages.

"Oh, Siren," she said. "Oh, honey, it's so bad."

"I know."

"What can I do? Tell me what to do. I'll do anything."

There it was. Anything. That word. Every single person I've ever touched says that word eventually, and they always mean it, and it's always because I made them mean it, and I sat there on the mattress with my eyes clouded over and I thought very hard about whether this time was different.

Did she mean it because of me? Or did she mean it because she's Wren?

I couldn't tell. That's the real horror of being me, if you want to know. Not the body stuff. Not the door. It's that I can never, ever tell. Every kindness anyone has ever done for me, I don't know if it was real. I made them remember things that never happened. I can't un-make it. I don't know where I end and they start.

I could have told her the truth.

I had it all ready. I'd practiced it on the walk back. I'm not who you think. I never held a door. I'm a thing from the Nursery and I'm wearing a girl I mutilated and I put a lie in your head so you'd take care of me. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. Let me go. Let me die. Sing the river song while I do, if you can stand to.

That's what a good person would have done. I know that. You know that. Eri, under the floor, was screaming it at me.

Tell her. TELL HER. Tell her the truth, you coward, you fucking coward, be one thing in your whole life that isn't a lie.

And I looked at Wren, at her shaved head and her scarred lip and her gray gauze and her terrified, loving face, and I thought about the hallway with the chairs and nobody coming.

"Wren," I said. "You'd do anything for me. Right?"

The blue came up in her eyes.

"Anything," she said.

"Even let me in?"

"Yes."



Chapter Eight: The Far Shore

It was easier than Eri. That's the worst part. She didn't fight. Her mind opened like a hand.

I didn't have to take her eyes. I didn't have to fold her down very far. There was room. She'd made room for me. She'd been making room for me for two weeks, every night on the bucket by the skylight, and I just walked into it and closed the door behind me and there I was, warm, alive, breathing with lungs that didn't hurt, in a body that worked.

Her skin didn't go teal. I think because she said yes. I think because she let me. Her eyes went blue, just a little, at the very edges, the kind of blue you'd only notice if you were looking for it.

And under the floor, very quietly, a new voice.

Siren? Wren said. Siren, what's happening? It's dark. Where's Pip?

I didn't answer her. I'm learning.

Eri's body fell off me like a coat. It lay on the mattress, teal and peeling and blind, and then, slowly, it took a breath on its own. Then another. Its fingers twitched. Its empty eye sockets squeezed. And from that ruined mouth came a croak.

"You," Eri said. Out loud, in her own throat, for the first time in two weeks. "You did it. You actually did it."

"You got your body back," I said, in Wren's voice. "You're welcome."

"I'm going to kill you."

"You can't see."

"I'll find you." She was crawling now, feeling across the mattress with her black finger and her good ones. "I'll find you, I swear on everything, in this world or the grey or wherever they send you, I will find you, Siren."

"Okay," I said. And I stood up in Wren's strong legs, and I picked Eri up, she was so light, so ruined, and I carried her out to the tin cans and set her down in the dirt where Wren first found me, and I walked back inside.

She did find me, later. A long time later. But that's a different story, and it's not mine.



Chapter Nine: Pip

Pip was asleep on the concrete by the pillar when I came back in, curled around his charcoal and a half-drawn stegosaurus.

He woke up when I sat down. He rubbed his eyes. He looked at me.

I waited. My whole new heart was banging. I thought, kids know. Kids always know. Dogs and kids. He's going to look at me and see the blue at the edges and he's going to scream and he's going to run and all the others will wake up and I'll have to do the thing to all eleven of them, one at a time, and I'll do it, I know I'll do it, I've learned that about myself now too.

Pip yawned. He crawled over and put his head in my lap.

"Wren," he said, sleepy. "Sing the river one."

Under the floor, Wren started screaming. Not words. Just screaming. Pip's name, over and over, from a basement nobody can hear.

I looked down at him. His dinosaur drawings on the concrete. The tarp moving in the wind. The tin cans clinking.

I didn't know the words. I'd heard it a dozen times but I never learned the words, because they were Wren's mom's words and Wren's words and not mine. Nothing is mine. I was born out of borrowed parts and I'll live in borrowed bodies and I'll lie to everybody I meet so they'll love me long enough to let me in, and then I'll find the next one, and the next one, forever, because I would rather do this forever than walk through the door one time.

That's the choice. I made it. I'd make it again.

I put my hand on Pip's hair, the way I'd watched her do it. I opened Wren's mouth. And I hummed.

I got the tune wrong. I always get it wrong. Pip didn't notice. He was already asleep.

Under the floor, Wren stopped screaming. She was listening. Even down there, even now, she was listening for the song.

I hummed it wrong all night.
