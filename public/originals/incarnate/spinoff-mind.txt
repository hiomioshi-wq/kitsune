Chapter One: Whose Thought Is This

Esme woke up at 4:12 in the morning with a thought in her head that was not hers.

It was a small thought. That was what frightened her. Not a scream, not a command, not the vast cold voice that had filled her skull for the eleven days Galactix wore her like a coat. Just a small, neat, quiet thought, sitting in the middle of her mind like a stone in a shoe.

The tea is too hot.

She was not drinking tea. She was lying in bed in the dark. There was no tea.

Esme sat up. She was the anti-god of the mind. She had been able to hear other people's thoughts since she was six years old, the way other people can hear rain on a roof. She had spent her whole life learning to tell the difference: this one is mine, that one is the man next door, that one is the woman down the street dreaming about her dead mother. She could sort a crowded room of thoughts the way a librarian sorts a returned cart of books, fast and without looking.

And now she sat in the dark with one small neat thought about tea, and she could not tell where it had come from.

She reached out. Carefully. The way she had been taught by the old anti-gods, before the wars, before the corruption: open the mind a crack, like a door, and listen.

The apartment building was asleep. Thirty-one minds, dreaming. The baker on the ground floor, already awake, already thinking about flour. A child on the fourth floor having a nightmare about a dog. An old man across the hall dreaming, as he did every night, that he was young again, and running.

None of them were drinking tea.

The tea is too hot, said the thought again, patiently. Wait for it to cool.

Esme put her hands over her face.

It's in me, she thought. It's still in me. I know it is. She left something in me.

And then, worse, so much worse, the small neat voice answered.

Who left something? it asked, mildly curious. And why do you assume it isn't you?


Chapter Two: The Annoyed One

Everyone in the restored world knew the story by now. The anti-god of the mind, possessed by the master Galactix, used as a vessel to rebuild a nexus point; her own power turned against her friends; her neck snapped when the master was done with her. And then the impossible part: Ena and Modify, the brother and sister, going back through master time on a thread of residual corruption, and somewhere in the folding of the timelines, Esme coming back. Whole. Alive. Standing with the other anti-gods at the hermitage when Galactix fell, helping Positron hold the master still.

People told it like a happy ending. Esme had never once corrected them.

She went to see Ena.

Ena lived alone, at the edge of the restored world, in a house that kept glitching slightly at the corners, the way everything near Ena glitched. She opened the door before Esme knocked. Her silver hair was loose and her eyes flickered red in the grey dawn.

"You're awake early," said Ena.

"Don't," said Esme. "Don't do the friendly thing. I can hear you thinking about how I look terrible."

"I wasn't going to say it."

"You don't have to say it. That's the whole problem with me." Esme pushed past her into the house and sat down at the kitchen table, which was flickering very slightly in and out of existence. "Make me tea. Not too hot."

Ena made tea. She brought it to the table and sat down across from Esme and waited. She was very good at waiting. She had waited for eons, after Modify died, at his monument.

"There's something in my head," said Esme.

Ena went still.

"It's not her," said Esme quickly. "At least, I don't think it's her. It's not loud. It doesn't tell me to do anything. It just—comments. On things. Small things. This morning it told me my tea was too hot. I wasn't drinking tea." She stared down into the cup in front of her. "And then it asked me why I assumed it wasn't me."

"Is it you?" said Ena quietly.

"I don't know." Esme's voice cracked on the last word, and she hated that, hated it, hated how it sounded. "That's the thing. I don't know. I'm the anti-god of the mind. Knowing which thoughts are mine is the only thing I've ever been really good at. When Galactix was in me, I could still tell. Every second. I could feel exactly where she ended and I began, like a seam. It was horrible, but at least I knew. Now there's no seam. Now there's just—thoughts. And some of them sound like me and some of them don't, and I can't feel the edge anymore."

Ena reached across the flickering table and put her hand over Esme's.

"When I was corrupted," she said, "I couldn't tell either. I'd hurt someone, and then I'd cry, and then I'd laugh, and I didn't know which one was me. I still don't, some days. Residual corruption still has my voice sometimes. I hear myself saying things and I think: was that me? Or was that what's left of it?"

"How do you live with that?"

Ena considered.

"Mods used to say," she said slowly, "that you could always tell it was really me, because I said sorry afterward. Every time. Even when I was the worst I ever was, I always came back and said sorry. He said corruption never apologized. Only Ena did." She smiled, a little crooked. "So when I'm not sure, I check. Am I sorry? If I'm sorry, it's me."

Esme thought about this for a long time.

Then she said, "I'm never sorry. I'm just annoyed."

Ena laughed. It was the first time Esme had heard her laugh in a year.

"Then maybe," said Ena, "that's how you'll know."


Chapter Three: The Library of Everyone

The voice came back that night. And the next. And the next.

It did not get louder. It did not get crueler. That was the frightening thing about it. If it had raged, if it had threatened, Esme would have known it for an enemy. Instead it was quiet and calm and reasonable, and it noticed things.

That man across the street is lonely, it said one evening, as Esme walked home. He keeps looking at the bakery window but he never goes in. You should tell him the baker would like company.

The child on the fourth floor is afraid of dogs because one bit her when she was three, it said another night. She doesn't remember why she's afraid. You could take the fear out. Gently. She'd never know.

Your friend Rui misses her braid, it said, while Esme sat with Rui and Tsui on the cliff by the sea, and Rui laughed at something Tsui said. She cut it off years ago and she still reaches for it when she's nervous. Look. There. She just did it.

Rui had just done it. Her hand had gone up to the back of her neck, to the place where a braid used to hang, and come away empty.

Esme stood up so fast she knocked over her chair.

"I have to go," she said.

"Esme?" said Tsui. "Are you okay?"

"I'm fine. I'm—I have to go."

She walked down the cliff path so fast she nearly fell. At the bottom she sat on the black rocks where the sea came in and pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw colors.

"Stop it," she whispered. "Stop it. Stop reading them. I don't read my friends. I never read my friends. That's a rule. That's my rule."

Is it? said the voice mildly. Or is it a rule you made because you were afraid of what you'd find?

"Get out of my head."

I'm not in your head, said the voice. I'm only where everyone is. In the place where minds meet. You used to go there all the time, when you were young. Do you remember? Before the war. Before you learned to keep your door shut.

And then, before Esme could stop it, the rocks were gone, and the sea was gone, and she was standing in a library.

It was enormous. It went up and up and up, shelf after shelf after shelf, into a darkness that might have been a ceiling or might have been sky. The shelves were full of books, but the books were not books. They were thoughts. Every one of them. Bright, flickering, alive, whispering to each other on the shelves. Some glowed blue and some glowed gold and some glowed a soft, sick grey. Some of them were tiny, no bigger than a matchbox. Some were so large they took up whole shelves on their own.

Esme knew this place. She had not been here since she was nine.

The old anti-gods had called it the Library of Everyone. The place where every mind in a world brushed against every other, the way branches brush in a forest. Most anti-gods of the mind visited it once, as children, and were so frightened by it that they never came back. Esme had come back. Three times. And on the third time she had gotten lost among the shelves for two days and come out speaking in her grandmother's voice, and her mother had cried, and that was when Esme had learned to keep her door shut.

"Why am I here?" she said. Her voice echoed up into the dark.

Because this is where I live now, said the voice. And it was not in her head anymore. It was in front of her, at the end of the aisle, where the shelves opened out into a round reading room lit by a single grey lamp.

Someone was sitting in the reading room.

Someone small, in a grey dress, with her back to Esme, reading.


Chapter Four: The Vessel's Ghost

"Galactix," said Esme.

The figure in the grey dress turned a page.

"No," it said. "She's gone. Your friends did that. They sealed her in a pocket dimension where her powers are void. She's not coming back. Not ever. I'd know."

"Then who are you?"

The figure closed the book and turned around in its chair.

It had Esme's face.

Not exactly. Younger. Paler. The eyes were wrong: not Esme's brown, but a deep, starry, shifting blue, the color Esme's eyes had glowed for eleven days when the master was wearing her. And the expression was wrong too. Esme had never in her life looked that calm.

"I'm what grew in the dent," said the thing with her face. "When Galactix wore you, she pressed down on your mind. Hard. For a long time. When she left, there was a hollow there, shaped like her. Minds don't like hollows. They fill them. And something filled this one." It shrugged. "Me. I'm made of you. Your memories, your power, your voice. But I grew in her shape. So I think a little like she did. Quiet. Patient. Interested in everyone." It smiled, very slightly. "You could say I'm your sister."

"I don't want a sister."

"Nobody asks for sisters," said the thing. "Ask Rui."

Esme's hands curled into fists.

"What do you want?"

The thing tilted its head, considering, the way Esme sometimes tilted hers when she was about to explain something to Tim for the third time.

"I want to help," it said.

"Help with what?"

"Everyone." It gestured at the shelves rising around them, the millions of glowing thoughts whispering to each other in the dark. "Look at them. All of them, in pain. The child afraid of dogs. The lonely man at the bakery window. Rui reaching for a braid that isn't there. Ena standing at her brother's tower every evening, thinking it was her fault, it was her fault, it was her fault. Do you know how many minds in this restored world are still screaming about the war? Thousands. Thousands, Esme. And you could fix them."

"I don't fix people."

"You could," the thing repeated. "Gently. A little at a time. Take the fear out of the child. Put a little courage into the lonely man. Smooth over Ena's guilt, just a little, so it stops cutting her. You know how. You've always known how. You just never let yourself." It leaned forward. Its starry eyes glittered. "Galactix wanted to rule minds. I don't. I only want to heal them. Isn't that what a mind anti-god is for?"

Esme stood very still in the reading room.

And the worst part, the very worst part, was that it sounded right.


Chapter Five: Eri

She went to hell.

Not all the way. Not into the collapsed part, the ruins, where the portals opened and the fire never stopped. Just to the edge of it, where the residual corruption made the ground soft and glitchy, and where, if you knew how to listen, you could find the one creature in any dimension who understood minds the way Esme did.

Eri found her first. She always did.

The demon of the mind came up out of the glitching ground like smoke rising, her horns curling, her fly-like wings folded against her back, the old flame-shaped scar on her face glowing faintly. She looked at Esme for a long moment.

"Oh," said Eri. "Oh, you've got one too."

Esme sat down on a rock.

"You can see it?"

"Can I see it?" Eri laughed, a high, ragged laugh. "Sweetheart, I'm a demon of the mind. I can see it sitting in your skull like a cat in a window. Little grey thing. Starry eyes. Very polite." She crouched down in front of Esme. "Let me guess. It wants to help."

Esme stared at her.

"How do you know that?"

Eri's smile faded. She sat down on the soft ground beside Esme's rock, wings rustling.

"Because I had one," she said. "Long time ago. Before Tombstone. Before the corruption. When I was still an anti-god, not a demon. A master wore me, once, the way Galactix wore you. And when it left, something grew in the hollow. And it was so reasonable. It was so kind. It wanted to fix everyone." She stared out across the glitching ground. "I let it."

"What happened?"

"I fixed everyone," said Eri. "Every mind in my realm. A little at a time. Took the fear out. Put the courage in. Smoothed the guilt. And they were all so happy. For a while." She was quiet for a moment. "And then I noticed they'd stopped arguing. Stopped changing. Stopped growing. Every one of them had become a little bit more like the version of them that I thought was best. And none of them knew. And none of them could ever, ever find out, because I'd taken out the part of them that would have been angry."

The ground glitched. Somewhere far away, in the depths of hell, a portal opened and closed.

"That's why I'm a demon now," said Eri. "Not because I'm evil. Because I did the kindest thing in the world, to everyone, without asking. That's the most evil thing there is, Esme. Kindness nobody gets to say no to."

Esme put her face in her hands.

"How do I get rid of it?"

"You can't get rid of it," said Eri. "It's you. It's made of you. If you rip it out, you'll rip out half your own mind with it. I've seen anti-gods try. They don't come back."

"Then what do I do?"

Eri looked at her sideways. Her scar glowed.

"Tell me something," she said. "When it talks to you. When it says, the child is afraid of dogs, you could fix it. What do you feel?"

Esme thought about it.

"Tempted," she said honestly.

"And under that?"

Esme thought harder.

"Annoyed," she said. "Really, really annoyed. Like when Tim asks me what a nexus is for the fourth time. Like, I already told you. Pay attention. Do it yourself."

Eri began to laugh.

She laughed so hard her wings shook, and the ground under her glitched, and the old scar on her face flared bright and then went dim, and when she was done she wiped her eyes and looked at Esme with something that was almost fondness.

"Oh, you're going to be fine," she said. "You are going to be absolutely fine. Hold onto that. The annoyed part. That's the part that grew back first. That's the part it doesn't have."


Chapter Six: Do It Yourself

The thing with Esme's face was waiting for her in the reading room.

"You went to see Eri," it said. "That was clever. She's had one of me."

"She told me what happened when she listened to it."

"She told you her side," said the thing. "She didn't tell you how many people stopped crying because of her. How many children slept through the night for the first time. How many widows stopped waking up screaming." It folded its hands in its lap. "She thinks she ruined them. But they were happy, Esme. Truly happy. Isn't that worth something?"

"Were they happy," said Esme, "or did they just stop being able to tell that they weren't?"

The thing with her face did not answer.

Esme walked into the reading room. She did not sit down. She stood over the small grey figure in the chair, with her arms crossed, the way she stood over Tim when he was being slow, the way she had stood over the whole resistance when she explained what a nexus point was and nobody listened.

"Here's what I figured out," she said. "On the way back from hell. Everything you say sounds like me. Your voice, your words, your memories. You know everything I know. But there's one thing you don't have, and Eri saw it right away, and I should have seen it too."

"And what's that?"

"You're patient," said Esme. "You're so patient. You're calm and gentle and you notice everything and you'd never in a million years lose your temper. You're the version of me that the master would have liked." She leaned down until her face was inches from its starry eyes. "I'm not patient. I've never been patient in my life. When people don't understand things, I get annoyed, and I explain it again, annoyed, and then they understand it themselves, and that's the only way anyone ever learns anything. Because I make them do it."

The thing blinked.

"The child afraid of dogs," said Esme. "You want me to reach in and take the fear out. And she'd never know. And she'd never be scared again. And she'd also never find out she can be braver than a dog. Because I did it for her." She straightened up. "The lonely man. You want me to give him courage. And then he'd go into the bakery, and talk to the baker, and they'd be friends. And he'd never know the courage wasn't his. Every friend he made for the rest of his life, he'd have made with my courage instead of his own. And Ena." Her voice dropped. "You want me to smooth over Ena's guilt. Ena, who spent ten thousand years becoming someone who says sorry. Who built her whole self out of being sorry. You want me to sand that off her. Because it hurts her."

"It does hurt her."

"Of course it hurts her!" Esme shouted. The shelves around them shuddered. Thousands of glowing thoughts flickered like candles in a draft. "It's supposed to hurt! It's hers! She earned it! Who the hell am I to take it away?"

The reading room was very quiet.

"So what will you do instead?" said the thing at last. "Just let them suffer? Just stand there with all that power, hearing every mind in the world, and do nothing?"

Esme took a breath.

"No," she said. "I'm going to do the most annoying thing I can possibly think of."

"Which is what?"

"I'm going to ask them," said Esme.


Chapter Seven: Asking

It took her a month.

She went to the child on the fourth floor first. She knocked on the door, and the child's mother opened it, and Esme said, awkwardly, because she had never done anything like this before, "Hi. I live upstairs. I'm—I'm the anti-god of the mind. I can hear when people are scared. Your daughter's scared of dogs. Did you know?"

The mother stared at her.

"She's been scared of dogs since she was three," said Esme. "Because one bit her. She doesn't remember it. I could help her remember. If she wanted. Not take it away. Just help her see where it comes from. So she can decide what to do about it." She shifted from foot to foot. "Or not. Totally up to her. I'm just. Offering. This is so weird. I'm going to go."

The mother caught her arm.

"Come in," she said. "Ask her."

Esme went in. And she sat on the floor of the child's bedroom, and she asked. And the child, who was seven, and very serious, thought about it for a long time and then said yes. And Esme reached into the child's mind, very gently, not to take anything out, but only to hold up a lamp, and show her the old memory sitting in the dark: a big dog, a hot summer day, a sharp pain, a scream.

"Oh," said the child. "Oh. That's why."

"That's why," said Esme.

"I was so little," said the child.

"You were tiny."

The child thought about it some more. Then she said, "I'm bigger now."

"You're bigger now," Esme agreed.

The child went to the window and looked down at the street, where a large brown dog was trotting past with its owner. She stared at it for a long time.

"I'm still scared," she said finally. "But it's my scared now. I know where it lives." She turned around. "Thank you for not just taking it."

Esme found that she could not speak.

She went to the lonely man next. She stood beside him at the bakery window and said, "You want to go in, don't you?" And he jumped, and stared at her, and said, "How do you know?" And she said, "I'm the anti-god of the mind, it's very annoying, I know," and he laughed, and she said, "I'm not going to make you. But I'll stand here while you think about it." And they stood there together for twenty minutes, and then the man said, "Oh, hell," and went in, and Esme watched through the window as he bought a loaf of bread and fumbled the change and the baker laughed, and he laughed, and they kept talking.

It was his. All of it. His courage. She had not touched it at all.

She went to Rui and told her about the braid. Rui stared at her and then burst out laughing and said, "You read me? You never read your friends!" and Esme said, "I know, I'm so sorry, it's a whole thing, there's a ghost in my head," and Rui said, "Tell me everything," and she did, sitting on the cliff by the sea while Tsui made tea that was not too hot.

And then she went to Ena.

Ena was at the tower. She was always at the tower in the evenings. She stood at the base of it with her silver hair blowing, looking up at the one name, MODIFY, and the line beneath it that somebody had carved in the night with a chisel: AND EVERYONE WHO STOOD BESIDE HIM.

"I can hear you," said Esme, coming up beside her. "Thinking it's your fault. Every night. You know that, right? I can always hear you."

"I know," said Ena.

"I could make it stop," said Esme. "If you wanted. I've got this thing in my head now. It wants to fix everyone. I could just reach in and smooth it over. You'd never feel it again."

Ena was quiet for a long time, looking up at the tower.

"No," she said at last.

"Okay."

"It's mine," said Ena. "It's the last thing I have that he gave me. Mods. He taught me how to be sorry. If I stop feeling it, I stop being the person he saved." She turned and looked at Esme, and her eyes flickered red in the dusk. "Thank you for asking. Nobody ever asked me anything, when I was corrupted. It just happened to me. Thank you for asking."

Esme stood beside her at the foot of the tower until the sun went down. Neither of them said anything else. Neither of them needed to.


Chapter Eight: The Reading Room, Last Time

When she went back to the Library of Everyone, the thing with her face was not sitting in the reading room anymore.

It was standing at the shelves. Looking at the glowing thoughts, the millions of them, whispering in the dark. As Esme came down the aisle, it turned, and she saw that its eyes had changed. Not quite brown. Not quite starry blue. Something in between.

"You asked them," it said.

"I asked them."

"And they said no. Most of them."

"Most of them," said Esme. "The child said yes. Sort of. She said yes to seeing. Not to fixing."

The thing was quiet.

"I don't understand," it said at last. "I offered them everything. Peace. No more fear. No more guilt. And you went to every one of them and gave them a choice, and almost all of them chose to keep hurting." It looked up at the endless shelves. "Why?"

Esme came and stood beside it.

"Because it's theirs," she said. "That's all. It's not complicated. It's just theirs. The scared and the sorry and the lonely. It's theirs, and they get to decide what to do with it, and if I take it, even to help, even to be kind, then I'm Galactix. I'm a master. I'm wearing them." She shoved her hands in her pockets. "You grew in the shape of a master. You can't help thinking like one. Everything you want is kind. But you want it for them, instead of with them."

The thing looked at her.

"Then what am I for?" it said quietly. "If I can't fix anyone. If you won't let me help. What's left of me?"

Esme had been thinking about this all month. On the cliff with Rui. In the bakery window. At the foot of the tower.

"You notice things," she said. "That's the part of you I don't have. I'm too impatient. I'm always rushing ahead, annoyed, explaining. I don't see the man at the window. I don't see Rui reaching for her braid. I don't see the child at all. You see everything. Quietly. Patiently. You saw every one of them before I did." She turned to face it. "So stay. Stay and notice. And tell me. And then I'll do the annoying part. I'll go and ask."

The thing with her face stood very still.

"You want me to stay?"

"I want you to stop trying to drive," said Esme. "But yeah. Stay. Ride along. Point things out. We'll take turns."

The thing looked at her for a long, long moment. Then, very slowly, something happened to its face that Esme had never seen it do before.

It frowned. Just a little. A small crease between the eyebrows.

"That's going to be very annoying," it said. "Having to wait for you to ask every single time."

Esme stared at it.

Then she laughed, so loudly that every glowing thought on every shelf in the Library of Everyone flared at once, bright as a sunrise.

"There it is," she said. "There it is. Welcome to being me."


Chapter Nine: The Seam

She woke up at 4:12 in the morning with a thought in her head.

The baker downstairs is up early. He's worried about the oven. It's been making a noise.

Esme lay in the dark and listened.

It was not her thought. She could feel that now, the faint edge of it, like a seam in fabric. It was the quiet one's thought. Its voice. Patient and calm and noticing. But there was a seam between them now, clear and clean, and she could feel exactly where it ended and she began.

It was not a scar. It was a stitch.

Should we go down? said the quiet voice. He might want help with the oven. Or he might not. You'll have to ask.

"Ugh," said Esme out loud, to the ceiling. "It's four in the morning."

You said we'd take turns, said the voice. I noticed. Your turn.

Esme groaned and got up. She put on her coat. She went downstairs in the dark, past thirty-one dreaming minds, past the child on the fourth floor who was dreaming, for the first time in four years, about a dog that was friendly. She knocked on the baker's door.

He opened it, flour on his hands, surprised.

"Hi," said Esme. "I'm the anti-god of the mind. It's a whole thing. I heard your oven's making a noise. Do you want help? You can say no."

The baker blinked at her.

Then he laughed.

"Do you know," he said, "I've been listening to that noise for three days and wondering if I'm going mad. Yes. Please. Come in. I'll make you tea."

"Not too hot," said Esme, and stepped inside.

And far down in her mind, in the Library of Everyone, in a round reading room lit by a lamp that was no longer grey but a soft and ordinary gold, something with almost her face settled back into a chair, picked up a book, and smiled.

Not patient. Not quite.

But close enough to wait.
