Chapter One: The Girl Who Never Said Anything

Pia Lund had not said what she meant in four years.

She kept a list. Not on paper, because paper could be found. In her head. Every time someone said something to her that she wanted to answer and didn't, she added it to the list, the way other people add coins to a jar.

Her mother, at breakfast: Why can't you be more like your cousin? Pia's answer, swallowed: Because my cousin isn't afraid of you. Number one thousand, four hundred and six.

A girl in her class, laughing: Did you cut your hair yourself? Pia's answer, swallowed: Yes, because I couldn't stand the hairdresser asking me questions. Number one thousand, four hundred and seven.

Her history teacher, handing back an essay: This is technically correct but completely without opinion. Pia's answer, swallowed: Opinions are how people find out where to hit you. Number one thousand, four hundred and eight.

She was seventeen. She lived in the restored world, in a narrow grey town a long way from the monument and the sea, in the second year after the end of everything. Most people her age talked about the war constantly. About Modify and Corrupt, about the anti-gods, about Galactix and the broken world and the nine minutes on the western edge. They argued about it, loudly, in the town square after school. Who was the strongest. Who should be on the tower. Whether Corrupt had been a monster or a victim.

Pia never joined in. She had opinions. She had a great many opinions. They went on the list.

On the evening the list got her killed, she was walking home the long way, past the old quarry at the edge of town, because the short way went past the square and she didn't want anyone to look at her. The quarry had been fenced off since the war. Things had come up out of it, people said, during the worst of the corruption. Things that had been people once.

It was getting dark. The fence had a hole in it.

Something was sitting in the hole.

It looked, at first, like a dog. A big, grey, sick-looking dog, curled up in the gap in the wire with its head on its paws. Then it raised its head, and Pia saw that its face had too many eyes, and that its eyes were the faint, flickering, glitching red of residual corruption, and that its mouth went back much, much further than a dog's mouth should.

It looked at her.

Pia did not run. She did not scream. She did the thing she always did when something frightened her, which was to go very still and very quiet and wait for it to lose interest.

It did not lose interest.

"Please," Pia said, which was the first thing she had said out loud in hours, and it came out as barely a whisper. "Please, I'm not—I'm nobody. I'm not worth it."

The thing tilted its head. All its eyes blinked, one after another, like a row of lights going off.

Then it lunged.

She did not feel it bite her. She felt the ground hit her back, and the cold, and a strange enormous tiredness, and she looked down and saw that her coat was wet and dark, much too dark, much too wet. The thing was standing over her, chewing on something. She did not want to know what.

So this is it, thought Pia. And then, absurdly: I never said any of it. Not one. One thousand, four hundred and ninety-one things I never said, and I'm going to die in a quarry and nobody will ever know any of them.

And a voice that was not hers, in the dark at the very back of her mind, said:

How many?

Pia blinked. The sky was going grey at the edges.

"What?" she whispered.

How many things, said the voice, impatient now, rough and low and old as stone, have you not said?

"One thousand, four hundred and ninety-one," said Pia. "Why?"

The voice laughed. It was the worst laugh Pia had ever heard. It sounded like a battlefield when the fighting has stopped.

Girl, it said. That is the biggest arsenal I have seen in ten thousand years. Make a deal with me, and I'll keep you alive.


Chapter Two: The Horseman in the Dent

She woke up in her own bed with no hole in her.

That was the first thing. She sat up and pulled up her shirt and stared at her stomach, which was whole and pale and perfectly ordinary, except for a thin raised line across it, silvery, like an old scar, in the shape of a sword.

The second thing was that her room was wrong.

Not wrong like broken. Wrong like rearranged. Her desk had been pushed against the door. Her chair was wedged under the window. Her hairbrush, her pens, her lamp, and her one stuffed rabbit had been arranged on the floor in a neat, careful line, like soldiers in formation, all facing the door.

"Good," said Pia's mouth. "You're awake. We need to talk about your defenses."

Pia put both hands over her mouth.

It was her voice. It was her mouth. But she had not said it. She had not even thought it. The words had simply come out of her, the way a cough comes out, low and rough and certain.

Calm down, said the voice in the back of her mind. I'm not going to hurt you. If I wanted to hurt you, I'd have let the hound finish eating.

"Who are you?" whispered Pia through her fingers.

There was a pause. When the voice answered, it was almost proud.

I am War.

Pia lowered her hands slowly.

"The Horseman," she said. "War. The Horsemen of the Apocalypse. You were in the war. You were in the stories. You—"

I was in hell, said War. Thank you for remembering. Most people don't. Famine gets a statue. Famine gets songs. Famine gets to be the nexus and hold up the world and be very noble about it. Nobody writes songs about War. They only write songs about wars.

"How are you in my head?"

The way anything gets anywhere these days, said War. Residual corruption. Displacement. Cracks between dimensions. I was weakened, at the end. Badly. I'd spent everything. There wasn't enough of me left to hold a body together. So I drifted. Through the cracks. For two years. Looking for somewhere to land. And then a girl lay dying in a quarry, full to the brim with every fight she never had, and I thought: there. There. That's a home.

Pia stared at the line of objects on her floor. The hairbrush. The lamp. The rabbit.

"You said a deal," she said.

I did.

"What deal?"

I keep you alive, said War. I hold your insides together. I'm holding them together right now. If I let go, you'll be back in that quarry, bleeding. And in return—

"In return what?"

There was a long silence in the back of her mind. When War spoke again, her voice was low and hard and quiet as a blade being drawn.

In return, you help me fight someone.

"Who?"

The strongest thing in this world, said War. The one who ended the last war. The one they all whisper about. I need to know if I can beat her.

"Who?" said Pia again.

Corrupt, said War. Ena. The sister.


Chapter Three: The Arsenal

War explained the rules at school.

It was the worst possible place to have the conversation, and Pia said so, silently, sitting in the back row of her history class with her face carefully blank. War did not care. War had been born, she said, in the first quarrel two people ever had over a piece of meat, and she had no patience for good timing.

Here is what I can do, said War, inside her head, while the teacher droned on about the anti-god wars. I can make weapons. That's all I am, really. Weapons. Every fight that has ever happened has a weapon in it somewhere, even if it's only a word. I can find the weapon in a fight and pull it out and make it real.

Pia wrote that down in her notebook, very small, in the margin: weapon in a fight?

Every argument you never had, said War, is a fight that's still waiting. I can feel them in you. Stacked up. One thousand, four hundred and ninety-one. Each one of them has a weapon in it. I can forge it.

Pia wrote: how?

Watch.

And Pia's right hand, without her moving it, reached into her pocket, closed around nothing, and pulled out a knife.

It was small. A short, plain, grey blade, no longer than her palm, with a handle wrapped in something that looked like old paper. And when Pia looked at it, she knew, with a jolt like missing a stair, exactly which fight it had come from.

Did you cut your hair yourself?

Number one thousand, four hundred and seven.

That's a small one, said War, approvingly. Small fight, small weapon. But they get bigger. The things you swallowed the hardest, the things that cost you the most not to say, those make the best steel. You've got some in you, girl. I can feel them. Things you've been not-saying for years. When I pull those out, they're going to be swords.

Pia put the knife back in her pocket with shaking fingers.

"What happens to them?" she whispered, so quietly that the boy beside her didn't even turn his head. "The arguments. When you make them into weapons. What happens to them?"

They're used up, said War. Gone. You'll never have to say them. Isn't that what you wanted?

Pia didn't answer.

But that night, lying in bed, she tried to remember what she had wanted to say to the girl who laughed at her hair.

And she couldn't.

She remembered the girl. She remembered the laugh. She remembered the hot, stinging feeling in her chest. But the words, the answer she had held onto for three weeks, polishing it, turning it over—gone. As if someone had reached in and taken it out of her. As if it had never been there at all.

There was only a small, clean, empty space, the size and shape of a knife.


Chapter Four: The Square

War wanted to train.

She made Pia go to the quarry, every evening after school, and pull weapons out of her arsenal one by one, and swing them at rocks. Small ones first. A dagger. A hatchet. A pair of brass knuckles that came from the time her uncle told her she'd never amount to anything, at a wedding, in front of everyone, and she'd smiled and said nothing.

That one was satisfying to swing. Pia hated that it was satisfying.

You're getting stronger, said War, on the fifth evening, as Pia stood panting over a boulder she had cracked in half with a short-handled axe. My arms are getting stronger. Your arms. Our arms. In a month, we'll be ready.

"Ready for Ena," said Pia.

Ready for Ena.

"Why?" said Pia. "Why do you want to fight her? The war's over. Everyone says she saved the world. Her and her brother."

There was a long silence in the back of her mind. The quarry was quiet. Somewhere at the bottom of it, a corrupted hound howled, and then thought better of it.

Because I didn't, said War at last.

"Didn't what?"

Save the world. Help save it. Anything. I was in hell when it ended. I'd spent everything I had on a demon in the pit, a big one, a nasty one, I turned it into a halberd and broke the halberd fighting the next thing that came, and by the time I crawled out, it was over. Corrupt and Modify and the anti-gods had done it all without me. They didn't need me. War. The second Horseman. The red one. The one who's supposed to be there at the end of everything. I wasn't even invited.

Pia lowered the axe.

So I want to know, said War, quieter now, if I'd been there. If it would have mattered. If I could have beaten what they beat. And the only way I know how to find that out is to fight the strongest thing that walked out of it.

"That's—" said Pia, and stopped.

That's what?

The answer was right there. The perfect answer. Pia could feel it sitting in her throat: That's the saddest reason to start a fight I've ever heard. You're not trying to win. You're just trying to find out if anyone would have missed you.

She swallowed it.

Number one thousand, four hundred and ninety-two.

And in her right hand, before she could stop it, War pulled a sword.

It was long and slim and grey, and it was the most beautiful weapon Pia had ever seen. Its edge glittered in the dusk. Its hilt was wrapped in something like paper, and on the paper, very faintly, there were words. Pia could almost read them. Almost.

Then they faded, and the sword was only a sword, and the space in her throat where the answer had been was clean and empty and gone.

Oh, that's a good one, breathed War. What was it? What didn't you say?

"I don't remember," said Pia, and her voice cracked. "I don't remember. You took it."


Chapter Five: The Expert

Esme found her.

Pia did not know, at first, who the woman was. Only that she was tall and dark-haired and looked tired, and that she was standing at the edge of the quarry fence one evening with her arms crossed, watching Pia swing a battle-axe at a boulder, and that her eyes were glowing very slightly brown and gold.

"There are two of you," said the woman.

Pia froze with the axe over her head.

"Don't panic," said the woman. "I'm not here to—well, I'm here to do something, I haven't decided what yet, it depends on what you tell me. I'm Esme. The anti-god of the mind. I heard you from three towns over. You're very loud. Both of you." She came through the hole in the fence, stepping carefully around the place where Pia had nearly died. "One of you is screaming. The other one's planning a battle. I couldn't sleep."

Tell her to leave, said War.

"She says to tell you to leave," said Pia.

"She would," said Esme. "She's a Horseman. They never want anyone else in the room." She sat down on a rock with a sigh. "I know what it's like. Having something else in your head. I had a master in mine for eleven days. And then I had something that grew where she'd been, and it's still there, actually, it's very polite, we take turns." She looked at Pia. "Does yours let you take turns?"

Pia lowered the axe. She found, suddenly, that she was crying.

"No," she said. "No. She takes things. She takes the things I didn't say and she makes them into weapons and then they're gone. I don't know what they were anymore. I can't remember. She took my answer to my uncle. She took my answer to the girl who laughed at me. She took—" Her breath hitched. "She took something I was going to say to her, I think. Last night. And I can't remember what it was, and I think it was important, and I'll never know."

Esme was quiet for a long moment.

Then she reached out, very slowly, and took Pia's hand.

"Can I look?" she said. "You can say no."

Pia stared at her. Nobody had ever asked her that. About anything.

"Yes," she said.

Esme's eyes glowed. And Pia felt, for a moment, something cool and careful move through her mind, like a hand going along a shelf in the dark, not taking anything, only touching.

Then Esme let go, and sat back, and her face was strange.

"They're not gone," she said.

"What?"

"The things she took. The arguments. They're not gone. They're down there. In her. In the part of your mind that's hers now." Esme looked at Pia with something that was almost wonder. "She's been keeping them. All of them. One thousand, four hundred and ninety-two. Stacked up neat in the dark. Every single thing you never said." She shook her head. "Pia, she hasn't been throwing them away. She's been saving them."


Chapter Six: What War Keeps

That's private, said War. That was private.

Pia sat on her bed in the dark, with her knees pulled up and her arms around them.

"Why?" she said. "Why are you keeping them?"

No answer.

"You said they were used up. You said I'd never have to say them. You said they were gone."

I said that, agreed War. Her voice was very quiet. Quieter than Pia had ever heard it.

"So why?"

The silence went on for so long that Pia thought War had gone, somehow, slipped back out through the cracks between dimensions and left her alone. And then, very low, so low it was almost a whisper:

Because they're the best thing about you.

Pia did not move.

I've been in a great many people, girl, said War. Ten thousand years of generals and kings and soldiers and murderers. Every one of them said everything. Shouted it. Screamed it. Every thought they had came straight out of their mouths and turned into a fight. I was never hungry in any of them. There was never anything to forge. It all just spilled out onto the ground and was gone.

You're the first one, said War, who kept it. Every cruel thing someone said to you. Every unfair thing. Every time someone hurt you and expected you to hurt them back. You kept it all, and you held it, and you didn't throw it at anyone. Do you know how rare that is? Do you know how strong you have to be to hold one thousand, four hundred and ninety-two fights in your chest for four years and never once let them go?

"It's not strong," whispered Pia. "It's scared."

Same thing, said War. From where I'm standing. Scared and strong look identical, from inside a body. They both hold still.

Pia pressed her face against her knees.

When I forge them, said War, I take them out of your memory, but I keep them. Down here. In my part. I didn't want you to have to carry them anymore. But I didn't want to throw them away either. Because they're yours. They're the best steel I've ever had. And I thought—

War stopped.

"Thought what?"

I thought, said War, very quietly, that if I kept them, then when we fought Corrupt, I could bring them all out at once. Every one. The biggest arsenal in ten thousand years. And whatever happened, whether I won or lost, at least everybody would see. Everybody would see what you'd been holding. Everybody would finally know.

Pia lifted her head.

"You wanted to fight her," she said slowly, "so people would see me?"

I wanted to fight her to find out if I mattered, said War. And then somewhere along the way it got mixed up with wanting people to find out that you matter. I don't know when. I'm War. I'm not very good at telling the difference between those two things.

Pia sat in the dark for a long, long time.

Then she said, "Give it back."

What?

"The sword. The one from last night. The one I was going to say to you." Pia uncurled her arms. "Give it back. Not as a sword. As what it was. I want to say it."

War said nothing.

"Please," said Pia. "I've never asked for anything. In four years. I'm asking."

And slowly, slowly, something rose up out of the dark at the back of her mind, the way a fish rises out of deep water. Not a sword. A sentence. Shining, sharp-edged, whole. Exactly as she had held it in her throat at the edge of the quarry, the night before.

Pia opened her mouth, and for the first time in four years, she said what she meant.

"That's the saddest reason to start a fight I've ever heard," she said out loud, to the empty room. "You're not trying to win. You're just trying to find out if anyone would have missed you."

The silence that followed was the loudest Pia had ever heard.

Then, very quietly, in the back of her mind, War said:

Yeah.

And then: Yeah. That's it. That's exactly it.

And then, after a long while, almost too faint to hear: I would have, by the way. Missed you. If the hound had finished.


Chapter Seven: Corrupt

They went to see Ena anyway.

Not to fight. Pia made that clear, out loud, standing at the bottom of the tower with her hands shaking in her coat pockets and War pacing inside her head like a caged animal. Not to fight. To ask.

Ena was there, the way Esme had said she would be. She stood at the foot of the monument in the evening light, silver hair blowing, looking up at the name and the line beneath it. When Pia came up beside her, she did not turn around.

"There's a Horseman in you," she said.

"Yes," said Pia.

"War." Ena's eyes flickered red. "I can feel her. She's very angry."

"She wanted to fight you," said Pia. "She wanted to know if she'd have mattered. In the war. If she'd been there."

Now Ena turned. She looked down at Pia, at the thin pale face and the bad haircut and the coat with a mended tear in the side where a hound's teeth had gone in, and she looked for a long time.

"Does she still want to fight me?"

Pia felt War go very still.

"I don't know," said Pia. "I think she does. A little. I think it's the only way she knows how to ask the question."

Ena was quiet. Then she did something Pia did not expect. She sat down on the steps of the monument, right in the middle of the scattered flowers, and patted the stone beside her.

"Sit," she said. "Both of you."

Pia sat.

"I was the strongest thing in the war," said Ena. "That's what they say. I killed anti-gods. I broke the nexus. I corrupted a whole world. I flew into Galactix with wings of fire. And do you know what I'd give, every single day, to have been weaker?" She looked at her hands. "To have been the girl who stayed home. Who didn't matter. Who nobody needed at the end of everything. Mods would still be alive. Half the world would still be alive." She laughed, a little, without any humor in it. "War wants to know if she'd have mattered. I want to know if I could have not mattered. We'd make a terrible fight. Neither of us would be trying to win."

Inside Pia's head, War said nothing at all. But Pia could feel her. Listening.

"You want a fight?" said Ena, to the space behind Pia's eyes. "I'll tell you about the only fight that ever mattered in that whole war. It wasn't against Galactix. It was against me. Mods fighting me. Every time I was corrupted, every time I came at him with a sword, he fought me. And he never once tried to win. Not once. He only ever fought to keep me from hurting myself." Her eyes were wet. "That's what War is for, at the end of everything. Not winning. Holding the line. Holding it so someone else can get through."

There was a long silence on the steps of the tower.

Then Pia's mouth opened, and War's rough low voice came out of it, very quietly.

"Who are you holding the line for now?" said War. "Now that he's gone?"

Ena looked at the girl beside her. At the eyes that were half Pia's, half something old and red and tired.

"Everyone," she said. "No one. I don't know. I've been standing at this tower for two years trying to figure it out." She smiled, crooked. "Who are you holding it for?"

Pia felt War turn the question over inside her. Felt the old, stone-heavy mind of the second Horseman considering it, from every side, the way she considered a battlefield.

And then War said, out loud, in Pia's voice, almost surprised:

"Her."


Chapter Eight: The Last Fight

The hounds came that night.

They came up out of the quarry in the dark, a dozen of them, maybe more, grey and many-eyed and glitching with residual corruption, the way the one had come out of the hole in the fence. They came through the narrow grey town, down the main street, past the square where the young people argued about the war in the afternoons. They were hungry. They had smelled something in the quarry, a month ago, something that had bled there. And they had been waiting for it to come back.

Pia woke to the screaming.

Up, said War. Up, up, now, girl, up—

She was at the window. Down in the street, by the light of a single lamp, a hound had a man pinned against a wall. It was her uncle. The one from the wedding. The one who had said she'd never amount to anything, in front of everyone, while she smiled and said nothing.

Further down the street, the girl who had laughed at her hair was standing frozen on her doorstep in her nightgown, staring at three hounds that were slowly turning toward her.

You know what to do, said War. We've been training for this. Every weapon. All of them. Everything you've got.

Pia stood at the window with her hands on the cold glass.

"If I use them all," she said, "they're gone. Even from you. Aren't they? If we pull out everything at once, in a fight, it all gets used up. For real this time."

War was quiet.

Yes, she said at last.

"One thousand, four hundred and ninety-one," said Pia. "Everything I never said. Gone."

Yes.

Down in the street, the hound opened its mouth wide, wide, much wider than a dog's mouth should go, over her uncle's face.

"Okay," said Pia. "Okay. Then do it. But do it with me."

She climbed out of the window.

What happened next, the people of the narrow grey town talked about for the rest of their lives. How the quiet Lund girl, the one with the bad haircut who never said anything, came down the street in her nightclothes at two in the morning with a sword in each hand. How her eyes glowed red and brown at the same time. How she spoke out loud, in two voices, one girl's and one ancient, and every time she spoke, a weapon came out of the air.

She didn't fight like a soldier. Everybody agreed on that afterward. She fought like someone standing in front of a door.

She put herself between the hound and her uncle, and pulled an axe out of the air, and the axe was every unkind word he had ever said to her, and it rang off the hound's jaws like a bell. She put herself between the three hounds and the girl on the doorstep, and pulled out a spear, and the spear was three weeks of a perfect answer about a haircut, and she held the line with it while the girl ran inside and slammed the door. She pulled out a shield made of every time her mother had said why can't you be more like your cousin, and she held it up over a family of four who had come out of their house to see what the noise was, and the hounds broke their teeth on it.

One thousand, four hundred and ninety-one.

She used every single one.

When the last hound fled back down into the quarry, howling, its many eyes flickering out one by one, Pia Lund stood alone in the middle of the main street of her narrow grey town in her bare feet, holding nothing, while the sun came up behind her.

She was empty. There was nothing left in her chest. No list. No arsenal. No fights waiting. For the first time in four years, there was nothing she had not said.

How do you feel? said War, very softly, in the back of her mind.

Pia thought about it.

"Light," she said. "I feel so light."


Chapter Nine: Something New to Say

Her uncle came to see her the next day.

He stood on her mother's doorstep with his hat in his hands, a big man who had always seemed so much bigger than he was, and he looked at the girl he had said would never amount to anything, and he did not seem to know what to say.

Pia did. It was easy, now. There was nothing in the way.

"You were wrong about me," she said. "At the wedding. You said I'd never amount to anything, and I didn't say anything back, because I was scared of you. I'm not scared of you anymore. I just wanted you to know you were wrong."

Her uncle's face went red, and then white, and then something else.

"I know," he said. "I know I was. I'm sorry."

It was not a fight. It did not turn into a weapon. It was just a sentence, said out loud, on a doorstep, in the morning. And when it was over, it was over.

The girl who had laughed at her hair came by in the afternoon with a plate of cookies, and stood awkwardly in the kitchen, and finally said, "You saved my life," and Pia said, "Yeah. Also, your laughing at my hair really hurt," and the girl went very pink and said, "I know. I knew it did. I'm sorry," and then, after a pause, "it does look better now that it's grown out," and Pia laughed. Actually laughed. Out loud.

It was the strangest feeling.

That night, lying in bed, she said, "War?"

I'm here.

"You're still here. Even though the arsenal's gone. There's nothing left to forge."

I noticed that, said War dryly. Very observant of you.

"Why didn't you leave?"

There was a long silence. The kind Pia was getting used to. The kind that meant the second Horseman of the Apocalypse was thinking something she did not know how to say.

Ena asked me who I was holding the line for, said War at last. And I said you. I meant it. I didn't know I meant it until I said it. That's what happens, I suppose, when you say what you mean. You find out what it is.

Pia smiled into the dark.

Besides, said War, rougher now, a little embarrassed, you're going to start a whole new list. You know you are. Everybody does. You'll swallow something tomorrow, or the next day, something small, because you're tired, or because it isn't worth it. And I'll be here when you do. And I'll ask you if you want to forge it, or say it. And you'll choose.

"We'll choose," said Pia.

We'll choose, agreed War.

They lay there together in the dark, the quiet girl and the second Horseman, two minds in one narrow bed in one narrow grey town at the edge of the restored world. Outside, the street was quiet. The quarry was quiet. Somewhere a long way off, at the foot of a white tower, a silver-haired woman was standing alone in the night, and for the first time in two years, she was not alone in thinking about who she was holding the line for.

"War?" said Pia, just before she fell asleep.

What?

"Thank you. For saving me. In the quarry. I never said."

A pause. Then, gruff and quiet, almost too low to hear:

Number one, said War. Of the new list. Keep that one. Don't you dare let me forge it.

"I won't," said Pia, and fell asleep smiling, with nothing unsaid in her chest at all.
