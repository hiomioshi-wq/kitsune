Chapter One: Everybody Loves Dolly

Dolly Adore had forty thousand people devoted to her, and she was so bored she could scream.

She did not scream. Screaming was off-brand. Instead she sat on the pink velvet throne in the middle of her pink glass palace at the top of the tallest building in the brightest city in the restored world, and swung her legs, and ate a pink macaron, and watched the line.

The line went out the door of the throne room, down the pink glass stairs, out through the lobby, around the block, and halfway across the city. Every person in it was holding a gift. Flowers, chocolates, jewelry, poems, a hand-knitted scarf, a live goat in a bow. Every person in it had the same expression: starry-eyed, dazed, glowing pink around the eyes. Every person in it was devoted to Dolly Adore.

They had all, at some point, looked into her eyes.

That was all it took. That was her whole power. Dolly was a lesser anti-god of devotion, the youngest daughter of a very old and very minor line, and she had been born with eyes the color of strawberry ice cream and the ability to make anyone who looked into them adore her. Instantly. Completely. For as long as she liked.

She called it a Glamour.

"Next," said Dolly.

A young man knelt at the foot of the throne and held up a ring with a diamond the size of a thumbnail.

"I sold my house," he said dreamily. "For you."

"Aw," said Dolly. "That's so sweet. Put it in the pile."

The young man put it in the pile, which was taller than he was, and wandered off looking blissful, to sit in the waiting room with the other nine hundred people who had already given her things today and could not quite bring themselves to leave.

"Next."

Dolly had been doing this for two years. Ever since the war ended. Before the war, lesser anti-gods like her had been nothing. Hidden. Ashamed. The cowards, people called them, the ones who ran and hid while the real anti-gods fought and died in the first great war. Dolly's grandmother had hidden in a cave for four hundred years. Dolly's mother had hidden in a laundry. Dolly had been born in that laundry, among the steam and the sheets, and spent the first fifteen years of her life being told to keep her eyes down.

Then the world ended, and got fixed, and in the confusion afterward nobody was checking very carefully who was hiding what. And Dolly had walked out of the laundry, and lifted her eyes, and looked at the first person she saw.

That was forty thousand people ago.

"Next."

A woman stepped up and curtsied and held out a pink cake shaped like Dolly's face.

"I baked it all night," she whispered.

Dolly looked at the cake. It was a very good cake. The likeness was excellent. Somebody had spent hours on the frosting of her eyelashes.

And Dolly felt, as she had felt a hundred times a day for two years, the small cold empty feeling right in the middle of her chest, the one that nothing pink could fill.

She doesn't know me, said the feeling. She loves the Glamour. If I closed my eyes right now, she'd wake up and wonder why she spent all night baking a stranger's face.

"Thank you," said Dolly, with her best smile. "It's perfect. Put it in the pile."

And then she said, to nobody in particular, so quietly that only she could hear:

"I just want one person. One. Who looks at me and doesn't fall."


Chapter Two: The Girl From the Hound Night

Her assistant brought her the news.

Her assistant's name was Vince. He had been a bank manager before he looked into Dolly's eyes. Now he carried her schedule in a pink binder and wore a pink tie and was, by a very wide margin, the best assistant in the restored world, because he would have walked into the Deadly Desert if she asked him to and thanked her for the opportunity.

"There's a new girl trending," said Vince. "Over in the grey towns. Out by the quarries."

Dolly looked up from her macaron. Nobody trended in the grey towns.

"Who?"

Vince opened the pink binder.

"Pia Lund. Seventeen. Saved her whole town from a pack of corrupted hounds a few weeks ago. With swords, apparently. Swords that came out of the air. People say she talks in two voices. People say there's a Horseman in her." He turned a page. "People say she's the most interesting person in the restored world. Her name's being said more than yours, this week."

Dolly put down the macaron.

"More than mine."

"Only slightly," said Vince quickly.

"More," said Dolly, "than mine."

She stood up from the pink velvet throne. Forty thousand people devoted to her, and some girl in a grey town with a bad haircut was getting said more.

"Get the carriage," said Dolly. "The pink one. We're going to the grey towns."

"What for?" said Vince, and then, blushing, "I mean, of course, anything, but—what for?"

Dolly smiled. It was her best smile. Her brand smile. The one on the posters.

"I'm going to look her in the eye," she said.


Chapter Three: Glamour

The grey town was very grey.

Dolly had not been anywhere this grey since the laundry. Grey houses, grey street, grey sky, a grey quarry at the edge of it all with a fence that had recently been mended with a great deal of extra wire. Her pink carriage rolled down the main street like a cupcake in a coal mine, and everybody stopped to stare.

Dolly made sure to look at every single one of them as she passed.

By the time she reached the house at the end of the street, there were thirty people walking behind her carriage, glowing pink around the eyes, carrying whatever they'd had in their hands when she looked at them. A loaf of bread. A bucket. A very confused cat.

She stepped down from the carriage in her pink heels and knocked on the door.

A thin, pale girl opened it. Seventeen. A bad haircut, mostly grown out. A coat with a mended tear in the side. She looked at Dolly with wide, wary brown eyes.

And Dolly looked back, right into them, and Glamoured.

She felt it go out of her the way it always did, a warm pink rush, like stepping into a hot bath. Felt it slide into the girl's eyes, down into her mind, wrapping around her heart, squeezing, softly.

Pia Lund's face went slack. Her eyes went pink at the edges. Her mouth fell open a little.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, you're so beautiful."

"I know," said Dolly sweetly. "Can I come in?"

"Of course," said Pia. "Anything. Anything you want."

Dolly stepped inside, past the girl, already bored. Another one. Forty thousand and one. She'd stay a week, she thought, get the girl to tell the whole restored world how wonderful Dolly Adore was, get her name trending again, and then go home to her pink throne and her pink macarons and the small cold empty feeling in her chest that nothing ever filled.

Then a voice came out of Pia Lund's mouth.

It was not Pia's voice. It was low, and rough, and very old, and it sounded like a battlefield when the fighting has stopped.

"Get," said the voice, "out of her head."

Dolly turned around.

Pia was standing in the doorway, swaying. One of her eyes was pink and soft and dazed. The other eye was red. Dark red. The red of iron, and old blood, and fire on a hillside at night.

And the red eye was looking at Dolly, unblinking, with no love in it at all.

Not one drop.


Chapter Four: The One Who Didn't Fall

"You," said Dolly slowly, "are not devoted to me."

"I am War," said the voice from Pia's mouth. "I am the second Horseman of the Apocalypse. I have been in ten thousand generals and kings and soldiers, and I have watched every one of them swear loyalty to a hundred bright banners, and I have never once sworn loyalty to anything." The red eye narrowed. "Least of all something that comes in pink. Take your hooks out of her. Now."

Dolly stared.

Forty thousand people. Every single one of them had fallen. Kings and bankers and bakers and grandmothers and a goat, once, accidentally, at a market. She had looked into eyes on every street of the brightest city in the restored world, and not one person had ever, ever looked back at her the way this red eye was looking now.

Clear. Unimpressed. Seeing her.

"Say please," said Dolly, because it was the only thing she could think of.

"No."

"Then I won't."

The red eye blinked, once, slowly.

And then Pia's right hand reached into empty air, closed around nothing, and pulled out a short grey sword with a hilt wrapped in something like paper.

Dolly took a step back.

"I'm only going to say it one more time," said War. "Pia is mine to protect. Not yours to collect. Take your hooks out of her heart, or I'll find out what a lesser anti-god of devotion looks like on the inside."

Dolly looked at the sword. She looked at the red eye. She looked at the pink one, beside it, dazed and starry-eyed and full of a devotion that was not real, and for some reason, for the first time in two years, it made her feel slightly sick.

She let go.

It was like letting go of a rope. The warm pink rush went back out of Pia, back across the room, back into Dolly's own eyes. Pia gasped. Both her eyes went brown again. She sat down hard on the floor of her own hallway and put her hands over her face.

"What was that?" she whispered. "What did you do to me? I felt—I felt like I'd die if you left."

"It's called a Glamour," said Dolly. Her voice came out smaller than she meant. "Everybody gets one. When they look at me."

"Everybody?" said Pia through her fingers.

"Everybody," said Dolly. And then, before she could stop herself, she looked at the red-eyed space behind Pia's face, where the Horseman lived, and said, "Except her."

There was a short silence in the hallway.

Then War, through Pia's mouth, said dryly:

"Lucky me."


Chapter Five: An Arrangement

Dolly did not go home.

She told herself it was strategic. She told Vince it was strategic, sitting in the pink carriage that evening, parked at the end of the grey street, eating a grey bun from the grey bakery because the pink macarons had run out. The Horseman was a threat, she said. A Horseman who could resist a Glamour could tell other people how. That was bad for the brand. She needed to understand it. Neutralize it.

Vince nodded and agreed, glowing pink around the eyes, because he would have agreed if she had told him the sky was made of cheese.

But that wasn't why.

The truth was much simpler, and much more embarrassing. The truth was that for the first time in her whole life, somebody had looked at Dolly Adore and seen Dolly Adore. Not the pink. Not the brand. Not the strawberry-ice-cream eyes. Just a fifteen-year-old girl from a laundry, standing in a stranger's hallway, refusing to say please.

And Dolly wanted to be looked at like that again more than she had ever wanted anything.

So the next morning she knocked on Pia's door again.

Pia opened it. She did not look in Dolly's eyes. She looked at a spot on Dolly's left shoulder, very carefully, like someone looking at the sun's reflection instead of the sun.

"What do you want?" said Pia.

"I want to talk to her," said Dolly. "The Horseman. War."

"She doesn't want to talk to you."

"She can tell me that herself."

A pause. Pia's left eye flickered red.

"I don't want to talk to you," said War, through Pia's mouth.

"Great," said Dolly. "We're talking. Can I come in? I won't look at anyone. I promise. I'll look at the floor." She held up a paper bag. "I brought grey buns. From the bakery. They're actually really good. I didn't know grey food could be good."

Pia and War considered her. Dolly could feel it, the two of them deciding, the girl and the Horseman. It was the strangest thing. She had never had anyone think about her before. Not really. Not with their own minds.

"Fine," said Pia at last. "But you look at the floor."

Dolly looked at the floor. She sat at Pia's kitchen table and looked at the floor the whole time, which was very difficult, because it was a very boring floor. Grey tiles. Twenty-two of them visible from where she sat. She counted them nine times.

"Why doesn't it work on you?" she asked the floor, eventually.

"Because I don't want anything," said War.

"Everybody wants something."

"I want one thing," said War. "I want to hold the line in front of Pia. That's all. That's the whole list. A Glamour works by making people want you more than they want anything else. But there's nothing else in me to push aside. You'd have to make me want you more than I want to protect her." The red eye watched her from across the table. "And you can't. Nobody can."

Dolly counted the floor tiles a tenth time.

"That must be nice," she said quietly. "Wanting one thing that much. Knowing what it is."

Pia, who had been very quiet, said, "Don't you?"

"I want everyone to worship me," said Dolly.

"No," said War. "That's what you have. I asked what you want."

Dolly looked up from the floor before she could stop herself. Her eyes went straight to Pia's.

And Pia, who was not looking at her shoulder anymore, who had forgotten for one moment to be careful, looked back.

The Glamour went out of Dolly like a sneeze. She couldn't stop it. It went into Pia's brown eyes, warm and pink, and Pia's face went slack and starry-eyed—

—and Dolly yanked it back.

She had never done that before. Never once in two years. She grabbed the Glamour before it could close around Pia's heart and pulled it back into herself, hard, so hard that it hurt, like biting her own tongue. She gasped. Pia gasped. Both of them sat at the kitchen table, panting, staring at each other.

"You stopped it," said Pia. "Why did you stop it?"

Dolly did not know.

"Because," she said finally, "I wanted to know what you'd say. If you were you."


Chapter Six: Forty Thousand

War made her an offer.

"Stay," said the Horseman, two days later, through Pia's mouth, at the kitchen table. "For a month. Here, in the grey town. Don't Glamour anyone. Not one person. Keep your eyes down, the way you did when you were small. And at the end of the month, if you still want everyone to love you, go home. I won't stop you."

"Why would I do that?" said Dolly.

"Because you're lonely," said War. "And I can smell it on you from across the room. You smell like a general the night before a battle he knows he's going to lose. You have forty thousand people who would die for you, and not one of them knows your name."

"They all know my name. It's on posters."

"Not that name," said War. "The one from the laundry."

Dolly went very still.

Nobody knew that. Nobody. She had never told anyone. Not Vince, not one of the forty thousand. She had walked out of the laundry with her eyes up and her name changed and she had never looked back.

"How do you know about the laundry?" she whispered.

"Esme told me," said War. "The anti-god of the mind. She came by yesterday. She's been listening to you since you got here. She said you're very loud. She said—" The red eye flickered. "She said you have forty thousand people devoted to you, and every night you lie awake and say the same name over and over in your head, the name your mother used to call you in the steam, the one nobody has said out loud in two years. She said it's the loneliest sound she's ever heard."

Dolly's eyes were stinging. She pressed the heels of her hands into them, hard, the way she used to in the laundry when the steam got in them.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. One month."


Chapter Seven: The Grey Month

It was the hardest month of Dolly's life.

She kept her eyes down. She sent Vince home in the pink carriage, and told him, very carefully, without looking at him, that he could go back to being a bank manager if he wanted, and Vince burst into tears and said he didn't want to, and she said, "Okay, but you could, if you ever did," and he went home confused.

Without the Glamour, the grey town did not worship her. The grey town thought she was weird. The baker charged her full price for buns. The children on the main street laughed at her pink heels until she bought a pair of grey boots from the shoe shop, and then laughed at her for buying grey boots. The woman next door to Pia said, quite loudly, while hanging out her washing, "Who does that pink girl think she is?" and Dolly stood at the window and did not know the answer.

Who did she think she was?

At home in the brightest city in the restored world, she had been the most loved person alive. Here, she was nobody. A strange girl with a bag of buns, staying in a spare room at the Lund house, who looked at the floor when people talked to her.

But.

But Pia talked to her. Every evening, at the kitchen table, about everything. About the hound night. About the list of things Pia had never said. About the girl who had laughed at her hair and then brought cookies. About Pia's mother, who was difficult, and Pia's uncle, who had apologized, and the strange light feeling of having nothing left unsaid.

And War talked to her. Less. Gruffly. In short, sharp sentences, mostly about battle tactics and how Dolly was holding her fork wrong. But War talked to her, and every word of it was real, and not one word of it was because of a Glamour.

On the fourteenth evening, Dolly made Pia laugh. Really laugh. With a story about the goat at the market, the one she had Glamoured by accident, which had followed her around for three weeks bleating praise poems. Pia laughed so hard she fell off her chair.

Dolly sat very still at the kitchen table and listened to that laugh, and the small cold empty feeling in the middle of her chest did something it had never done before.

It got a little bit smaller.

On the twentieth evening, the woman next door, the one who had said who does that pink girl think she is, slipped on the icy step and fell and broke her wrist, and Dolly was the only one in the street who saw. Dolly ran to her and knelt in the grey slush and held her hand and kept her talking and did not look in her eyes once, not once, while Pia ran for the healer.

Afterward, with her wrist in a splint, the woman looked at Dolly for a long time.

"You didn't do your eye thing," she said. "I've heard about it. You could have done your eye thing. Made me feel better. Made me love you."

"Yeah," said Dolly. "I could have."

"Why didn't you?"

Dolly thought about it.

"Because then I wouldn't know," she said, "if you'd have let me help anyway."

The woman looked at her a little longer.

"I would have," she said gruffly. "For the record." And she went inside, and the next morning there was a jar of grey jam on the Lunds' doorstep with a note on it in shaky handwriting that said, FOR THE PINK GIRL. THANKS.

Dolly put it on the windowsill of her room and did not eat it, and looked at it every morning for the rest of the month.


Chapter Eight: Forty Thousand and One

Vince came back on the twenty-eighth day.

He came in the pink carriage, but he was not alone. Behind the carriage, filling the whole grey main street and spilling out down the side roads, were people. Thousands of them. Glowing pink around the eyes. Carrying gifts. Carrying signs: WE MISS YOU DOLLY. COME HOME DOLLY. DOLLY DOLLY DOLLY.

"I'm sorry," said Vince, wringing his pink tie, standing on the Lunds' doorstep. "I'm so sorry. I told them you were here. I couldn't help it. They kept asking. They love you so much. They can't stop. And without you there, they don't know what to do. They've stopped going to work. They've stopped eating. They just stand outside the palace and wait." His eyes were pink and wet. "Some of them are getting sick, Dolly. They won't leave. They're waiting for you to come back and look at them again."

Dolly stood in the doorway in her grey boots and looked out at the street.

Forty thousand people. Every single one of them, glowing pink. Every single one of them, devoted to a girl who didn't exist.

"I can't hold them anymore," she whispered. "I let go of Pia. I let go of Vince, sort of. But the rest of them—I've had them so long. It's like they're stuck. Like the Glamour got so deep it's just part of them now." She turned to Pia, desperate. "I don't know how to let go of forty thousand people at once. It's too many. It's too big. I'll break."

Pia's left eye flickered red.

"You won't break," said War. "But you'll need someone to hold the line."

And Pia stepped out of the house, and walked down the steps, and stood in the grey street between Dolly Adore and forty thousand people devoted to her, with her back to the crowd and her face to Dolly. Both her eyes were brown and red at once. One girl and one Horseman, standing in front of a door.

"Look at them," said Pia. "All of them. And let go. We'll hold the line while you do."

Dolly lifted her eyes.

She looked at the crowd. All of them. Forty thousand people looking back at her with pink, adoring, hollow eyes. And she reached into every single one of them, and found the warm pink hook in every heart, and she let go.

It hurt. It hurt more than anything had ever hurt. It felt like tearing out forty thousand pieces of herself at once. The pink rushed back out of the crowd and into her eyes like a flood, like a tide, like a wave breaking on a cliff, and she staggered under the weight of it, and would have fallen—

—if Pia hadn't been standing there. If War hadn't been standing there. A girl and a Horseman, braced in the grey street, holding her up.

When it was over, the street was very quiet.

Forty thousand people blinked. All at once. Their eyes went brown and blue and green and grey, every color but pink. They looked down at the gifts in their arms, the flowers and the chocolates and the signs that said DOLLY DOLLY DOLLY. They looked at each other. They looked, finally, at the strange girl in the grey boots, swaying at the end of the street with tears running down her face.

Somebody near the front said, slowly, "Why did I come here?"

And somebody else said, "I sold my house."

And somebody else, much further back, began to laugh, the bewildered laugh of someone waking from a very long, very strange dream.

Dolly stood in the middle of the grey street and waited for them to hate her.

Most of them just went home.

Some of them were angry. She let them be angry. She stood there and let them shout at her, and did not lift her eyes, and did not Glamour anyone, and said, over and over, "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I'll give it all back. All of it. I'm sorry." And slowly, one by one, even the angry ones went home too.

And a few of them—not many, but a few—stayed.

The young man who had sold his house. The woman who had baked the cake shaped like Dolly's face. Vince, in his pink tie. They stood in the grey street and looked at Dolly with their own eyes, their real eyes, uncertain and confused.

"I don't love you anymore," said the woman with the cake, slowly. "I can tell. It's gone. But I remember making that cake. All night. And I remember—I was happy. Making it." She frowned. "I think I'd like to make another one. Not because of the eyes. Just because." She looked at Dolly. "What's your real name? The one from the posters isn't real, is it?"

Dolly looked at Pia. Pia nodded.

Dolly looked at the woman.

And for the first time in two years, she said it out loud. The name from the laundry. The name her mother used to call her in the steam. A small, plain, ordinary name, the kind nobody would ever put on a poster.

"Oh," said the woman with the cake. "That's pretty. That suits you."


Chapter Nine: One

Dolly did not go back to the pink glass palace.

She gave it away. She gave away the throne and the pile of gifts and the pink velvet and the posters, every last one, to the people who had given them to her, or sold them and gave the money back when she couldn't find who they belonged to. It took months. Vince helped. He did not have to, he kept reminding her, he wasn't devoted to her anymore, he could go back to the bank. And then he came back the next day and helped anyway.

She stayed in the grey town. She got a job at the grey bakery. She was bad at it at first and then she was good at it. The children on the main street stopped laughing at her boots. The woman next door with the healed wrist brought her grey jam every week.

She kept her eyes down, mostly. Not because she had to. Because she wanted to know, every time someone was kind to her, that the kindness was theirs.

But sometimes, on purpose, she looked up.

She looked up at Pia, at the kitchen table in the evenings, and Pia looked back, and Dolly did not Glamour her. It was easier now. She had learned how to hold it. Like holding your breath. Like holding a line.

"You're doing it again," said Pia one evening. "The not-doing-it thing. I can see you doing it. Your eyes go all pink at the edges and then you scrunch them up."

"I'm practicing," said Dolly.

"Practicing what?"

"Being looked at," said Dolly, "and not making anybody fall."

Pia smiled. Behind her, in the red edge of her left eye, Dolly could see War watching, the way War was always watching, with no love in it at all. Or, no. That wasn't quite right anymore. Not love. War didn't do love. But something. Something steady and clear and unimpressed, that saw her exactly as she was, a girl from a laundry in grey boots with flour on her hands.

"For the record," said War, gruffly, through Pia's mouth, without being asked, "you hold your fork better now."

Dolly laughed.

And the small cold empty feeling in the middle of her chest, the one that had been there since the laundry, since forever, the one that forty thousand people's worth of love had never once been able to fill, was not there.

She didn't notice it go. She only noticed, one morning, walking to the bakery in the grey dawn, that she wasn't saying her old name over and over in her head anymore. That she didn't need to. That other people were saying it now, out loud, in the street, in the bakery, at the kitchen table.

Just a few people. Not forty thousand. Not even forty.

But every one of them meant it.

And that, Dolly thought, turning her face up to the grey sky with her eyes wide open and nobody to Glamour, was more than enough.
