Chapter One: Before This Era Had a Title

Long before this era had a title, before there was a restored world or a broken one, before Tombstone or Galactix or a girl with shining blonde hair, there were the anti-gods, and there were a great many of them, and they were at war.

Nobody alive remembers how it started. Kaina did. She was there at the beginning, and she was there at the end, and in between she killed more of her own kind than anyone in the history of the multiverse. That was her job. She was the anti-god of endings.

Every power in the multiverse had an anti-god. Not a god. Gods were older and simpler and mostly asleep. Anti-gods were what came after: the living opposites of the forces that made things. Where there was creation, there was an anti-god of creation, who could unmake it. Where there was fire, there was an anti-god of fire, who could turn it on its maker. Where there was mind, space, water, reality, there was someone who held its other edge.

And where there were beginnings, there was Kaina.

She did not look like much. She was small and dark and quiet, with a pale scar through one eyebrow and grey eyes that did not glow when she used her power. That was the thing about endings. They did not need to glow. They only needed to arrive. Kaina could look at anything, a star, a city, a sword, a person, and see the shape of its end. Where it would stop. How. And if she wanted to, she could reach out and move that ending a little closer.

That was what she did in the war. She moved endings closer.

"There are four thousand of them on the eastern front," said the commander, the morning the war turned. She was the anti-god of composition, tall and old and patient, and she was the closest thing the anti-gods had to a leader. "Shifters. They'll break through by evening if we don't stop them."

"I can see the end of every one of them," said Kaina.

"Then go and bring it."

Kaina went. She walked onto the eastern front alone, small and dark and quiet, through the burning grass and the falling ash. Four thousand anti-gods of shape and change saw her coming, and laughed, because she was so small.

Then she looked at them.

When she came back that evening, the commander was waiting at the edge of the camp. She did not ask how it had gone. She could see Kaina's face.

"How many?" she said quietly.

"All of them," said Kaina.

"Are you all right?"

Kaina thought about it. She thought about four thousand endings, every one of them a shape she had seen and reached out and pulled closer, like a thread pulled tight.

"I don't know," she said. "I don't feel anything. Is that all right?"

The commander put a hand on her shoulder.

"No," she said. "But it's war. Nothing's all right."


Chapter Two: Thousands Every Second

The war had a sound. Kaina would remember it for the rest of her life, which was a very long time. It was the sound of anti-gods dying: not screaming, not quite, but a kind of thin, high ringing, like a glass rim when someone runs a wet finger around it, and then a silence where the ringing had been.

By the second century of the war, that sound never stopped. Thousands every second, the commander said. Thousands every second, across ten thousand dimensions, and every one of them a power that would never come back. When an anti-god of a particular rain died, that rain stopped falling. When an anti-god of a particular color died, that color went grey in every world that had held it. The multiverse was growing quieter and greyer and smaller, and still the war went on.

Nobody could remember why anymore. There were reasons. There were always reasons. A border between two dimensions. An insult at a council two hundred years ago. A master, one of the great old powers who lived above even the anti-gods, who had whispered something to someone, and then something else to someone else. Kaina had stopped listening to the reasons. Reasons were just the shape a thing took before its ending.

She fought beside the others. The anti-gods of fire and water, sisters, who stood back to back and made storms of flame and steam that boiled whole armies. A small fierce anti-god called Psion, who could reach into minds and steer them, and who rode into battle on the back of Formality, the shapeshifter, in the form of a dragon. Cosmi, the anti-god of space, who could fold the distance between two points until it was nothing, and who laughed when she did it, a bright and startled laugh, as if she still could not believe it worked.

Kaina liked Cosmi. Nobody liked the anti-god of endings, as a rule. They were polite to her. They thanked her. They stood a little further away from her at the campfires. But Cosmi sat right next to her, and passed her bread, and asked her questions.

"What does my ending look like?" Cosmi asked once, on a quiet night, with the stars spinning overhead in ways stars should not spin.

"I don't look at my friends," said Kaina.

"Why not?"

"Because then I'd know."

Cosmi was quiet for a moment. Then she laughed her startled laugh, and nudged Kaina with her shoulder, and said, "Then don't. I'd rather be surprised."

Kaina never looked.

She would think about that, afterward, for ten thousand years.


Chapter Three: The Battle at the Seam

The worst battle of the first war was fought at a place called the Seam.

It was not a place in any single dimension. It was the edge between many. A long, raw, shimmering line where a dozen realities had been stitched together, long ago, by the masters, so that they could pass between them. Whoever held the Seam could move armies from any of those dimensions into any other. Whoever lost it would be surrounded.

Everyone went. Every anti-god who was left.

Kaina remembered it in pieces, afterward. She could never put them all together.

Fire running across the ground like water, while water wailed and boiled away into the sky. The two sisters standing back to back at the center, one burning, one drowning, holding a circle of storm around the wounded.

Psion riding Formality, the dragon's wings blotting out half the sky. Psion's eyes glowing as she reached into the mind of something huge on the other side. And then something hit her. A shard of broken reality, flying out of the Seam, and Psion's head snapped back, and when she turned again, one of her eyes was gone.

Psion, half-blind and screaming, reaching out with her mind in pure panic. Grabbing the nearest power she could find. Grabbing Cosmi.

Cosmi's face going blank. Her eyes glowing with Psion's light. Her hands rising. And space folding, the way it always folded when she laughed, except that this time she was not laughing. This time space folded around Psion, and tightened, and tightened.

Kaina saw it happen. She saw the shape of Psion's ending come rushing in, faster than any ending she had ever seen, and she understood what was happening. Psion had lost control. Psion had grabbed Cosmi without meaning to, and now Cosmi, under her control, was going to kill her. And then, once Psion was dead, the control would snap, and Cosmi would come back to herself, and find what she had done.

Cut Psion's brain out, the old stories would say, much later. That was how they would tell it. Short. Simple. As if there had been a choice in it.

There was one choice. Only one. And it was Kaina's.

She could see Cosmi's ending, too. It was right there. She had never looked at it on purpose. But in the chaos of the Seam it was suddenly in front of her, the way a cliff edge is suddenly in front of you in fog: Cosmi, folding space around everyone she loved, one after another, under the hand of a half-mad mind she could not shake off, until there was nobody left.

Unless something stopped her.

Kaina raised her hand. She did not glow. Endings do not glow. She only reached out, and found the shape of an end that was not Cosmi's death, only her stopping. A wound. A sharp, small, precise wound, in the neck, that would break Psion's hold and drop Cosmi to her knees and stop the folding. That was all. That was all Kaina meant to do.

Kaina shot Cosmi's neck.

It worked. Cosmi's hands dropped. The space around Psion unfolded with a sound like a sheet of glass shattering, and Psion fell, gasping, alive. Cosmi's eyes went dark, then her own again, then wide.

And out of the wound in her neck, where there should have been blood, came something black.


Chapter Four: What Came Out of the Wound

It was liquid. That was what Kaina thought, at first. A thick black liquid, slow, shining, pouring out of the small precise wound in Cosmi's neck and running down her collarbone.

Then it screamed.

Not Cosmi. Cosmi was screaming too, her hands at her throat, but this was something else. The black liquid itself was screaming, a high glitching noise like a broken signal, and as it screamed it began to move. Not down. Out. It sprayed from the wound in a long dark arc and hit the Seam, and the Seam, the raw shimmering edge where a dozen realities met, drank it in.

And tore.

Kaina would remember the sound of that for the rest of her life too. A tear in the multiverse. Like the biggest sheet of cloth in the world being ripped in half, slowly, by enormous hands.

"What is that?" somebody shouted. "What is it? What did she do?"

Kaina did not know. She had seen the shape of a wound. She had not seen what was inside it.

Later, much later, when there were scholars again, they would argue about what it had been. Some said space itself had been sick for a long time, poisoned by the war, by thousands of anti-gods dying every second, and that Kaina had simply lanced the boil. Some said a master had planted it in Cosmi long ago, a seed, waiting for someone to break the skin. Some said it had always been there, in everything, a little black thread at the heart of creation, and that the first war had simply made it strong enough to come out.

But they would all agree on its name.

Corruption.

It came out of the wound in Cosmi's neck. It came out of the tear in the Seam. It came out across the battlefield in black glittering waves, and where it touched an anti-god, that anti-god's eyes went red, and their face went slack, and then they smiled. A wide, wrong, delighted smile. And then they turned on whoever was standing next to them.

The war had been terrible. This was something else. This was the end of the war and the end of the world, both at once.

Kaina saw it happen and could see the ending of almost everyone she had ever known, all at once, rushing in like a tide.

She had never been able to stop an ending. Only move one closer.

So she did the only thing she could think of. She moved the end of the battle closer.

She looked at the Seam, the torn and screaming Seam, and she saw its ending, the place where it would finally rip all the way through and collapse, and she reached out and pulled that ending toward her. Fast. As fast as she could. She pulled until her hands bled and her grey eyes went black and her ears filled with the ringing of glass.

And the Seam closed.

Not healed. Closed. It fell in on itself, all dozen realities collapsing inward like a house with its walls kicked out. The black waves of corruption that had not yet reached the battlefield were sucked back into it. And the Seam folded down, and down, and down, into a single grey point, and the point fell out of the multiverse altogether.

Into a world broken from the rest of reality.

Taking with it, screaming, everything inside.


Chapter Five: Twenty-Seven

When it was over, there were twenty-seven of them left.

Twenty-seven anti-gods, out of thousands and thousands, standing on a grey plain under a grey sky, in the wreckage of ten thousand years of war. Kaina counted them twice, because she could not believe it.

The commander was not among them. The anti-god of composition had gone into the collapsing Seam with her arms open, Kaina had seen it, holding it shut from the inside. Her ending had come exactly the way Kaina had pulled it.

Psion was there, one eye gone, shaking, being held up by Formality in human shape. The fire and water sisters were there, burned and soaked and holding hands so tightly their knuckles were white. Cosmi was there.

Cosmi was sitting on a broken rock with her hand pressed to her neck, where the wound was, and something black was still seeping between her fingers. Very slowly. A little at a time.

Kaina went to her. She did not know what else to do.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I'm so sorry. I didn't know. I only meant to stop you. I didn't know what was in there."

Cosmi looked up at her. Her eyes were still her own. For now.

"You looked," said Cosmi quietly. "At my ending. You said you never would."

"I had to. You were going to—"

"I know what I was going to do." Cosmi took her hand away from her neck and looked at the black on her fingers. "I know. You saved them. You saved all of them." She laughed, but it was not her startled laugh. It was something thinner. "I just wish I'd been surprised."

Kaina sat down on the rock beside her. Cosmi did not move away. Even now. Even after everything.

"It's still coming out," said Cosmi, after a while. "Slowly. I can feel it. In me. It's not done."

"I know," said Kaina.

"What happens when it's done?"

Kaina did not answer. She did not look. She refused to look. She sat beside her friend on the grey rock and held her hand, black and all, and did not look at her ending, because Cosmi had asked her not to, and because she was the only person left who could keep that promise.

Around them, the twenty-seven began to argue.

"We have to go after it," said someone. "Into the broken world. Before it gets out."

"Nobody can go in there," said someone else. "It's sealed. Kaina sealed it."

"Then we stay here. We guard the edge."

"Guard it with what? There are twenty-seven of us. We can't guard anything."

And then a voice that Kaina knew, a clear sharp voice with a scar in it, said:

"I'll guard it."

Everyone turned.

It was a young anti-god of the mind. She was standing a little apart from the others, with a fresh burn across her face in the shape of a flame. She looked very tired.

"Hell," she said. "The pit at the bottom of everything. It touches every dimension, even the broken ones. If corruption gets out of the broken world, it'll come up through hell first. Someone has to be there. Watching." She shrugged. "I'll go. Nobody else wants to."

"You'll never come back up," said one of the sisters. "Nobody comes back from hell."

"I know," said the young anti-god of the mind. Her name was Eri. "That's why it should be me. I don't have anyone left to come back up for."

She went that night. Down through the cracks in the grey plain, alone, into the dark. Kaina watched her go, and saw the shape of her ending, far, far away down the long tunnel of years: a demon of the mind, horned and winged, crying in a burning ruin, killing her only friend.

Kaina looked away. There was nothing she could do. She could only move endings closer. She had never once learned how to move one further away.


Chapter Six: The Cowards

Not everyone stayed.

In the weeks after the Seam, the twenty-seven became twenty-five, then twenty-two, then nineteen. Not because they died. Because they left.

Kaina understood why. Everybody understood why. They had seen what corruption did. They had watched their friends' eyes go red, and their faces go slack, and their smiles go wide and wrong. They had seen the commander walk into the collapsing Seam with her arms open. And they were tired, and they were frightened, and they did not want to be next.

So they hid.

They went to small, quiet dimensions at the edge of the multiverse, where nobody would look for them. They gave up their names. They gave up most of their power, folding it down small so that it would not shine and draw attention. They became something less. Lesser anti-gods, the others started calling them, with contempt in their voices. Cowards. The ones who ran.

One of them, a small, soft-spoken anti-god of desire, came to say goodbye to Kaina before she went.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I'm sorry I can't stay. I'm not brave like you."

"I'm not brave," said Kaina. "I'm the anti-god of endings. There's nowhere for me to hide. Everything I look at, I see how it stops."

"Then you're braver than any of us," said the anti-god of desire, "because you keep looking." She hesitated. "Will you look at mine? Just once? I want to know if I'll be safe."

Kaina looked.

She saw a cave. Four hundred years in a cave, hiding. And then a laundry, with steam and sheets. And then a granddaughter, much, much later, with eyes the color of strawberry ice cream, walking out of the laundry with her head held high, and making forty thousand people love her, and then letting every one of them go.

"You'll be safe," said Kaina. "And someday, someone in your family will stop hiding. And it'll go wrong at first. And then it'll go right."

The anti-god of desire smiled, and cried, and went.

Kaina did not blame the cowards. Not then, and not later, when the stories turned them into villains. She had seen what was coming. Some of them had seen it too, and had looked at it, and had chosen to live. That was not cowardice. That was only a different kind of ending.


Chapter Seven: Going Incarnate

The ones who stayed began to die.

Not all at once. Not in battle. Slowly. In a way none of them had ever seen before.

It started with the sisters. The anti-gods of fire and water had fought harder at the Seam than almost anyone, holding their circle of storm around the wounded, and they had spent so much of their power that there was very little left. And one morning, the fire sister's eyes began to glow. Not her usual orange. A deeper color. A color with something else in it.

"It feels good," she said, staring at her hands, where the flames were rising higher and brighter than they had ever risen. "It feels so good. I've never been this strong."

By evening she had wings of fire. By the next morning she had armor, glowing and gold, that seemed to grow out of her skin. Her water sister stood beside her, horrified, watching her blaze.

"What's happening to you?" said the water sister.

"I don't know," said the fire sister. "I don't care. I feel like I could fight the whole multiverse. I feel like I'm finally what I was supposed to be."

They called it going incarnate. Nobody knew who said it first. Becoming the full, final, blazing form of your power, more than you had ever been, wings and armor and light.

What nobody understood, until it was too late, was what powered it.

Kaina understood first. Because she could see the ending.

"Stop," she said to the fire sister, on the third day, when the wings had grown so large they cast a shadow over half the camp. "Stop it. Pull it back."

"Why?"

"Because I can see where it ends." Kaina's voice was shaking. "It's corruption. It's the little bit of corruption that got into all of us at the Seam. It's feeding on it. That's what's making you so strong. It's eating the corruption in you and turning it into power, and when the corruption's gone, it'll start eating you. You'll burn. You'll burn and burn until there's nothing left, and then you'll still be burning."

The fire sister stared at her.

"Then I'll burn," she said. "I'd rather burn bright than hide."

She did not pull it back. Neither did most of the others. One by one, the anti-gods who had stayed went incarnate. Wings. Armor. Light. Every one of them stronger than they had ever been. Every one of them, slowly, horribly, losing themselves.

Some of them turned on each other. Some of them turned on themselves. Some of them simply blazed and blazed until they burned out, like stars, and left nothing behind but a scorch mark and the thin high ringing of glass.

And the ones who were left, the ones who were too far gone to save, the anti-gods with red eyes and wide smiles who had let the corruption all the way in: they were rounded up by the last of the others. Slain, some of them. Imprisoned, the rest. Thrown through the last crack in the sealed Seam into the broken world, where everything else had gone.

And there, in that broken world, cut off from the rest of reality, they began corrupting everything around them.

The fire and water sisters survived. Kaina never knew how. The water sister dragged the fire sister away from the edge of her own burning on the fourth night, held her in a river of her own making until the wings went out, and sat with her in the steam until morning. They lived. They were never quite the same.

Many years later, their descendants would carry the same powers. Fire and water. And a girl called Rui would stand on the western edge of another war and burn herself down to almost nothing to hold the line, and not know that her great-great-great-grandmother had done exactly the same thing, and been pulled back out of it by her sister.


Chapter Eight: The Last Anti-God of the Dead

At the end, there were only two of them left in the grey camp who had not gone incarnate, or hidden, or died. Kaina and Cosmi.

Cosmi was dying.

The black was still seeping out of the wound in her neck, a little every day. It had never stopped. It did not hurt, she said. It only made her tired. She sat on her rock at the edge of the grey plain and watched the stars spin overhead, and every day she was a little greyer, and a little quieter, and her laugh, when it came, was a little thinner.

"You could look now," she said one evening. "At my ending. I don't mind. I'd like to know how long."

"No," said Kaina.

"Why not?"

"Because you asked me not to." Kaina sat beside her and took her hand. "Because it's the only promise I've ever kept. Everything else I've ever touched, I've ended. I'm not going to end the not-knowing too."

Cosmi laughed. A real laugh. Startled. Almost the old one.

"You're very stubborn for the anti-god of endings," she said.

"I've been told."

They sat together in the dark for a long time. And then Cosmi said, very quietly:

"It's going to come back. Isn't it? The corruption. It's in the broken world, but it won't stay there. It's too hungry. Someday it'll get out. And there won't be anybody left who remembers how to fight it."

"I know," said Kaina.

"Can you see it? The ending? Of corruption?"

Kaina had been trying not to look. She had been trying very hard. But she was the anti-god of endings, and Cosmi had asked, and so she looked.

She looked out across the grey plain, past the sealed Seam, into the broken world beyond it, where the black glittering thing was still screaming. She looked for its end. She looked for the place where corruption finally stopped.

It was very, very far away. Further than she had ever looked at anything. Thousands upon thousands of years. She had to strain to see it, the way you strain to see a single light on a distant hill at night.

But it was there.

"I see it," she whispered.

"What does it look like?"

Kaina was silent for a long time.

"A girl," she said finally. "A girl with shining blonde hair. Her brother loves her more than anything. And the corruption finds her, and goes into her, all of it, the whole thing, all at once. And she becomes it. She becomes the corruption. The whole of it, in one person." Her voice was shaking. "And then she breaks. She breaks the world. And her brother has to fight her. Again and again. And it hurts them so much. And they lose everything. But at the end of all of it, after everything, the corruption is just—hers. Just a girl's grief. Residual. Something she carries. Not something that eats worlds anymore." She swallowed. "That's how it ends. With a girl and her brother. And it costs them everything. But it ends."

Cosmi was quiet.

"That's terrible," she said.

"Yes."

"Can you move it closer? So it happens sooner? So fewer people get hurt in between?"

Kaina had never been asked to move an ending closer for mercy before. Only for war.

"I don't know," she said. "I've never tried with something so far away. Something that hasn't been born yet."

"Will you try?"

Kaina looked at her friend. At the black seeping slowly between her fingers. At her grey face, still smiling.

"Yes," she said.


Chapter Nine: The Prophecy

Moving an ending that far away was not like moving a thread. It was like moving a mountain with a single hair. Kaina could not reach it with her hands. She could not reach it with her eyes. The only way to touch something that far into the future, she realized, was with words.

So she wrote.

She sat on the grey rock beside Cosmi, with a sheet of paper made from the bark of the last tree on the plain, and a pen made from a feather that had fallen from Formality's dragon wings. And she wrote down the ending she had seen. Exactly as she had seen it. A girl with shining blonde hair. A beloved sister. Grief. Corruption. The end of everything, and then, after it, a new beginning.

A prophecy. That was what it would be called. The first one ever written by the anti-god of endings, or by anyone. A shape of the future, set down in ink, so that the ending would know where to go. So that it would come.

It was the hardest thing Kaina had ever done. Harder than the eastern front. Harder than the Seam. Every word she wrote, she could feel the ending getting closer, straining toward her across the years like a dog straining at a rope. Every word, she could feel the black thing in the broken world turning its head toward her, listening.

She was nearly finished when it reached her.

It did not come through the Seam. The Seam was sealed. It came through Cosmi. Through the small black wound in Cosmi's neck, the wound Kaina had made, the wound that had never stopped seeping. A thin black thread rose up out of it, so thin Kaina did not see it, and crossed the space between them on the grey rock, and touched the tip of her pen.

For one second, Kaina was corrupted.

Only one second. She felt it go into her, cold and red and laughing, and she felt her own hand move on the paper, writing something she had not meant to write. And then Cosmi grabbed her wrist, hard, and pulled the pen away, and the black thread snapped, and Kaina came back to herself gasping, with her eyes stinging and her hand shaking and the prophecy finished on the page in front of her.

She looked down at it.

Most of it was hers. The girl. The shining hair. The grief. The ending. The new beginning, after. She had written those carefully, in her own small neat hand.

But in the middle, there was a line she had not written. A line in a different shape, wide and jagged, the letters pressed so hard into the bark that they had almost gone through:

It will only start when a man plunges a knife into his sister.

Kaina stared at it.

"No," she whispered. "No. That's not what I saw. I didn't see that. He doesn't—he wasn't supposed to—"

She reached for the line to scratch it out. And stopped. Because she could see, now, what it had done. It was already written. It was already ink. The ending she had moved closer had already bent toward it, the way a river bends toward a cut in the bank. The brother would believe it. Everyone would believe it. Because a prophecy written by the anti-god of endings was not a guess. It was a shape the future would grow into.

The corruption had put one sentence in her hand. And that one sentence would make the brother kill his sister. And the sister's grief at being killed by the person who loved her most would be what finally let the corruption all the way in.

It had written its own door.

"I can't take it out," Kaina said. Her voice was very calm. That was how she knew how bad it was. "If I cross it out, the ending won't know where to go. It'll come anyway, but it'll be worse. Wilder. More people will die. I can't take it out. It's part of the shape now."

Cosmi put her black-stained hand over Kaina's shaking one.

"Then write something else," she said. "Underneath. In your own hand. Something it can't touch."

Kaina looked at her.

"Like what?"

"I don't know," said Cosmi. "You're the anti-god of endings. What would you want someone to read, if they were going to live through that? What would you want the girl to know?"

Kaina sat for a long time on the grey rock, looking at the jagged line in the middle of her prophecy.

Then she picked up the pen again, and very carefully, at the very top of the page, above everything else, in her own small neat hand, she wrote a note. Not a prophecy. Just a note. From her, to whoever found it.

Note From The Master Writer: The Prophecist was Corrupted for a Second While Writing This Prophecy.

And then, underneath, because it was true, and because she wanted the girl to know, and because it was the only thing she could think of that might help:

Please don't hurt her.


Chapter Ten: Exile

They found out, of course. The last few anti-gods who were left. Not the cowards, not the incarnate, not the dead, but the handful who still came to the grey camp sometimes to see if there was anything left to save. They read the prophecy. They saw the jagged line.

"You let it in," they said. "You let corruption into the prophecy. You wrote the end of the world."

"I wrote the end of corruption," said Kaina. "Read the rest."

They did not read the rest. They only read the line about the knife, and the note at the top, and the words corrupted for a second. And they looked at Kaina the way the soldiers on the eastern front had looked at her, before she ended them.

They exiled her.

There was no place to exile anyone to, anymore, except the broken world or hell. So they sent her to a small dimension at the very edge of everything, where nothing lived, and nothing ended, because nothing began. A blank. A grey page. They left her there with the prophecy and a pen, and told her not to write anything else.

Cosmi came with her.

Nobody had asked her to. Nobody could have stopped her. She folded space one last time, with the very last of her strength, and stepped through after Kaina into the blank, and sat down on the grey nothing beside her, and took her hand.

"You didn't have to come," said Kaina.

"I know," said Cosmi. "But you kept your promise. You never looked at my ending. Not on purpose. So I'd like to be there when it happens. I'd like to be surprised with someone I love."

She died three days later. Quietly. With her head on Kaina's shoulder, and the black finally stopped seeping from the wound in her neck, and the last of her startled laugh still in the corner of her mouth.

Kaina did not look. Not even at the end. She kept her eyes closed, and held her friend's hand, and let the ending come on its own, the way endings are supposed to, without anyone pulling.

It was the first one in ten thousand years that she had not touched.


Chapter Eleven: Some Stories Start at the End

The prophecy left the blank dimension a long time after that. Nobody knows how. Prophecies have a way of getting out. It turned up, centuries later, in a library in a dimension called Terra, copied and recopied by hands that did not understand it, the note at the top half rubbed away, the please don't hurt her almost too faint to read.

People found it. People believed it. People looked at a little girl with shining blonde hair and whispered about her, and gave her weird looks in the street, and told her brother, again and again, that she would end the world.

And her brother said: I don't believe it. How can people resort to being this crazy?

He was right. And it happened anyway. Because the line about the knife was already ink, and the ending had already bent toward it, like a river toward a cut.

But the rest of the prophecy was in there too. Kaina's part. The part in her own small neat hand. The girl, and her grief, and the end of everything, and then, after, a new beginning. And that part happened too. All of it. It cost them everything. But it happened.

And at the very end, after the broken world and the Gray Abyss and Galactix and the war, a silver-haired woman sat at the foot of a white tower in the restored world, holding a copy of an old prophecy she had found in a ruined library. She read it over and over. The line about the knife. The note at the top. And the last faint words, at the very top of the page, almost rubbed away.

Please don't hurt her.

"Who wrote this?" she said out loud, to nobody. "Who wrote this, and why did they say please?"

The tower did not answer. But somewhere, very far away, in a blank grey dimension at the edge of everything, a small dark quiet woman with a scar through one eyebrow and grey eyes that did not glow sat on the grey nothing, alone, and felt the ending she had written ten thousand years ago finally, finally arrive.

She did not pull it. She did not push it. She only watched it come, the way she had watched Cosmi's.

And then, for the first time in all the long ages of her exile, Kaina, the anti-god of endings, the Prophecist, the last of the first war, put down her pen.

And let something begin.
