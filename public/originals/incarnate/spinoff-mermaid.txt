Incarnate: Mermaid
A spinoff of the Corruption Saga. Halcyon, Illinois, after Calypso.

Trigger warning: This story contains a child alone among hollowed-out adults, parents who can't love you back, power over grown-ups, a person locked up, and a narrator who is eleven and decides she likes being in charge.
Read at your own risk.



Chapter One: The Only One

My name is Hattie Lindqvist and I'm eleven and I live in Halcyon, Illinois, population 2,340, and I'm the only person here who's awake.

You probably saw us on the news. The town where everybody stood on Main Street smiling. The drone footage. The "mass psychogenic event." They don't show it on the news anymore. There's other stuff now. The whole world humming. Hospitals in Cleveland. Juniper Vance's mug shot. We got old fast.

The National Guard stayed three weeks. Doctors in yellow suits took blood from everybody and looked in their eyes with little lights and wrote things on clipboards. Then they decided nobody was in danger, because nobody was sick, because everybody was happy, and they left a fence around the town and a sign that said QUARANTINE and a man in a truck at the gate who changes every eight hours and hums.

Then the whole world started humming too, and nobody cared about one little town anymore.

So it's just us. Two thousand three hundred and thirty-nine smiling people. And me.



Chapter Two: What She Took

She was here for nine days. Calypso. The siren. I knew what she was the first day. I'd just read the Odyssey, the kids' version with pictures, the one with the mermaid on the cover even though sirens in the real one are birds, which the book gets wrong and which I wrote a letter to the publisher about.

Everybody else looked at her and saw the prettiest girl in the whole world.

I looked at her and saw the rocks under the water.

She sat next to me on the Methodist church steps. I told her I knew what she was. She sang into my face so hard my ears rang for a week, and my eyes went blue, and I said she was beautiful, and I meant it, I really meant it, for about four days.

And she took my book.

That's all she took from me. That's the weird part. She ate everybody else. My mom. My dad. My teacher Mrs. Okafor-Burns. Mr. Pruett at the Dairy Queen. Everybody's big want, the most important one, gone. But me she just sang at. And took my book. Like a souvenir.

I think she was saving me. Like dessert.

And then she left before she got hungry again.

So the song wore off. It took four days. It was like a cold going away. One morning I woke up and I didn't think she was beautiful anymore, I thought she was a monster, and I went downstairs, and my mom was standing in the kitchen, smiling at the toaster.



Chapter Three: Mom

My mom used to be a nurse at the clinic in Bremen. She used to want to go back to school and be a nurse practitioner. She talked about it all the time. She had a binder with the applications in it. She'd do flashcards at the kitchen table while I did homework. Drug names. Dosages.

That's what Calypso ate.

Now she stands in the kitchen. She makes breakfast if I ask. She drives me to the fence and back if I ask. She smiles. She says, "Good morning, Hattie, you're so beautiful," because that's how everybody in Halcyon says good morning now, Calypso made it a rule and nobody ever un-made it.

She doesn't do flashcards. She doesn't do anything unless somebody tells her.

I found the binder in the trash.

My dad wanted to fix up the old Ford in the garage. That was his thing. Every Saturday. Now the Ford sits there with the hood up and my dad sits on the couch.

They're not sad. That's the thing I need you to get. They're not sad and they're not scared and they don't miss anything. They're just... done. They're like houses with the lights on and nobody home.

I tried for a while. To bring them back. I read my mom her flashcards. I put the wrench in my dad's hand. I yelled. I cried. I called Grandma in Peoria and she hummed at me on the phone.

They just smiled.

"You're so beautiful, Hattie."



Chapter Four: Rules

Here's what I figured out on the fifteenth day.

They do what you tell them.

Not just me. Anyone. If you say it nice. If you say it like you mean it. It's like the song left a groove in them, a little dent where Calypso's voice used to go, and now any voice fits in it.

I found out by accident. I said, "Mom, can you make pancakes," and she made pancakes. Then I said, "Mom, can you make pancakes shaped like dinosaurs," and she did, and she's never done that in my life, she always said it was too much work. Then I said, "Mom, you think I'm the smartest kid in the world," and she said, "You're the smartest kid in the world, Hattie," and smiled, and I sat there with my dinosaur pancakes and I felt something I didn't have a name for.

It felt like the first warm day after winter.

So I tried it on Dad. "You think I'm a genius."

"You're a genius, Hattie."

I tried it on Mrs. Okafor-Burns. She used to give me Cs in math because I "didn't show my work." I walked into her house and said, "Mrs. Okafor-Burns, I don't have to show my work, right?"

"You don't have to show your work, Hattie."

"I get an A."

"You get an A."

"Say it like you mean it."

"You get an A, Hattie. You're so beautiful."

I walked out. It was snowing. I opened my mouth and caught snow on my tongue and laughed until my stomach hurt.



Chapter Five: Queen Hattie

You'd do it too. Don't lie. You'd do it too.

I was eleven and I was alone in a town full of grown-ups who had never once listened to me. Who'd told me I was too young. Too loud. Too much. Who'd said the siren was "just a pretty girl, Hattie, don't be jealous." Who'd told me to put down the book and go outside.

And now they'd do anything I said.

By day thirty I had rules. I wrote them down in a composition notebook. I'm good at rules. I got it from her, I think. She talked about rules a lot, when she was sitting on the church steps with me. Rule one, rule two, rule three. She thought it made her sound smart.

My rules:

1. Everybody in Halcyon calls me Queen Hattie.
2. The Dairy Queen is open every day and it's free for me.
3. School is cancelled, except the library, which is mine.
4. Nobody is allowed to be mean to anybody. (I made this one because of Tyler Brandt, who used to push me off the swings. Now he pushes me on the swings. Whenever I want.)
5. Mom and Dad tuck me in at night and tell me a story and say they're proud of me.
6. Nobody leaves.

Rule six is important. Rule six is for the man in the truck at the fence. And for anybody else who comes.

I made the high school art club paint the water tower. It said CALYPSOVILLE. Now it says HATTIEVILLE. It's not as good as Calypso's. Our art club wasn't very good before, either.

I found a crown in the costume closet at the high school. Paper, painted gold. From some play. I wear it.

Everybody tells me I'm so beautiful.

I know it's not the same as meaning it. I know that.

But it's so much better than nobody saying it.



Chapter Six: The Hum

Then the whole world started humming.

I felt it come in, through the radio, through the air, through the grain elevator, which hummed so loud the pigeons left. And everyone in Halcyon, who already had a groove where her voice went, lit up like Christmas lights. They hummed along, all two thousand three hundred and thirty-nine of them, all at once. It shook the windows.

And I didn't hum.

I don't know why. I think it's because she never ate me. She just sang at me. And I got over it. I'm like someone who already had the chicken pox. The song tried to get in and it couldn't find the door.

I watched it on TV. The whole world going like Halcyon. People in cities standing in the street. Cleveland. New York. London. Lagos. Tokyo. People smiling. People saying they felt so peaceful. Everywhere.

She'd done it. She'd done the whole world.

I sat on my bedroom floor with my paper crown and I watched it and I felt two things at the same time.

I felt scared.

And I felt jealous.



Chapter Seven: The Stranger

The stranger came on day forty.

A woman got off the bus at the county road. Young. Twenty, maybe. A big cardigan. Boots with paint on them. Messenger bag. Bandages on both thumbs. She walked up to the fence where the truck was, and the man in the truck smiled at her and hummed, and she walked right past him, and he didn't stop her, because he doesn't want anything.

Rule six. Nobody leaves. But nobody ever said anything about people coming in.

I watched her from the top of the water tower, which is where I go to think now. She walked down Main Street. She looked at everybody. At the grown-ups standing in their yards smiling. At the Dairy Queen. At the water tower, at my name. She stopped and looked at that for a while.

And then she started drawing on the ground.

Chalk. A circle. A big one. Right in the middle of Main Street, in front of the Methodist church, where I sat with the siren. She drew a circle inside a circle inside a circle. She set out black candles. Seven of them.

I knew who she was. I'd seen her on the news. Her mug shot. The hospital in Cleveland. Nine people died on the ninth floor. Eleven the next day.

Juniper Vance.



Chapter Eight: What Juniper Wanted

I climbed down and walked over. I was wearing my crown. I didn't think about it.

She looked up at me from inside her circle. She looked so tired. She looked like she hadn't eaten in a week.

"You're awake," she said. Surprised. "You're not humming."

"I'm Queen Hattie," I said.

She looked at the crown. She looked at the water tower. She did a little laugh, not a nice one.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, kid. Oh no. You too?"

"What are you doing in my town?"

And she told me. She told me everything. I think she'd been waiting a long time for anybody to listen to her. She told me about the receipts. About how everybody in Halcyon still had a thread to what Calypso took. About how if she pulled them all, all two thousand three hundred and thirty-nine, at once, the song would break. The song in everything. The whole world could wake up.

"And what happens to them?" I asked. "The people here?"

Juniper looked at the Methodist church for a while.

"Most of them would die," she said.

"My mom."

"Probably."

"My dad."

"Probably. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. But Hattie, you have to understand, it's one town. It's one town against the whole world. Against everything. You read, right? You read the Odyssey. You know about sacrifices. Sometimes the—"

"Odysseus tied himself to the mast," I said. "He didn't kill his whole crew."

"Half his crew died anyway," Juniper said. "It's a very sad book, Hattie."



Chapter Nine: Rule Six

I could have helped her.

I know that. I know what she was saying was probably true. I know the whole world is humming and it's not going to stop, and the siren's going to eat everybody, and someday everybody everywhere will be like my mom, standing in the kitchen smiling at the toaster. I know Juniper might be the only person who can stop it. I'm eleven. I'm not stupid.

And I know my mom isn't really my mom anymore. I know my dad is a house with no one home. I know.

But they tuck me in.

They say they're proud of me. Every night. Because I told them to.

And nobody ever said it before. Not once. Not like they meant it.

And Juniper wanted to take that away. For the world. For people I've never met in places I've never been. She wanted me to give back the only thing I've ever had that was all mine.

I looked at her in her chalk circle. At her bandaged thumbs. At her seven black candles.

And I turned around and I said to Main Street, "Everybody. Come here."

And everybody came.

Two thousand three hundred and thirty-nine people. Out of their houses. Out of the Dairy Queen. Out of the church. Walking. Smiling. They filled Main Street from the grain elevator to the fence.

Juniper stood up. Her face went white.

"Hattie," she said. "Hattie, don't."

"She's breaking rule six," I said. "She's trying to make you leave. All of you. Forever."

"We don't want to leave," said Halcyon.

"Take her candles," I said. "And take her to the grain elevator. And lock the door."



Chapter Ten: The Grain Elevator

She fought. She was a lot stronger than she looked. She got her thumb cut open again somehow and she screamed Calypso's name and some of the grown-ups flinched back, like it stung them. But there were two thousand of them. And they don't get tired. And they don't get scared. And they don't stop, if you don't tell them to.

They carried her down Main Street. Mr. Pruett from the Dairy Queen had her legs. Mrs. Okafor-Burns had one arm. My dad had the other.

Juniper was screaming at me the whole way. Not mean. Begging. "Hattie, please. Hattie, you're the only one who can see it. You're like me. Please don't. Please don't become her. Please."

That was the thing that made me mad.

I'm not like her. I'm not like the siren. I didn't eat anybody. I didn't sing at anybody. They were already like this. I'm just the only one telling them what to do. That's different. Somebody has to.

They put her in the grain elevator. It's huge. Metal. Concrete. There's a door at the bottom that locks from the outside, with a big bar.

My dad dropped the bar.

"Good job, Dad," I said.

"I'm so proud of you, Hattie," he said.

Inside, Juniper was pounding on the door. Her voice came out of the vents, echoing up the tower, high and thin.

"YOU'RE GOING TO BE ALONE. YOU KNOW THAT? WHEN EVERYONE'S LIKE THEM, YOU'RE GOING TO BE THE ONLY ONE, AND THEY'LL ALL TELL YOU WHATEVER YOU WANT, AND NONE OF IT WILL BE TRUE, AND YOU'LL BE ALONE FOREVER, HATTIE."

I put my hand on the warm metal of the door.

"I'm already alone," I said. "This is better."



Chapter Eleven: Hattieville

I bring her food every day. I'm not a monster. I make my mom make sandwiches and I slide them under the door. Peanut butter, no crusts. She doesn't eat them most days. She keeps chalking on the walls. I can hear the scratching.

I'm going to let her out, someday. When it's over. When the whole world's like Halcyon and she can't stop it anyway.

At night Mom and Dad tuck me in. They sit on the edge of the bed. Mom reads me a story. I pick the story. I pick the same one every night. I found a different copy of the Odyssey in the library, the grown-up one, not the kids' one. It doesn't have a mermaid on the cover. It's the real one, where the sirens are birds.

Mom reads the part where Odysseus is tied to the mast. Every night. Her voice is flat. She doesn't do the voices like she used to. But she reads it.

Then they both say, "We're so proud of you, Hattie. You're so beautiful."

And I close my eyes.

Sometimes when it's quiet and the grain elevator's quiet and the whole world's humming outside the window, I think about the siren. Calypso. Somewhere at the bottom of everything, singing to everybody at once. And I think, I wonder if anybody tucks her in.

Then I think: no. Nobody's ever going to tell her they're proud of her and mean it.

Nobody's ever going to tell me either.

It's okay. We have the next best thing.

I put my crown on the nightstand where I can see it.

"Good night, Queen Hattie," says my mom.

"Good night," I say.

Outside, the water tower says my name.
