Chapter One: Nothing

Galactix woke up with nothing.

That was the first thing she understood, before she understood where she was or how long she had been there. She reached for her power the way you reach for the floor when you get out of bed, without thinking, the way she had reached for it every waking moment for longer than most stars had been burning.

There was no floor.

She reached again. For the threads of reality that she had always been able to feel, humming, everywhere, like the strings of an enormous instrument. For the nexus points, the anchors of every dimension, each one a bright hard knot in the web of the multiverse. For the vessels, the anti-gods and mortals she could pour herself into and wear. For the fire she had made, and the black holes, and the beams of light that could cut a world in half.

Nothing. Nothing. Nothing.

She was lying on her back on something grey. Above her was more grey. Not sky. Not ceiling. Just grey, going up forever, the color of old paper, the color of nothing at all.

She sat up. Her body felt strange. Heavy. Small. She looked down at her hands, and they were hands, ordinary hands, a little thin, a little pale. Not the vast shining shapes of light she had worn in battle. Just hands.

She remembered.

The hermitage at the edge of time. A boy in silver armor, glowing blue, holding a beam she had fired. A girl with red wings. And then the anti-gods. All of them. Pouring out of the doorway of a stone hut, out of a mage's book, back from the dead. Fire and water and space and mind and shape, all striking her at once. And the boy, Modify, at the center of it all, redirecting her own power back at her, sealing her away.

In a pocket dimension, she had heard someone say, as the light closed over her. Where her powers are void.

Galactix sat on the grey nothing and looked at her ordinary hands.

"Well," she said. Her voice was small too. It did not echo. There was nothing for it to echo off. "That's inconvenient."


Chapter Two: What She Was For

Most people who fought Galactix never understood what she was trying to do.

They thought she wanted power. They thought she wanted to rule. They saw her possess an anti-god of the mind, and snap her neck when she was done, and burn a fire anti-god's face, and break a brother's arms off in front of his sister, and they thought: monster. Tyrant. Destroyer.

They were not wrong. She had done all of those things.

But they did not understand why.

Galactix was a master. One of the old powers, older than the anti-gods, older than the Horsemen, old enough to remember when the multiverse was young and the threads of reality were bright and tight and strong. Masters did not have dimensions of their own. They lived above them. Between them. Their job, if a master can be said to have a job, was to keep the threads from tangling.

And the threads had been tangling for ten thousand years.

Since the first war. Since a small dark anti-god of endings had fired one shot at the Seam and corruption had poured out of the wound. Since then, the whole web of the multiverse had been going slack. A little at a time. A thread here, a knot there. Nexus points failing. Dimensions drifting loose. Realities that should have been separate bleeding into each other at the edges, like watercolors on wet paper.

Galactix had felt it happening for ten thousand years. And she had been the only one who noticed.

When the nexus of Terra died, when a boy in silver armor killed a man named Tombstone, she felt the whole web lurch. Like a ship tilting. And she knew, with the cold certainty of something very old, that if one more major nexus failed, the multiverse would not lurch. It would fall.

So she did what masters do. She held it together.

She took Esme because Esme's mind was strong enough to become a nexus. She burned Rui because Rui was in the way. She broke the boy's arms because he would not stop trying to save his sister, and his sister was a conduit for corruption, and corruption was what had started the tangling in the first place. She did every terrible thing she had done because she could see the whole web, and nobody else could, and the web was falling, and she was the only one holding it.

That was what she was for.

And now she was sitting on grey nothing with ordinary hands, and somewhere out there, beyond the walls of this pocket dimension, the multiverse was still tangling. And she could not feel it. Could not reach it. Could not do anything about it at all.

For the first time in her long, long existence, Galactix was afraid.


Chapter Three: Someone Else

She walked.

There was nothing else to do. She could not fly. She could not fold space or tear holes or shoot beams of light. She could only walk, one ordinary foot in front of the other, across the grey nothing, in a direction that was not a direction because there was nothing to measure it against.

She walked for a long time. Days, perhaps. There was no day or night in the void to tell. She did not get hungry. She did not get tired, not exactly. She only got smaller, inside. Quieter. As if the grey were slowly seeping in through her skin.

And then, very far ahead, she saw something that was not grey.

A figure. Sitting. Small and dark.

Galactix stopped.

She knew, the way masters know things, that this was not part of her prison. Her prison was a pocket dimension, a bubble, sealed. Nothing was supposed to be in it but her. And yet here was someone, sitting on the grey ahead of her, perfectly still.

She walked closer.

It was a woman. Small, dark-haired, with a pale scar through one eyebrow and grey eyes that did not glow. She was sitting cross-legged on the nothing, with a pen in one hand made from a long dark feather. There was no paper. She was not writing. She was only sitting, and holding the pen, and looking at nothing at all.

Galactix knew her. Not her face. Her shape. The shape of her power, even faded, even quiet, even put down. Galactix had felt it once before, ten thousand years ago, from very far away: a single precise shot at the Seam, and then the whole multiverse beginning to unravel.

"You," said Galactix.

The small dark woman looked up.

"Hello," she said. "I wondered when someone would come. It's been a very long time."

"You're the anti-god of endings," said Galactix. "You're the one who shot the Seam. You started it. All of it. The corruption. The tangling. Everything I spent ten thousand years trying to hold together." Her ordinary hands were shaking. "You started it."

"Yes," said Kaina.

"How are you here? This is my prison. My pocket dimension."

Kaina considered this.

"I don't think it is, actually," she said. "I think it's mine. I've been here a very long time. They exiled me, after the first war. To a blank at the edge of everything. Nothing begins here, nothing ends. I think when they sealed you, they sealed you into the nearest empty place." She tilted her head. "And this was it. You're in my blank."

Galactix stared at her.

Then she began to laugh. It was not a good laugh. It was the laugh of something very old and very powerful that has just realized it has nothing left.

"Of course," she said. "Of course. Ten thousand years trying to fix what you broke. And they lock me up with you."


Chapter Four: Two Exiles

They did not speak for a long time after that.

Galactix sat down a little way off, on the grey, because her legs would not hold her anymore. Kaina went back to holding her pen and looking at nothing.

Eventually, because there was nothing else to do in the void, Galactix said:

"What are you doing?"

"Nothing," said Kaina.

"You've been doing nothing for ten thousand years?"

"Mostly. For a while I wrote. There was a book. I wrote a prophecy in it, about corruption and how it would end. And then there was a friend here with me. She died. And after that I waited." Kaina turned the feather pen in her fingers. "And then I felt the ending I'd written finally arrive. A girl and her brother. It cost them everything. But it ended. And after that I stopped waiting. I put down my pen. And I've just been sitting here since."

"Doing nothing."

"Letting something begin," said Kaina. "It's different."

Galactix looked at her with contempt.

"Nothing begins here," she said. "You said so yourself. It's a blank."

"I know," said Kaina. "That's why it's so hard."

Galactix turned away.

She sat with her back to the anti-god of endings and stared out across the grey nothing, and she thought about the multiverse. Out there. Beyond the walls of the void. The threads going slack, a little at a time. The nexus points failing. The restored world, with its tower and its monument and its new nexus point, a Horseman of Famine standing in a canyon holding everything together with her bare hands.

It would not last. Galactix knew it would not last. One Horseman could not hold a whole world forever. Sooner or later the nexus would fail. And then the web would lurch. And then it would fall.

And she would be here. In a blank. With nothing in her hands. Unable to do anything about it.

"Do you know what's happening out there?" she said, without turning around.

"Some of it," said Kaina. "I can still see endings. Faintly. From here. They're very far away."

"Then you know it's falling."

"I know it's tangling," said Kaina. "I don't know that it's falling."

"It's the same thing."

"Is it?"


Chapter Five: The Web

Galactix turned around.

"You don't understand," she said. "None of you have ever understood. You see a little piece of it. A world. A war. A girl with a prophecy. I see the whole thing. Every thread. Every knot. And it has been coming apart since the day you fired that shot." She leaned forward. "Do you know what the multiverse is supposed to look like? It's supposed to be a web. Tight. Clean. Every dimension separate, every nexus point bright and hard and holding. That's how it was made. That's how it worked for longer than you can imagine. And then you broke it."

"I know," said Kaina.

"And for ten thousand years I have been the only one holding it together. Do you understand that? The only one. The anti-gods hid or died or went incarnate. The Horsemen rode around ending things. Nobody else could see what was happening. So I held it. Alone. With my bare hands. And everything I did, every vessel I took, every world I burned, every terrible thing they say about me, I did it to keep the web from falling." Her voice cracked. "And they locked me in a box for it."

Kaina was quiet for a long moment.

"Can I tell you what I see?" she said. "From here? I can't see the web the way you do. I can only see endings. But I've been watching them for a long time."

Galactix said nothing.

"I see a restored world," said Kaina. "With a canyon in the middle of it, and a Horseman standing in the canyon, holding it up. And I see her get tired. I see her almost freeze. And I see two sisters come down and feed her. And later, when she needs to leave, I see seven people stand in a ring around her and hold the whole world while she goes." Kaina turned the pen in her fingers. "Seven people. Holding a nexus. Not one master. Seven people who had nothing to do with it, who just decided to help."

Galactix stared at her.

"That's not how it works," she said.

"I know," said Kaina. "It's not how it worked. It's not how it was made. But it's what's happening."

"It's tangling. A nexus held by seven people is a tangle. It's messy. It's not clean."

"Yes," said Kaina. "It's very messy. And it's holding."


Chapter Six: Messy

Galactix did not believe her.

She sat in the grey for a long time and refused to believe it. It was impossible. A nexus was a single point. A bright hard knot. One being holding one world. That was how the masters had made it, at the very beginning, when the multiverse was young. You could not share a nexus. You could not pass it from hand to hand like a bucket of water at a fire. The web did not work that way.

But she could not feel the web anymore. She was in the void. Her powers were gone. And so, for the first time in her existence, she had nothing to go on except what someone else told her.

"Show me," she said at last.

"I can't show you," said Kaina. "I can only tell you."

"Then tell me more."

So Kaina told her.

She told her about the restored world. About the tower with one name on it, and then a second line, carved in the night, and then a whole argument in stone, different hands, different years, covering the bottom of the tower like ivy. About a fire anti-god who had been burned by a master and kept burning anyway. About an anti-god of the mind who had been worn by a master and come out with a quiet voice in her head that she had learned to share. About a girl with a Horseman inside her who had held a line against a pack of hounds with every argument she had ever swallowed. About a lesser anti-god who had let forty thousand people go, and another who had let twenty thousand go, and a town at the edge of the world where the first Horseman wore a paper crown.

"It's messy," said Kaina. "All of it. Nobody's doing it right. They argue about everything. The trains are always late. Nobody can agree on anything. And the web is tangled. You're right. It's more tangled than it's ever been. Threads crossing everywhere. People helping people they have no reason to help. Holding things that aren't theirs to hold."

She paused.

"But I don't see it falling," she said. "I've been watching the endings for a very long time. And I don't see it falling. I see it holding. Not because it's tight. Because it's tangled. Because everyone's holding a piece of everyone else."

Galactix sat very still.

"That can't work," she said. But her voice was smaller than before.

"I didn't think so either," said Kaina. "But I'm the anti-god of endings, and I've been looking for this one for a very long time. And I can't find it. The end of the restored world. I keep looking and it isn't there. Not yet. Maybe not for a very long time."


Chapter Seven: What She Did

"Then everything I did," said Galactix slowly, "was for nothing."

Kaina did not answer.

"I took Esme," said Galactix. "I wore her for eleven days. I needed her mind to make a nexus. And then I snapped her neck. Because she was used up." She looked at her ordinary hands. "I burned the fire one. Rui. I touched her for less than a second and her face melted. I broke the boy's arms off. I broke his leg. In front of his sister. I tried to make her my vessel, his sister, the corruption one, because I thought if I held the corruption I could stop the tangling." Her voice was shaking now. "I did all of that because I thought the web would fall if I didn't. Because I thought I was the only one holding it. And you're telling me it didn't need me. That seven people with no power at all held a nexus I spent ten thousand years trying to save."

"Yes," said Kaina.

Galactix put her face in her ordinary hands.

She had never done that before. She had never had hands that small. She had never had anything to hide her face from.

"I'm a monster," she said.

"You did monstrous things," said Kaina. "So did I. I killed four thousand anti-gods on the eastern front in one afternoon. I shot my best friend in the neck. I wrote the line in the prophecy that made a boy stab his sister. Not on purpose. But my hand moved." She turned the pen in her fingers. "I don't know if that makes us monsters. I've had ten thousand years to think about it and I still don't know."

"Then what does it make us?"

Kaina thought about it for a long time.

"Tired," she said finally. "It makes us very, very tired. From holding on to things that were never ours to hold."


Chapter Eight: Letting Go

Galactix did not sleep, in the void. There was no sleeping there. But after a long time, sitting in the grey with her face in her hands, she found that she had stopped thinking. Just for a while. Just a little.

It was the strangest feeling. In her whole existence, she had never once stopped thinking about the web. The threads. The knots. Every second, every moment, holding. She had never once put it down.

Now she could not hold it. Her powers were gone. And without them, without the web humming in her fingers, she found there was a great quiet space inside her where all that holding used to be.

She did not know what to do with it.

"How did you do it?" she asked Kaina at last. "Put down your pen. After ten thousand years. How did you just—stop?"

Kaina looked down at the feather pen in her hand. She was still holding it. She had been holding it since Galactix arrived.

"I didn't just stop," she said. "I waited until the ending I wrote arrived. And then I saw it was over. And then I let it be over." She looked up. "And then, for a long time, I didn't know what to do. Like you. Sitting in the grey with nothing in my hands. And then I realized I had something I'd never had before."

"What?"

"Nothing to do," said Kaina. "No endings to pull. No prophecies to write. No war. Just—nothing. And nothing is a very strange thing to have, when you've only ever had something." She smiled, very slightly. "It's like a blank page. I've written on a lot of pages. I've never once looked at one and not written."

Galactix looked out at the grey. The endless, empty, page-colored grey of the void.

"A blank page," she said.

"A blank page," said Kaina.

And then Kaina did something Galactix did not expect. She held out the pen. The long dark feather. Formality's feather, from the first war, the pen that had written the prophecy that ended the world.

"Here," she said.

Galactix stared at it.

"What am I supposed to do with that? There's no paper."

"I know," said Kaina. "That's the point. You hold it. And you don't write anything. And you see what that feels like."

Galactix reached out with one ordinary hand and took the pen.

It was very light. Lighter than anything she had ever held. Lighter than a thread of reality. Lighter than a nexus. Lighter than a world.

She held it, and did not write anything.

It felt like falling. Like letting go of the edge of a cliff and finding out there was no bottom, only more air, forever, holding you up.


Chapter Nine: The Visitor

They sat together in the void for a long time after that. Two exiles. The anti-god who had started the tangling and the master who had tried to stop it. Neither of them did anything. That was, they found, harder than anything either of them had ever done.

And then, one grey day that was not a day, someone came.

Not through the walls. The walls of the void could not be broken; Modify had sealed them himself, with the mage weaving magic into every stitch. Someone came the way Kaina had once been visited by an ending, the way a dream visits a sleeper. Faint. Flickering. Barely there.

It was a girl. Silver-haired. Eyes flickering red. Standing on the grey nothing a little way off, looking at them.

Galactix knew her at once.

"Corrupt," she said. Her voice was very quiet.

"Ena," said the girl. "My name's Ena." She looked from Galactix to Kaina and back again. "I'm not really here. I'm displacing. Residual corruption. I can go almost anywhere for a moment, if I push hard enough. I can't stay. I just—" She stopped. "I just wanted to see you."

Galactix stood up. Her ordinary legs shook.

"You wanted to see me," she repeated. "I tried to make you a vessel. I broke your brother. I—"

"I know what you did," said Ena. "I was there."

"Then why are you here?"

Ena was quiet for a long moment. Her flickering form wavered in the grey.

"Because I found out why you did it," she said. "Esme told me. She saw it, while you were wearing her. She saw the whole web. Everything you saw. The tangling. The falling. She said you weren't trying to rule. You were trying to hold on." Ena's red eyes did not blink. "I know what that's like. I held on to my brother so hard I killed half the world. I held on to the corruption so long I forgot who I was. I know what it's like to do monstrous things because you're terrified of letting go."

Galactix could not speak.

"I'm not here to forgive you," said Ena. "I don't know if I can. Not for Mods. Not for Esme. Not yet. Maybe not ever." Her voice cracked. "But I'm here to tell you it's holding. The web. The world. All of it. We're holding it. Together. It's messy and everybody argues and the trains are late. But it's holding. You can stop."

She looked down at the feather pen in Galactix's ordinary hand.

"You can stop," she said again. "You can let go. We've got it."

And then she flickered, and was gone.


Chapter Ten: Blank

Galactix stood in the grey for a long time after Ena vanished, holding the pen.

Kaina did not say anything. She only sat beside her, small and dark and quiet, with her scarred eyebrow and her grey eyes that did not glow, and waited.

At last, Galactix sat down.

"She said we've got it," she said.

"She did," said Kaina.

"She meant it. I could tell. Even without my powers. She meant it."

"Yes."

Galactix looked down at the pen. At the long dark feather.

"I don't know how to be nothing," she said. "I've always been something. A master. The one holding the web. The one who saw everything. I don't know how to sit in a blank and not hold anything."

"Neither did I," said Kaina. "It took me a long time."

"How long?"

Kaina considered.

"I'll let you know," she said. "I'm still learning."

Galactix looked at her. At the small, dark, tired anti-god of endings who had started everything. Who had broken the web, and written the prophecy, and been exiled, and lost her only friend, and waited ten thousand years for an ending to arrive. Who was sitting in the grey with her, now, and not leaving.

"Why are you still here?" said Galactix. "You could have gone. When you let go. You could have—I don't know. Faded. Ended. You're the anti-god of endings. You could have ended yourself. Why are you still here?"

Kaina was quiet for a long time.

"Because I had a friend," she said finally. "A long time ago. In this same blank. She came here with me when they exiled me. She didn't have to. She just did. And she died here, with her head on my shoulder. And I never looked at her ending, not even once, because she asked me not to. And she told me she wanted to be surprised with someone she loved." She looked at Galactix. "I've been here alone a very long time. I didn't know I was waiting for anyone. But then you came. And I thought—maybe I'd like to be surprised too."

Galactix did not know what to say.

So she did the only thing she could think of. The thing she had never done in her whole long existence. She held out her ordinary hand, the one that was not holding the pen.

Kaina looked at it.

Then she took it.

And the two of them sat together in the void, at the edge of everything, in the blank page of the grey, holding hands, holding a pen they did not write with, holding nothing else at all.


Chapter Eleven: Surprised

There is no ending to this story.

That is the strange thing about it. Every other story about the multiverse, about the corruption and the anti-gods and the Horsemen and the girl with shining blonde hair, has an ending. Kaina could see all of them, faintly, from the blank at the edge of everything. Endings were what she was made for.

But she never looked at this one.

She never looked at how long she and Galactix would sit in the grey. She never looked at whether the master would ever learn to be nothing, or whether the void would ever open, or whether one day a silver-haired girl would come back, flickering, and say: I forgive you. She never looked at whether the web would hold, or fall, or tangle into something so strange and messy and alive that no master would ever recognize it.

She did not look because she had promised someone, once, a long time ago, that she would not. And because, for the first time in her very long existence, she wanted to be surprised.

Out there, beyond the walls of the void, the restored world went on. Messy. Loud. Tangled. A Horseman stood in a canyon holding it, and seven people held it with her when she needed them to. A tower stood at the center of it, covered in a long argument in stone. A girl with a paper crown taught in a schoolhouse with a good roof. A former soup drove west in a Buick. The trains were late.

It held.

And in a blank at the edge of everything, a master who had spent ten thousand years trying to hold the multiverse together sat in the grey with her ordinary hands, beside the anti-god who had broken it, and between them, held lightly, so lightly, a long dark feather that had written the end of the world.

Neither of them wrote anything.

The page stayed blank.

And for the first time in either of their lives, that was enough.
