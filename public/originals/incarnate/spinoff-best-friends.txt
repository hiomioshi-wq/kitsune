Incarnate: Best Friends
A spinoff of the Corruption Saga

Trigger warning: This story contains mind control, being trapped in your own body, grief, the death of a best friend, a family that can't reach you, and a narrator who takes something that isn't his and calls it fixing.
Read at your own risk.



Chapter One: The Sentence

We're still best friends.

That's what my mouth says. Over and over. I can hear it. I can hear everything. That's the part nobody gets.

They think I'm gone. My mom. The priest. The lady from the county with the clipboard who comes every Thursday and talks to me in that slow voice like I'm a dog that might bite. They look at my face and my blue eyes and they hear me say the sentence and they think: nobody home.

I'm home. I'm so home. I'm in here in the dark behind my own face, banging on the inside of my eyes like a guy trapped in a car underwater.

We're still best friends.

Mark did this. Mark, my best friend since second grade when he gave me half his Lunchable because my mom forgot to pack one. Mark, who I helped get five hundred gallons of pudding (forty) because he wanted a girl to look at him. Mark climbed in my window and told me not to tell anyone what he'd become, and I said no, and he said yes for me.

I don't think he meant to leave me like this. I think he meant to fix one thing and he fixed everything. I think that's what that song does. It doesn't have a small setting.

We're still best friends.

I hate the sentence. I hate it so much. I hate that it's true.



Chapter Two: What It's Like

Okay, so, what it's like.

You know when you're falling asleep and you try to move and you can't, sleep paralysis, and there's that second of pure terror where your brain's awake and your body isn't? It's that. Except it's been eleven days. Except my body isn't asleep. It's awake and doing stuff without me. It sits up. It eats if somebody puts food in front of it, slow, like a cow. It goes to the bathroom on its own, thank God, I don't know what I'd do if it didn't. And it says the sentence.

I can steer it a tiny bit. Like, a really tiny bit. If I push with everything I've got, I can move one finger. Day four I got my pinky to tap the blanket twice. My mom didn't see. She was crying into the phone in the hallway.

Day seven, I tried to say "mom." I pushed so hard I thought something in my head would snap. And my mouth opened and I felt the word come up my throat, M, M, and then the song caught it, like a hand catching a ball, and turned it.

"We're still best friends."

My mom stopped in the doorway and looked at me. For a second her face lit up. Like she thought it meant something. Like it was a message.

Then it went out again.

I'm sorry, Mom. I'm right here. I'm right here.

We're still best friends.



Chapter Three: Mom

My mom's name is Adaeze Okafor and she's a nurse at County General, night shift, and she's the toughest person I've ever met. She raised me alone after my dad left when I was four. She works twelve-hour shifts and then comes home and cleans and cooks and asks me about my day. She once chased a guy who stole her purse for three blocks in Crocs and got it back.

She's falling apart.

I can watch it happen from in here. I can't look away. That's another thing about this. If she's in front of my eyes, I have to see her. I can't close them. I can't turn my head.

She sleeps in the chair next to my bed now. She brings me my favorite foods, jollof rice, suya, those gross gas station nachos I like, she brings them like she's trying to remind my body who it is. She talks to me. She tells me stories about when I was little. She tells me about the time I tried to give the cat a bath in the toilet. She tells me about my first word, which was "more."

She thinks I can't hear.

Day nine the county lady said "facility." I heard it. My mom heard it. My mom said, "No." Then my mom went out to the car and sat in it in the driveway for two hours, and when she came back in, her eyes were red, and she sat in the chair and held my hand, and she said, really quiet, "I don't know what else to do, baby."

We're still best friends, I said.

She laughed. One little broken laugh. "Yeah," she said. "Yeah, I know."



Chapter Four: The Night It Broke

Day eleven, around eleven at night, something happened three states away.

I felt it before I knew what it was. Like a rubber band snapping, way off, and the snap traveling all the way back down the band to my end. A jolt. In my chest. In my head. In the song.

And the song in me screamed.

Not my mouth. The song. The blue stuff Mark put in me. It screamed like a dog when its owner dies, this high, thin, terrible sound only I could hear, and it pulled. It pulled back toward wherever Mark was, like it was trying to go home, like it was trying to follow him.

And then it let go.

Of me.

All at once. Like a hand opening.

I sat up in bed. Me. Not my body. Me. I sat up because I wanted to sit up. I put my hands in front of my face and my hands were mine.

My mom was asleep in the chair. Her head on her shoulder. Her mouth a little open. A plate of nachos on the windowsill getting cold.

I opened my mouth.

"Mom," I said.

She woke up so fast she fell out of the chair.



Chapter Five: Mark Is Dead

I knew before anyone told me. I knew because of the snap. You don't feel a rubber band let go like that unless the other end is gone.

Raven told me for sure.

She showed up three days later at my front door at six in the morning. My mom was at work. I was eating cereal like a normal person, which I'd been doing for three days straight, just eating cereal, standing in the kitchen, crying for no reason, saying words, any words, words that weren't the sentence.

Raven looked like hell. Like actual hell. Her clothes were torn. Her face was covered in dirt. Her eyes were normal again, brown, but they had this look like she'd seen the bottom of something and it had seen her back.

"It ate him," she said. "The thing I brought. It was supposed to eat both of them. Calypso pushed him in and ran."

I just stood there with the cereal spoon.

"I remember him now," Raven said. Her voice cracked. "When he went, it came back. Everything. The barn. The pudding. You. Him. All of it. He wiped me so I wouldn't stop him, and I fed a boy to a monster to get strong enough to stop him anyway, and then I watched him get eaten by it, and he looked at me right before. He was sorry. I could see it."

She sat down on my kitchen floor. Just sat down. Like her legs quit.

"Devon Krause is dead," she said. "That was me. I did that."

I put the spoon down. I sat down on the floor next to her. We sat there, two kids on a linoleum floor at six in the morning, and neither of us said anything for a really long time.

Then Raven said, "Brad. Your eyes."

I looked at the toaster. Chrome. You can see yourself in it if you lean.

My eyes were brown. But at the very, very edges. Just a little.

Blue.



Chapter Six: Leftovers

It didn't all leave.

That's what Raven figured out, sitting on my kitchen floor with her dirty hands holding my face, staring into my eyes like a doctor. When Mark died, the song let go of me. But some of it got left behind. Stuck. Like when you pour out a glass of chocolate milk and there's still a brown ring at the bottom.

"It's a little," she said. "It's so little. But it's there. And it's going to want to grow. It's going to whisper. That's what it did to him. It starts with a lock clicking open."

I thought about Mark at his locker, two weeks after the barn. I can hear the numbers. It's humming them.

"Pull it out," I said. "You can do that. You said you could do that. You had the vial thing."

"I lost the vial. Mark took it." She wiped her face. "I can do it without one. It'll hurt. It'll hurt more than it hurt him."

"Okay."

"And I don't..." She looked at her hands. At the chalk ground into the knuckles. "I don't have anything left, Brad. I gave it everything. The thing under the barn. It's gone. It went with Mark into the ground somewhere in Kansas and it's not coming back to me. I'm just a girl with chalk now. I'm just a girl."

"So you can't do it."

"I can try. Tomorrow. I need to sleep. I need to sleep so bad."

She fell asleep right there, on my kitchen floor, curled up next to the dishwasher.

And I sat there and listened to the leftovers.

They were quiet. They were humming. Not words. Just a feeling. And the feeling was: you could fix things.



Chapter Seven: The Clipboard

The county lady came that afternoon. Thursday. Like always.

She didn't know I was better. Mom hadn't called her. Mom was waiting to see if it would last, I think. Mom didn't trust anything anymore.

I answered the door.

The county lady's name was Ms. Pruitt. She had the clipboard. She looked at me, standing there, normal, talking, and her face went through about nine things.

"Bradley," she said. "You're... you're speaking."

"Yeah."

"This is wonderful. This is... I'll need to do a full evaluation, of course. And given the severity of the episode, I really do think, for your safety, a residential facility for observation would be—"

The leftovers hummed.

It was so easy. That's what I want you to understand. Mark said that too, I bet. It's so easy. I didn't plan it. I didn't sing. I just looked at her and thought really hard about how much I didn't want to go to a facility, how much my mom didn't want me to go, how tired my mom was, how she'd chased a purse thief three blocks in Crocs.

And I said, "You don't think I need to go anywhere. Right?"

Her pupils went big. Soft. A thread of blue at the edges.

"You don't need to go anywhere," Ms. Pruitt said.

"You're going to close my file."

"I'm going to close your file."

"And you're never coming back."

"I'm never coming back."

She walked to her car. She drove away. She didn't look back.

I closed the door. My hands were shaking. I felt good. I felt so, so good. I felt like I'd finally done something right.

Raven was standing in the hallway behind me. She'd woken up. She'd seen all of it.

She didn't say anything. She didn't have to.

"It was just her," I said. "It was just a clipboard lady. That's it. That's all I'm going to do."

Raven looked at me for a long time.

"That's exactly what he said," she said.



Chapter Eight: Ray

Mark's funeral was on Saturday.

There wasn't a body. There wasn't anything. There was a photo of him from school picture day, the one where his hair is doing that weird flip, on an easel at the front of Grace Methodist, and there were maybe twenty people, mostly church ladies, and there was me, and there was Raven in the back row in her black everything, and there was Ray.

Mark's dad. In a suit too big for him. With his apron on over it.

He hadn't changed. Nobody had fixed him. When Mark died, the song let go of me, but it didn't let go of Ray, because Ray didn't have any of Mark in him, just the order. Love you. Proud of you. Like a wind-up toy nobody wound down.

He stood next to the easel and shook everybody's hand as they came in.

"Love you! Proud of you!"

The church ladies cried. They thought it was grief.

When it was my turn, I shook his hand, and his eyes were blue all the way across, and he smiled at me with Mark's same crooked teeth.

"Love you! Proud of you!"

And I thought about Mark at seven, at my lunch table, with half a Lunchable. And Mark at twelve, after his mom left, sleeping on my floor for a week because he couldn't be in his house. And Mark at sixteen, flinching in his kitchen when the beer can hit the sink. And Mark in my window, saying I just need you to not tell. And Mark three states away, falling.

He never got to hear it. Not really. Not from the real one.

The leftovers hummed.

You could fix this.



Chapter Nine: Love You, Proud Of You

Here's what I did. I'm telling you so somebody knows.

After the funeral, after everybody left, after Raven went outside to sit on the church steps and not talk to me, I stayed. I sat down next to Ray in the front pew. He was still smiling at the easel.

I could have made him normal. Maybe. Maybe the leftovers were strong enough. I could have told him: you're Ray again. You remember everything. You're sad. Your son is dead and you're sad and you did a lot of things to him you need to feel bad about. Feel it. All of it.

That's what fixing would have been.

Instead, I said, "Ray. Who am I?"

He looked at me. Blue.

I pushed. So gently. The way you push a swing.

"I'm your son," I said. "Right?"

"You're my son," Ray said.

"Brad's your son."

"Brad's my son."

"And you've always been proud of me."

"I've always been proud of you." He put his big hand on the back of my neck. It was warm. "Love you, son. Proud of you."

I closed my eyes.

I'm not stupid. I know what I did. I know it's Mark's. All of it. The words, the hand, the son. I took my dead best friend's dad, and I took the thing his dad never once said to him for real, and I put it on like a jacket. Mark spent sixteen years waiting for that sentence and got a fake one for two weeks, and I took it off his grave and kept it.

I could say it was for Ray. So he'd have somebody. So he wouldn't be alone in that kitchen with that apron saying it to the toaster.

It wasn't for Ray.

My dad left when I was four. My mom is the toughest person I've ever met, and I love her, and she has never once in my life had time to say she's proud of me, because she was working, because she was always working, because of me.

I wanted to hear it. That's all. I just wanted to hear it once, from a dad.

So now I hear it every day. I go over after school. Ray makes pancakes. He's getting better at them. He tells me he loves me. He tells me he's proud of me. My mom thinks I'm volunteering, helping a grieving father. She's proud of me too, now. She says it at dinner, tired, over the jollof rice. It's real when she says it. That's how I know Ray's isn't.

I keep going back anyway.

Raven doesn't talk to me anymore. She just watches me from across the cafeteria with her arms crossed, like a bouncer, like she used to watch Calypso.

I know what she's waiting for. She's waiting for the locks to start humming.

They already are. A little. Just a little.

Sometimes, when I'm lying in bed, I catch myself saying the old sentence. Not because the song makes me. Because I want to. To him. To the ceiling. To the dirt somewhere in Kansas.

We're still best friends.

We're still best friends.

We're still best friends.
