Incarnate: Wall of String
A spinoff of the Corruption Saga. During Main Character, Uncut.

Trigger warning: This story contains obsession, occult ritual, self-harm for ritual purposes, the deaths of hospital patients, violation of consent, arrest, adult language, and a narrator who is right about the monster and wrong about everything else.
Read at your own risk.



Chapter One: The Wall

My wall is forty feet of red string.

It used to be my dorm room wall. Then my roommate moved out. Then the RA stopped knocking. Then the university sent three emails about "concerns for my wellbeing" and one about "unauthorized open flames" and then they stopped too. Now it's just me and the wall.

Pins. Photos. Printouts. A newspaper drawing from Switzerland, 1891. A Greek vase with a woman on a rock and a ship tilting toward her. A sailor's diary from 1712 describing "a maiden of foam who sang us under." Footage of a cafeteria with pudding on the floor. Footage of a gym in Kansas with a crack down the middle. Footage of a town in Illinois. Footage of a Walmart. Footage of a church in Harrisburg where four thousand people sing to nothing. And red string from all of them to the middle.

The middle is a photo of a girl in a crown.

My name is Juniper Vance. I'm twenty. I'm a sophomore at Ohio State, technically. I read tarot. I burn candles. In my junior year of high school at Fairmont West in Dayton, a cheerleader named Calypso walked up to my tarot table at the spring carnival and I flipped a card for her that wasn't in my deck.

It was blank. Totally white.

Nothing there. A space where a person should be.

I've been chasing that blank card for four years.



Chapter Two: What Burning Does

I'm not a witch. I want to be clear. I'm not like that goth girl in Kansas whose family had a deal with something under a barn. I don't have a deal. I don't have a grandmother with secrets. I have a library card and a lot of free time and a really, really bad feeling about one specific cheerleader.

But I figured out one thing. By accident. The first time she sang at me.

She sings, and you want her. That's how it works. It goes in through your bones and finds whatever you want most and turns it toward her. And then she eats it.

But she can't eat what you don't want. And there's one thing you can do that nobody wants. Pain.

So I cut my thumb. And I wrote her name in my own blood. And I burned it.

And for a second, I didn't want her. I hurt too much to want anything. And the song slid off me like water off wax.

And she felt it.

I don't know how I know. I just know. Across Dayton, in her perfect bedroom, the girl in the crown flinched.

I've burned her name nine hundred and twelve times since then. I keep count. On the wall. Under the string.



Chapter Three: The Hum

When the hum started, I was the only one in my dorm who didn't hum.

I'd burned her name that morning. I burn it every morning. Thumb, paper, candle. So when the song came through the walls, through the phones, through the radiators, through everyone on campus, I just felt it go past me like wind. Like standing on a platform while a train goes by.

And everyone else got on the train.

I watched it happen out my window. Kids on the quad stopping mid-step. Smiling. Humming. A frisbee dropping out of the air and nobody picking it up. By the end of the week, half of my lecture hall was sitting in their seats with their eyes blue at the edges, humming at the professor, who was humming back.

She did it. She actually did it. She's everywhere.

I made a new pin for the middle of the wall. It says: EVERYTHING.

And I started looking for doors.



Chapter Four: The Receipt

Here's my theory. I've had four years to work on it. I've read every book about sirens in three library systems. I've read Homer in Greek, badly.

When she eats a want, it doesn't vanish. Wants are stories. She's made of stories. She eats stories. And stories go somewhere. They go into her. She's got forty thousand years of other people's wanting in her like a landfill. Every sailor. Every Swiss girl. Every kid in every gym.

And everyone she's eaten still has a thread to what she took. Like a phantom limb. Like a receipt.

If I can find someone with a receipt, and pull on the thread hard enough, I can pull the want back out of her. And she'll feel it. If it's big enough, the song will stutter. A crack. In the voice of everything.

I don't know what happens after the crack. Maybe someone else does something. Maybe the gods get a second to fight back. Maybe the Ohio girl who's been right about everything for four years finally gets to say I told you so.

I need a receipt.

I found Noor in the background of a livestream. Week four. Eleven seconds. Basement apartment, Pittsburgh. A girl on a mattress, staring at the ceiling, while Calypso sang into a front desk clerk's phone.

The saddest face I've ever seen.

And then, two weeks later, the same girl, in the background of a different stream. Making tea. Smiling.

Something huge got eaten out of that girl.



Chapter Five: Noor Said No

You know this part. She told you.

I'll just say: I didn't expect it. Four years, and the one thing I never put on the wall was that someone might choose her. Not because they were under. Because they liked it better.

"Like me," Noor said. "Happy?"

And I didn't have anything to say to that. I'd spent four years making sure I was never happy, never wanting, never soft, so the song couldn't get in. And here was a girl who'd gotten everything I'd been fighting off, and she was sitting on her mattress with a boy who makes cats in foam, and she was fine.

I left.

Here's the part she didn't tell you, because she doesn't know.

On the way up her steps, I stopped at her door. There was a hairbrush on a shelf by the coat hooks. I took three hairs out of it.

Receipts don't need permission. They just need a body.



Chapter Six: Cleveland

There's a hospital in Cleveland. You've seen it on the news.

Saint Columba's. Twelve floors. Since the hum started, nobody there is sad. The cancer ward. The ICU. The hospice on the ninth floor. All of it. Patients who were screaming in pain three weeks ago are lying in their beds, smiling, humming. Families who were sobbing in waiting rooms are sitting in rows, humming, peaceful, watching their mothers and fathers and children die with smiles on their faces.

The news calls it a miracle.

The news is under too.

I knew what it was. She'd come through Cleveland on her way east. She'd eaten the whole hospital. All that wanting. All those people wanting more time, wanting a cure, wanting to say goodbye, wanting to not be afraid. The most concentrated wanting on the planet. A hospital is a church with worse lighting.

Hundreds of receipts. All in one building.

I took the bus to Cleveland with seven black candles, a piece of chalk, my good knife, three of Noor Haddad's hairs, and a folded bus ticket Calypso had touched.



Chapter Seven: Ninth Floor

Getting in was easy. Nobody in that building wants anything anymore. Not even to stop me. The security guard at the front desk smiled at me and hummed. The nurse at the ninth-floor station smiled at me and hummed. Nobody asked where I was going.

The hospice ward is a long hallway of rooms with the doors open. Each room has a bed. Each bed has someone in it. Each someone is smiling.

There was an old woman in 904 who'd been dying for months. Her chart was on the door. Pancreatic. Stage four. The note in the margin from three weeks ago, in a nurse's handwriting: patient very frightened, requested chaplain, asking for son. And under it, from after the hum: patient calm and content.

Her son was sitting next to her bed. Humming.

I drew the circle in the hallway. Big. Big enough to touch every doorframe. I set the seven candles at the points. I put the bus ticket in the center. I put Noor's three hairs on top of it. I cut my thumb, and I wrote her name in the center, nine hundred and thirteen.

And I lit the candles.



Chapter Eight: Pulling

It's not magic the way people think. There aren't sparkles. There's a feeling like a fishing line going taut.

I felt the receipts. Every one of them. Threads, coming out of every room on the ninth floor, out of every dying smiling person, up through the ceiling, out into the sky, toward the east, toward the voice. Hundreds of them. Glowing. Thin. Blue.

And one more. From the hair in the center. Out the window, toward Pittsburgh.

I grabbed them all. In my mind. In my bleeding hand. And I pulled.

And in every room on the ninth floor of Saint Columba's, the smiling stopped.

It went out of them like a light. And what came back in was everything she'd eaten. All at once. The fear. The pain. The wanting more time. The wanting a cure. The wanting to say goodbye. The old woman in 904 opened her eyes and screamed. Her son stopped humming and grabbed her hand and screamed too. Up and down the hall, room after room, a hundred people woke up into the worst moment of their lives, the one she'd taken from them, all at once, at full strength.

The sound was unbearable.

I kept pulling.

Because I felt it. Far away, east, at the bottom of everything. The voice. The song that's in every world. It hitched.

For one second, across the whole multiverse, the song of Calypso skipped a beat, like a record when someone bumps the table.

I felt her flinch.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. I was standing in a hallway full of screaming dying people with blood running down my wrist and I laughed, because four years, because the blank card, because I was right.

Then the old woman in 904 stopped screaming.

She'd died. Mid-scream. Afraid. Asking for her son, who was right there.

And then the man in 905.

And then 907.



Chapter Nine: Pittsburgh

I didn't feel the thread to Pittsburgh go until later.

The hair burned up in the center of the circle. All three. Bright blue. And something came loose at the other end of it. Not all of it. Not the whole boy. Just a piece. A little piece of somebody's brother came flying back out of the voice like a spark out of a fire, back toward where it belonged.

But Noor didn't want it. She'd said no. She'd closed every door to it.

So it went to the next place that wanted him.

I found out on the news, later, in a holding cell. A lemon tree in Pittsburgh. In a pot. On a porch. Bloomed overnight. First time in nine months. A woman holding a lemon, crying.

I don't know if that's him. I don't know if that's a piece of a twelve-year-old boy who used to water that tree and believe in it hard enough. I think it might be.

I think I put a dead kid in a lemon tree because his sister wouldn't take him back.

I put that on the wall in my head. Next to 904.



Chapter Ten: Holding

Security came eventually. It took them a while. They were all humming. But the screaming got through even to them, eventually, enough that a guard came up the stairs and found a girl in a circle of candles, bleeding, laughing, with a hallway of dead and dying people around her.

Nine people died on the ninth floor of Saint Columba's that night. Eleven more the next day. They would have died anyway. That's what I told the detective. They were in hospice. They were going to die this week, this month. All I did was give them back what was theirs.

"They were happy," the detective said. He'd been humming when he came in. He stopped, after he saw the pictures. "They were dying happy. You made them die terrified."

"They were dying eaten," I said. "That's not happy. That's empty."

"Who are you to decide that?"

"Somebody who's not humming," I said.

He hummed. He couldn't help it. It came up out of him like a hiccup. He looked embarrassed.

They put me in a cell. Charged me with nine counts of something, I don't know, they read it too fast. They let me make one call.

I called a basement apartment in Squirrel Hill.

"Noor," I said, when she picked up. "Noor, listen to me. It worked. The song skipped. I felt it. If you'd helped, if you'd let me pull you, the whole thing, your brother, all of him, it would have been so much bigger, it could have—"

She hung up.

I screamed her name at the cameras on the way to the van. I don't know why. I think I wanted her to know. I think I wanted someone to know that I was right.



Chapter Eleven: Halcyon

I'm writing this in a cell in Cuyahoga County with a pencil they let me have for my "statement."

Here's what I learned in Cleveland. One hallway of receipts made the voice of everything skip for one second. One hundred people. One second.

Halcyon, Illinois has two thousand three hundred and forty.

She ate the whole town. Every main want of every person. The mayor's ambition. The Dairy Queen manager's son. The old lady's dead husband. Two thousand three hundred and forty receipts, all in one place, all standing on Main Street, smiling, waiting.

If I pull all of them, at once, the song won't skip. It'll break.

It'll also kill them. Probably. Most of them. Everyone she ate down to the floor. When the wanting comes back that hard, all at once, into somebody who's been empty for months, the body can't hold it. Like the old woman in 904. Screaming, then gone.

Two thousand three hundred and forty people.

Against everything.

I know what you're going to say. Everyone in this saga says it to everyone. It's a sacrifice we have to make. Himo said it. Eri said it. The goth girl in Kansas said it with a boy named Devon. I've read the whole saga, okay? I know how it sounds. I know every single person who ever said that sentence was wrong.

I don't care.

Because I'm the only one who's not humming. I'm the only one who saw the blank card. I'm the only one who's been right this whole time, and everybody called me crazy, and the RA sent emails, and my roommate moved out, and my mom stopped calling, and Noor Haddad hung up on me. I'm right. I'm right. I'm right.

My public defender is coming tomorrow. She hums. Everyone hums. It's going to be so easy to walk out of here. Nobody wants anything enough to stop me.

I've got the pencil. I've got my thumb. I've got her name.

Nine hundred and fourteen.

Halcyon's six hours by bus.
