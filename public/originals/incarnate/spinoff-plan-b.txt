Incarnate: Plan B
A spinoff of the Corruption Saga

Trigger warning: This story contains experimentation on sentient beings, the murder of parents in front of their children, manufactured grief, war, genocide, and a narrator who has never once been sorry.
Read at your own risk.



Chapter One: Log Zero

LOG 0.
Subject: me.
Objective: end the gods.
Method: pending.
Note: Do not get attached.

I wrote that on the first page of the first notebook a very long time ago, before the war had a number, and I have kept that notebook ever since. The pages are brown now. The ink has gone the color of dried blood. I still read it every morning. It's the only scripture I've ever believed in.

My name is Himo. You've heard of me. You've heard I started the first war, and betrayed the other gods, and made the corruption, and built the Nursery, and threw my children into hell, and that I am, in the words of a very old and very foul-mouthed friend of mine, "that fucking bitch."

All true.

What nobody ever tells you is why. They don't tell you because they don't know, and they don't know because they never asked, because it's easier to have a monster than a reason.

So here's the reason. Then you can decide.



Chapter Two: Before

Before the war, there were the anti-gods, and there were a great many of us, and we did not die.

That's the whole problem. Write it down. We did not die.

Do you know what something becomes when it doesn't die? When it has forever? When nothing it does ever has an ending, and so nothing it does ever has a cost?

It becomes bored.

And bored gods are the most dangerous things in any universe.

I watched them. For longer than your sun has burned. I watched Conquest ride into worlds just to watch them kneel. I watched Famine starve a planet to see what the last ones would eat. I watched gods of water drown continents because they were having a bad century. I watched a god of fire set a sky alight because a mortal had sung a song she didn't like. And when they were done, they went back to their palaces and slept for a thousand years and woke up and did it again, and again, and again.

Nobody stopped them. Nobody could. You can't punish something with forever. Forever is the reward.

I was one of them. I was the anti-god of making. I could build anything. Bodies. Minds. Machines. Worlds. And one day, after watching a god of space fold a solar system in half like a napkin because it was in the way of his view, I sat down in my lab and I thought:

What if they could die?

What if I made something that could make them die?

Chapter Three: Corruption

It took me forty thousand years.

You don't need the chemistry. You couldn't follow it anyway, no offense. Here's the shape. A god is a story that's been told so many times it can't stop being told. Corruption is a typo. A tiny error that goes into the story and repeats. Every time the god is told, the error is told too, and it grows, and it changes the words around it, until the story is saying something else. Until the god of fire is the god of ash. Until the god of the mind is a screaming hole. Until the god is something that can end.

Corruption-001 killed my lab assistant in eleven seconds. He was a minor god of doorways. I liked him. He made good tea. I wrote down the time of death and moved on.

Corruption-002 through -026 were failures. Too fast. Too slow. Too loud. One of them made everything it touched fall in love with itself. One of them turned out to be a song, a hungry blue song, and it got out of the jar and into one of my early subjects, a pretty little thing I'd made to test it on, and she ate four of my technicians and swam away laughing, and I didn't have time to chase her. I hear she's in high school now. Good for her.

Corruption-027 worked.

I sat with the jar in my lap for three days before I opened it. I'll give myself that. Three days. I thought about everyone I knew. Chimera, who I'd loved once, in the way gods love, which is badly and for a very long time. My sisters. My makers. The god of doorways and his tea.

Then I thought about the folded solar system. I thought about the planet Famine starved. I thought about the song the fire god didn't like and the mortal who sang it.

I opened the jar.



Chapter Four: The First War

They call it a betrayal. It was.

I went to the great hall where the anti-gods met once every ten thousand years to argue about nothing, and I walked in with corruption on my hands like perfume, and I shook every hand in the room.

Every hand.

Chimera's last. She looked at me. She'd always been able to tell when I was lying. She held my hand a second too long.

"What did you do?" she said.

"Soi Kijo," I said. It's an old phrase. Older than the hall. "Joi Kiieanian Kio."

Sorry. But the war is over.

It wasn't over. It had just started. It took three days for the first ones to turn. Then a week for the first to die. Then it was everywhere, gods turning on gods, corrupted ones hunting clean ones, clean ones hunting me, worlds burning in the crossfire. Kaina, my best killer, the only one who understood what I was doing, went through my own kind like a scythe through wheat. Trillions of mortals died in the fighting. I did the math. I'd done it already, before I opened the jar. Trillions now, against the countless trillions who would die over forever if the gods kept going.

It was a good trade. I'd make it again. I've made it every day since.

But the gods are hard to kill even when they can die. The war went on for an age. The corruption burned through most of them and then, like all sicknesses, it slowed. The survivors learned to resist. Some of them learned to use it. Tombstone. Galactix. Things worse than the ones I was trying to kill.

And the gods who were left, the clean ones, found my lab.

They didn't kill me. That's the cruelty. They couldn't, not right away. I'd made myself very hard to kill. So they sealed me under the bottom of the war, in a place called the Gray Abyss of Shattered Ruins, with everything I'd made.

Including the Nursery.



Chapter Five: The Nursery

Plan A had failed. So I made plan B.

If corruption wouldn't finish the gods, I'd make new ones to finish the job. Gods of my own. Gods who would do what I said. I took the bodies of the dead ones from the war, cooked them down, poured them into molds, and grew them in tanks. Rows and rows of them. Sapient but not sentient. Weapons with faces.

That's what the Nursery is. That's what I built down there in the dark with my cold hands. An army of the dead, remade.

And I made a few special ones. By hand. Experiments. Things that could go into minds. Things that could steal bodies. Things that could break a story open from the inside.

Experiment Nine worked. The others didn't. I put the failures in jars and I put Nine on a table, and when she opened her eyes and looked at me, I felt it. The thing I'd written on the first page. The thing I'd told myself not to do.

She looked at me like I was her mother.

I was. I'd made her.

I trained her for a long time. Then the clean gods found the Nursery, and plan B started to collapse, and I had to run, and I couldn't take her with me. She'd slow me down. She'd ask questions. She'd look at me.

I put my hand on her back at the edge of the pit.

I could have taken her. I'll say that. I could have picked her up and carried her and run slower and maybe been caught and maybe not. I thought about it for exactly as long as it took me to remember the first page of the notebook.

I pushed.

She didn't scream. She just kept looking at me all the way down. I can still feel her looking. That's my only complaint about my own work. I made her mind too well. I made it able to stay in mine.



Chapter Six: The Prophecy

Plan B failed slowly. The Nursery babies were good soldiers but bad gods. They didn't want anything. You can't build a world-ender out of something that doesn't want.

I needed want. Want is the fuel. Want is the thing that gets in under a story and changes it from inside. The gods I hated all wanted things. Bored things, cruel things, but things. My Nursery babies wanted nothing, so they changed nothing.

So I went looking for want. The biggest want there is.

Grief.

Grief is want with nowhere to go. It's the most powerful fuel in any universe. A grieving mind will burn down every star to get back one face. If I could find the right griever, and make the right grief, and point it at the right target, it would do what plan A and plan B couldn't. It would end everything. Every god. Every story. And then, in the silence after, something new could start. Something that dies. Something that matters.

I found a prophet in a small dimension. An old woman who wrote down futures. I sat with her one evening, and I held her hand, and I let a little of the corruption in. Just for a second.

She wrote what I wanted.

When the end begins, corruption will fall, and there shall be one with shining blonde hair, the beloved sister, fueled by grief.

At the bottom, after I let go of her hand, she added a little line on her own. She was shaking. Note from the Master Writer: the Prophecist was corrupted for a second while writing this prophecy.

Honest woman. I liked her. I left her alive. I thought that was generous.



Chapter Seven: The Parents

Now I needed the sister.

I looked for a long time. It had to be the right girl. Someone loved, really loved, by someone who would be willing to do the unthinkable. Someone whose grief would be a bomb.

I found her on a small world in a small house with a small family. A mother. A father. A boy and a girl. The girl had shining blonde hair. The boy followed her around like she hung the moon. They didn't have much money. They had a lot of love. I watched them through their kitchen window for a whole year. They had pancakes on Sundays. The father burned them every time and the children screamed laughing. The mother sang to the girl at night, something about a river.

They were perfect.

Grief needs something to grieve.

I won't tell you the details. The children saw. That was the point. They saw everything. I came through the kitchen window on a Sunday morning with corruption on my hands, and I touched the mother and the father, and I let it all in at once. They turned in front of their children. Red eyes. Backward words. And then they were gone, and they were dust, on the kitchen floor, next to the burned pancakes.

The boy screamed. The girl didn't. She just stood there and looked at me.

Not like Nine. Not like I was her mother. Like I was the end of the world.

"Why?" she said.

I could have told her. I could have explained the bored gods and the folded solar system and the forty thousand years and the math. I think she might even have understood. She was a smart girl.

"Soi Kijo," I said. "Joi Kiieanian Kio."

And I climbed out the window.

They call it, in the stories, "what happened to my parents." After They... The boy never finishes that sentence. He never could.

Chapter Eight: Chimera

Chimera found me once in all of that. Only once.

It was in the Abyss, in the ruins, a long time after. She was older. We all were. She was wearing a body that was mostly scars, and she sat down on a broken highway next to me where I was watching the sister grow up through a little window I'd cut in the air.

"That fucking girl," Chimera said. "You know what you're doing to that fucking girl?"

"Yes."

"You killed her parents in front of her."

"Yes."

"For a prophecy you wrote."

"Yes."

Chimera was quiet a long time. Then she took my hand. Just like in the great hall. The last hand I shook.

"Stop," she said. "Please. Himo. I'm asking you. Not as a god. As the idiot who loved you. Close the window. Let the kid have her life. Let the brother grow up and be a dumbass and get a job and have a cat. It's not too late. It's never too late. You can still just stop."

And I want you to know that I thought about it.

I looked through the window at the sister. She was fourteen. She was on a rooftop with her brother, eating chips, laughing at something. Her hair was shining in the sun. The brother was watching her like she hung the moon.

I thought about stopping. I really did. I let myself imagine it. A girl who grows up. A boy who gets a job. A cat. A long, boring, mortal life, ending in an old bed, ending.

And then I thought about Conquest. And Famine. And the god of space and his napkin.

"Soi Kijo," I said to Chimera.

She let go of my hand.

"Fuck you," she said, and she cried, and she left, and she has hated me every day since. She'll be there at the end. She'll be the one screaming the loudest. I'm glad. Someone should.



Chapter Nine: Five Seconds

It happened the way I wrote it.

The sister glowed. The brother believed. The brother put a knife in her. She came back as Corrupt and ended a world, then another, then the resistance, then everything. The grief went off like a star. Galactix rose. Tombstone rose. The gods I hated died in the hundreds.

It worked. All of it. Plan B. It worked.

And then, at the very end, the sister found me.

She'd learned to travel in time. Five seconds at a time. That's all. Five seconds. She came back from wherever she was, and she found me standing in the Nursery with my cold hands in my coat pockets, watching the tanks, and she went incarnate, and she put a blade in my chest.

It hurt. I'll admit that. I haven't been hurt in a very long time.

And then the five seconds were over, and she snapped back to her own time, and I stood there in the Nursery bleeding, and she, somewhere else, saw the blood on her own hands and screamed.

Not because she'd hurt me. Because she'd liked it.

That's the thing about grief. It's the best fuel. But it burns the engine too.

I didn't die. I'm very hard to kill. I made myself that way, a long time ago, in case something like her ever happened.

I sat down on the floor of the Nursery with my hand pressed to the hole, and I took out the old notebook, the one with the brown pages, and I read the first page.

LOG 0.

Objective: end the gods.
Method: pending.

Under it, I wrote one more line. The handwriting's shaky. I was bleeding.

Note: I didn't.

That's a lie. You know it's a lie. I knew it was a lie when I wrote it. I was attached to all of them. To Nine. To Chimera. To the man who made good tea. To the girl with the shining hair, who I watched grow up for fourteen years through a little window, who I knew better than anyone in any universe ever has.

But being attached never stopped me. Not once. That's the only thing I'm proud of.

I got up. I went to the back of the Nursery, to the room nobody knows about, where there's a single tank, bigger than the others, humming.

Plan B is over.
