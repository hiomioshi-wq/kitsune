Chapter One: The Copyist

Before he was a mage, before he lived at the edge of time, before he had lost his voice, Teodor Ashe was a copyist in a library on Terra, and he loved his job more than anything in the world.

The library was old and cold and very quiet. It sat at the top of a hill above a grey city, and its shelves went up into the dark further than any ladder could reach. Teodor's work was simple. When a book grew too old to read, too brittle and faded and crumbling, he copied it out by hand into a new one, word for word, letter for letter, so that it would not be lost.

He was very good at it. He had a small, careful, steady hand, and the patience of a stone, and he believed with his whole heart that every book deserved to be remembered. Even the boring ones. Especially the boring ones. Nobody else was going to remember those.

He was twenty-three the winter he found the prophecy.

It was not in a book. It was a single sheet, folded into quarters and tucked inside the cover of an atlas of dimensions that no longer existed. The paper was strange. Not paper at all, really. Something like bark, grey and thin and very old, older than anything else in the library. The writing on it was faded almost to nothing, and in two different hands. Most of it was small and neat. One line in the middle was wide and jagged, the letters pressed so hard they had almost gone through.

And at the very top, almost too faint to see, there was a note.

Note From The Master Writer: The Prophecist was Corrupted for a Second While Writing This Prophecy.

And under that, fainter still, three words that Teodor had to hold up to the window to read:

Please don't hurt her.

He sat in the cold library for a long time with the strange grey sheet in his hands.

It was crumbling. He could see that. Another winter, perhaps two, and the bark would fall apart into dust, and the words would be gone forever.

He did not know what it meant. He did not know who the Prophecist was, or who the girl with shining blonde hair might be, or what corruption was. But he knew one thing, the one thing he had always known, since he was a small boy following his grandmother through the stacks.

Every book deserved to be remembered.

So he copied it.

He copied it carefully, word for word, letter for letter, in his small steady hand, onto a fresh clean page. The neat lines. The jagged line in the middle. The note at the top. He worked on it for a whole week, because the faded letters were so hard to read. When he was done he held the copy up beside the original, and they matched exactly, and he was proud.

He put the copy on the shelf in the section for prophecies, where anyone could find it.

That was the first mistake.

He would spend the rest of his very long life trying to fix it.


Chapter Two: People Found It

People found it.

That was what happened to things in libraries. That was the point of libraries. Someone came in looking for a book about something else, and saw the copy on the shelf, and read it, and was frightened, and copied it again. And someone else read that copy, and was frightened, and copied it again. And with every copy, the note at the top got a little smaller, and a little fainter, and the copyists who did not understand it began to leave it out.

Please don't hurt her was the first thing to go. Nobody understood it. It was not part of the prophecy. It was just a note, scribbled at the top. Why copy a note?

Then the line about the Prophecist being corrupted went too. It made the prophecy sound less certain. Nobody wanted an uncertain prophecy. An uncertain prophecy was not worth being frightened of.

Within twenty years, there were hundreds of copies. Within forty, thousands. And not one of them had the note at the top. Just the neat lines, and the jagged line in the middle, in the same plain print.

It will only start when a man plunges a knife into his sister.

Teodor watched it happen. He was an old man by then, still copying, still careful, still climbing the long ladders in the cold library on the hill. He saw the copies come in. He saw them pass from hand to hand. He saw people in the grey city below begin to whisper about it in the street, and begin to look at a particular little girl with shining blonde hair a certain way, as she walked to school holding her older brother's hand.

He went to see her once. Just once. He did not know why. He stood across the street from the girl's house in the grey city, an old man in a copyist's coat, and watched her come out the door with her brother. She was about seven. She was laughing at something he had said. He was about twelve, and he had his hand on her shoulder, and he was looking at the people across the street who were staring at her, and his face was hard and angry and protective.

"I don't believe it," the boy said loudly, to nobody, to everybody. "How can people resort to being this crazy?"

Teodor stood on the corner and felt his heart break.

He had copied it. He had put it on the shelf. He had only wanted it to be remembered. And now this little girl was going to grow up with the whole city looking at her that way, and this boy was going to grow up fighting them, and somewhere at the end of it all, if the prophecy was right, there was a knife.

He went home to the library and took down his own copy, the first one, the careful one, and read it again. And this time he saw the note at the top. The note he had copied so carefully. The note nobody else had bothered with.

Please don't hurt her.

Teodor sat down on the cold library floor, an old man with a sheet of paper in his hands, and wept.


Chapter Three: The Edge of Time

He went looking for the Prophecist.

He did not know how to find someone who had written something thousands of years ago. He did not know anything, really, except how to copy books. But he had spent sixty years in the largest library on Terra, and he had read a great many books that nobody else had read, and some of them were about magic.

He started small. A spell to find a lost thing. It did not work. A spell to read a faded page. It worked a little. A spell to follow the threads of a piece of writing back to the hand that wrote it.

It took him eleven years to make that one work.

By then he was very old indeed, and his hands shook too much to copy anymore, and his voice had gone thin and cracked from disuse, because he spoke to nobody, and nobody came to the library anymore except to find the prophecy. But on a cold morning at the end of his eleventh year of trying, he laid the original sheet of grey bark on the floor of the library, and drew a circle around it in chalk, and spoke the words of the spell.

And the bark began to glow.

A thin grey thread rose up out of it. Out of the small neat lines, not the jagged one. It rose up into the air, and wavered, and pointed. Not north or south or up or down. It pointed in a direction that did not exist. A direction Teodor's eyes could not quite follow, that made his head ache to look at.

Sideways. Out. Away from everything.

He followed it.

He did not know how. He only knew that he stepped into the chalk circle, and took hold of the grey thread, and pulled, and the library around him went thin and faded like an old page, and then it was gone.

He was standing on a hill.

It was not a hill on Terra. It was not a hill anywhere. It stood alone in a grey, empty, silent space that went on forever in every direction, and the sky above it was not a sky, but a sort of pale mist, the color of very old paper. There was nothing on the hill but a single small stone hut with a wooden door, and a garden of grey grass around it.

And the thread in his hand did not point any further. It went straight down into the ground under the hut and stopped.

Teodor stood on the hill for a long time. Then he went to the door of the hut, and knocked.

Nobody answered.

He pushed the door open.

The hut was empty. A bed, a table, a chair, a fireplace with no fire in it. Dust on everything, thick and grey. And on the table, lying open, a book of blank pages, and a pen made from a long dark feather.

Beside the book, written on the table itself in small neat letters, scratched into the wood with a knife, were three words.

I let go.


Chapter Four: The Hermitage

He stayed.

He did not mean to. He meant to rest for a night, and then follow the thread back to Terra, and go home, and die in his library among his books, having failed. But when he woke the next morning on the dusty bed in the stone hut, he found that he was not as old as he had been.

Not young. Not even close. But his hands did not shake quite so badly. His back did not ache quite so much. And when he went outside and looked at his reflection in the still grey water of a pool at the bottom of the hill, his hair was a little less white.

It was the place, he slowly understood. The hill. The hut. It sat outside of time. Not before it, or after it. Just outside. At the very edge, the way a margin sits at the edge of a page. Nothing that happened here was written into the main story of the multiverse. And so nothing here aged. Or ended. Or began.

The Prophecist had lived here. That much he was sure of. The pen was hers. The blank book was hers. The scratched words on the table were hers. But she was gone. Where, he did not know. The thread from the prophecy stopped here, at the hut, and went no further.

I let go.

Teodor sat at her table, with her pen in his hand, and thought about what that meant for a very long time.

And then, one grey morning that was not a morning, he heard a sound at the bottom of the hill.

It was a woman. Young, in torn armor, crawling up the grey slope on her hands and knees, bleeding from a wound in her side. Her eyes were wild and terrified. She was not from Terra. She was not from anywhere Teodor knew. She looked up and saw the hut, and the old man in the doorway, and she said, in a language he somehow understood:

"Where am I? I was falling. I was falling through time, and there was nothing, and then there was this. Where am I?"

Teodor looked at her.

He opened his mouth to answer. He had not spoken to anyone in almost twenty years. His voice had gone thin and cracked and nearly silent, and when he tried to use it now, nothing came out at all. Only air.

So he did the only thing he could think of.

He went down the hill, and helped her up, and brought her into the hut, and sat her on the Prophecist's bed. He cleaned the wound in her side with water from the grey pool. He gave her bread, which he found, to his surprise, in a cupboard that had been empty the night before. He sat with her while she slept.

And when she woke, frightened, not knowing where she was, he took the Prophecist's pen and wrote on the first page of the blank book, in his small steady copyist's hand:

Welcome in. Please make yourself comfortable.

The woman read it, and cried, and slept again. When she woke the second time, she was well enough to stand. She told him she had fallen out of her own time, through a crack opened by a dying anti-god, and that she did not know how to get back. Teodor did not know either. But he found that if she stood in the chalk circle he drew on the floor of the hut, and held the thread of her own story, the way he had held the thread of the prophecy, it would lead her home.

She went. She thanked him. She asked his name. He wrote it down for her. And then she was gone.

That was the first one.

There were a great many more.


Chapter Five: The Ones Who Fall

People fell out of time more often than anyone in the main story of the multiverse ever knew.

Soldiers wounded in battles that tore holes in reality. Travelers who took a wrong step through a portal. Anti-gods dying slowly in incarnate forms, their bodies burning so hot they burned through the walls of time itself. People who had been displaced by residual corruption and could not find their way back. People who had lost everything and simply stopped being anywhere at all.

They fell, and fell, and most of them fell forever, into the grey nothing outside of everything.

But some of them, the lucky ones, landed on the hill.

Teodor never knew why. Perhaps the Prophecist had built something into the place before she left. Perhaps the hill was simply the only solid ground in all that grey, the way an island is the only solid ground in a sea. Perhaps it was only luck. But they came, every few years, every few decades. A frightened face at the bottom of the grey slope. A hand reaching up out of the mist.

And Teodor was always there.

He could not speak. His voice never came back. He had used it so little, for so long, in the cold library, that it had simply worn away, like ink on an old page. But he had his pen, and his blank book, and his small steady hand, and he found that he did not need a voice.

Welcome in, he would write. Please make yourself comfortable.

And then he would feed them, and heal them, and let them sleep, and when they were ready, he would draw a chalk circle on the floor and help them find the thread of their own story and follow it home.

He learned magic, in the long years on the hill. Real magic, not library spells. He learned it from the people who fell: a mage from a burning dimension who taught him fire wards in exchange for soup; a lesser anti-god of desire, hiding from the end of the first war, who showed him how to hide a place so that only the right people could find it; an old Horseman, very tired, with a heart she was eating like an apple, who showed him how to feed a stranger so that they would feel full for a hundred years.

He wrote it all down. Every lesson. Every face. Every story. In the Prophecist's blank book, which never ran out of pages no matter how many he filled.

He became, in the long ages on the hill outside of time, a mage. A real one. Small and quiet and patient as a stone.

He called the place the hermitage, in his head. A place for someone to wait.

But he always knew, deep down, what he was really waiting for.


Chapter Six: The Failed Years

The first time they came, he was not ready.

He knew them at once. He had seen them once before, a very long time ago, on a street corner in a grey city on Terra. A little girl with shining blonde hair, laughing. A boy with his hand on her shoulder, angry at the people staring.

They were older now. So much older. The girl's hair was not shining anymore; it was tangled and streaked with something dark. Her eyes flickered red. She was crying. The boy, a young man now, was in torn silver armor with a big H on the chest, and one of his eyes was a glowing machine, and he was holding his sister up with one arm, and they were both covered in blood and ash.

They had fallen out of time. On a thread of residual corruption. Running from something.

Teodor ran down the hill to them. He could not speak. He did not need to. He helped them up the slope and into the hut and sat them by the fire, and wrote, with shaking hands:

Welcome in. Please make yourself comfortable.

The girl read it and laughed, a cracked, broken laugh.

"We lost everything," she said. "Everything. Galactix. She—we were going to stop her and she—" Her voice broke. "Everyone's dead. The anti-gods. Esme. Rui. Everyone. We came back as far as we could. Is this—where is this? When is this?"

Teodor did not know how to explain the hill. So he wrote the only thing that he understood about it.

This is the year before everything ends.

It was not quite true. The hill was outside of time. But it touched every timeline at the same point: the edge. The margin. The last moment before the main story of the multiverse reached its end. That was where the hill was. Always one year away from the last page.

The girl stared at the words for a long time. Then she looked at her brother.

"It's not enough," she said. "A year. It's not enough to change anything."

Her brother put his metal hand over hers.

"Then we'll try anyway," he said.

They tried. They stayed one night in the hermitage, and Teodor fed them and healed them as best he could, and in the morning they went out, back into the main story of the multiverse, to fight Galactix again with everything they had.

They lost.

He knew they had lost because, a year later, they fell back onto the hill. The same girl. The same young man. Bleeding. Crying. Not remembering that they had ever been there before.

He wrote welcome in again.

They lost again.

And again. And again.

Each time, they fell back onto the hill not remembering. Each time, they went out to fight. Each time, something went wrong. The girl was possessed as a vessel. The young man was torn apart. The anti-gods were killed too soon, or too late. Each time the timeline split, and broke, and became one more shattered fragment in the broken world, and they fell back to the margin, and the old mage wrote welcome in on a fresh page, in the same small steady hand.

He lost count, after a while. A hundred times? A thousand? He filled whole sections of the Prophecist's book with their faces. Every one the same. Every one a little more broken than the last.

He did not know how to help them. He was only a copyist who had learned some magic. He could heal their wounds. He could feed them. He could let them sleep. But he could not change the ending.

He was not the anti-god of endings.

He was only the man who had put the prophecy on the shelf.


Chapter Seven: The One Thing Different

On the thousand-and-somethingth time, something was different.

It was small. Teodor almost did not notice it. They fell onto the hill the way they always did, the girl and her brother, bleeding and broken and running from Galactix. He ran down the hill. He helped them up. He brought them into the hut and sat them by the fire and wrote welcome in on a fresh page.

And the girl looked at the words, and then up at him, and frowned.

"I know your handwriting," she said.

Teodor's pen stopped.

"I know it," said the girl again, slowly. "I've seen it before. Somewhere. A long time ago. On a—" She closed her eyes, concentrating. Her face twisted. "On a piece of paper. In a library. When I was small. Someone had copied something, really carefully, and I remember thinking the handwriting was so neat. So careful. Like whoever wrote it really loved the words." She opened her eyes and stared at him. "That was you. Wasn't it? You copied it. The prophecy. The first copy. The one with the note at the top."

Teodor could not move.

He had never told them. Not in a thousand timelines. He had never once written it down. He had been too ashamed.

"Ena," said her brother, quietly. "What are you talking about?"

"The prophecy," said the girl. Ena. "The one about me. When we were kids. Everyone had copies of it. Remember? Everyone. But there was one in the big library on the hill that was different. It had a note at the top. Grandma took me to see it once, before she died, because she said it was the original copy, the first one, the one the others came from. And the note said—" Her voice caught. "The note said please don't hurt her."

The hut was very quiet.

"Nobody else's copy had that," said Ena. "Just that one. Just yours. You kept it. When everyone else threw it away." She was crying now. "Why? Why did you keep it?"

Teodor looked down at the page in front of him. At the words welcome in, in his small steady hand. At the pen made from a long dark feather.

He could not speak. He had not spoken in thousands of years.

But he wrote.

Because every word deserves to be remembered, he wrote. Even the small ones. Especially the small ones. Nobody else was going to remember those.

And then, underneath, because he had been waiting thousands of years to say it, and because she was here, and because it was true:

I'm so sorry. I put it on the shelf. I'm the reason everyone read it. I'm the reason everyone looked at you that way. I'm the reason he had the knife. I only wanted it remembered. I didn't know. I'm so sorry.

Ena read it. Her brother read it over her shoulder.

And then the young man in the silver armor with the H on the chest did something that, in a thousand timelines, he had never done before.

He reached across the table and took the old mage's hand.

"I had the knife," he said quietly. "Not you. Me. I made that choice. Nobody else. I've spent years blaming everyone else. The prophecy, the city, the corruption, Tombstone. But it was my hand." He looked at his sister. "And she forgave me anyway. Somehow. So I think—" His voice cracked. "I think if she can forgive me, then you can stop carrying this. You kept the note. You kept please don't hurt her. That's the only good thing in the whole prophecy. And you're the only one who kept it."

Teodor stared at him.

Then he put his face in his hands, and his shoulders shook, and the old mage at the edge of time wept the way he had not wept since the cold floor of the library on the hill, ten thousand years ago.


Chapter Eight: The Mage Was Wrong

They did not go out to fight that morning.

For the first time in a thousand timelines, they stayed. They sat in the hermitage, the three of them, by the fire that never went out, and talked. Or rather, Ena and her brother talked, and Teodor wrote, page after page, in the Prophecist's book. He told them about the library. About finding the grey sheet in the atlas. About copying it. About watching the note disappear from every copy but his. About following the thread to the hill, and finding the Prophecist gone, and the words scratched into the table.

I let go.

"What does it mean?" said Ena.

I think she let the ending come on its own, Teodor wrote. Without pulling. Without pushing. I think she waited here too. For you. And then she understood that she could not change it by holding on. So she let go.

"Then maybe we have to let go too," said Ena slowly. "We've been trying to fight Galactix head on. Over and over. Just the two of us. And it never works. We keep trying to fix it ourselves, alone, the way we always do." She looked at her brother. "What if we stopped trying to win? What if we just—held on? To each other? And let everyone else come?"

"Everyone else is dead," said her brother. "In every timeline. Galactix kills them before we can get back."

Teodor shook his head.

He took the Prophecist's book and turned back. Back through hundreds of pages. Thousands. Past a thousand pictures of the same broken brother and sister. Back to the very beginning of the book, to the people who had fallen onto the hill before them.

A mage from a burning dimension. A lesser anti-god of desire, hiding. A fire anti-god and her water sister, pulled out of their own burning by each other. An anti-god of the mind, young and scarred, on her way down into hell to guard it. A space anti-god with a black wound in her neck, laughing her startled laugh even as she bled.

Every one of them had fallen onto the hill, at one time or another, in one timeline or another. Every one of them had been welcomed in. Every one of them had stood in his chalk circle and followed their own thread home.

And every one of them, Teodor realized, staring down at the faces in his book, every one of them still had a thread leading back here. To the hermitage. To the margin. To the edge of the last page.

He looked up at Ena and her brother. Then he wrote, very slowly, in his small steady hand:

They're not dead. Not here. Here, nothing ends. I've kept all of them. Every face. Every story. In this book. If you hold on to each other, and I pull on every thread at once—

He stopped. His hand was shaking.

—I think they'll come.

Ena stared at him.

"All of them?"

All of them, wrote Teodor. Every anti-god who ever fell through here. I can't change your ending. I'm not the Prophecist. But I can copy. That's all I've ever been able to do. I can copy them out of my book and back into your story. If you want.

Her brother looked at his sister. His sister looked at him.

And then, for the first time in a thousand timelines, they smiled at each other.

"Yeah," said Ena. "Yeah. Do it."


Chapter Nine: Welcome In

Galactix came to the hermitage that afternoon.

She had always come, eventually. In every timeline. She could feel residual corruption the way a shark feels blood in water, and when the brother and sister hid at the margin too long, she came looking. Usually Teodor hid them. Usually he used the trick the lesser anti-god of desire had taught him, and the master passed by the hill without seeing it.

This time, he did not hide them.

This time, when the master came striding up the grey slope, vast and shining and terrible, Ena and her brother were standing in the doorway of the hut waiting for her. The girl's eyes were glowing pink. Her brother's were glowing blue. They were holding hands.

"You're out of places to run," said Galactix.

"We're not running," said Ena.

Galactix laughed, and raised one enormous hand, and shot a beam of light at the girl in the doorway.

Her brother stepped in front of it. He did not try to destroy it. He did not try to win. He only held it, the way you hold a door shut in a storm. His wings ripped out of his back in transparent blue and gold, and he braced himself in front of his sister and held the beam, and did not let it through.

And behind them, inside the hut, at the table, with the Prophecist's book open in front of him and the long dark feather in his hand, Teodor Ashe began to copy.

He copied as fast as he had ever copied in his life. Faster. His small steady hand flew across the page. Every face in the book. Every story. Every thread that led back to the hermitage. He copied them out of the margin and into the main story of the multiverse, one after another, word for word, letter for letter, and as he did, the chalk circle on the floor of the hut began to glow.

And then, from the circle, they came.

A fire anti-god, her hands blazing. Her water sister beside her, already calling a storm. A shapeshifter, already halfway to dragon, with a one-eyed anti-god of the mind on her back. A space anti-god, laughing, her hands folding the air. The anti-god of the mind, young and scarred, eyes glowing gold. Every anti-god who had ever fallen through the hermitage, in any timeline, coming back out of the book and into the light.

Seven powers. Then more. Pouring out of the doorway of the hut and down the grey hill, past the brother holding the line, past the sister glowing pink, straight at the master who had killed them all a thousand times.

Galactix's face changed.

"No," she said. "No, you're dead. All of you. I killed you. I killed every one of you."

"Not here," said Ena, in the doorway, with her brother holding the line in front of her and her friends pouring past her down the hill. "Here, nothing ends. Here, someone kept us."

The old mage in the hut looked up from his book for just a moment, at the battle on the hill. At the fire and water and space and mind and shape all striking the master at once. At the brother and sister in the doorway, holding hands.

He could not speak. He had not spoken in ten thousand years.

But he found, to his surprise, that he was smiling. And that his lips were moving. And that a very small, cracked, worn-out sound was coming out of them, so faint that nobody on the battlefield could possibly have heard it.

"I guess I was wrong," said Teodor Ashe.

And went back to copying.


Chapter Ten: The Last Page

The rest of the story is told in other places. How Galactix fell. How the broken world was mended. How Modify used the residual corruption to sew the shattered realities back together, with the mage beside him, weaving his small steady magic into every stitch. How they won. How it cost them everything anyway, in the end. How there is a tower now, at the center of the restored world, with one name at the bottom and a second line beneath it, carved by someone in the night.

But there is one thing the other stories do not tell.

After the battle, when the anti-gods had all followed their threads home and the brother and sister had gone back down into the main story of the multiverse to live out the rest of their lives, the old mage sat alone at the table in the hermitage with the Prophecist's book open in front of him.

He turned to the very last page that had writing on it. The page where he had copied the anti-gods out of the margin and back into the world.

And underneath, he wrote one more line.

Not welcome in. Not please make yourself comfortable.

He wrote, in his small steady hand, the same three words he had found scratched into the wood of this table ten thousand years ago, when he first came up the hill.

I let go.

And then he closed the book.

He did not follow a thread home. He had no home anymore. The library on the hill on Terra was long gone, crumbled to dust with all its books, the way everything in the main story of the multiverse eventually crumbled. He had outlived it all.

But that was all right. He had copied what mattered.

He went outside and sat on the grey grass in front of the hut and looked out at the pale mist that was not a sky, and the grey nothing that went on forever in every direction. And for the first time in ten thousand years, he did not wait for anyone to fall.

Somewhere far away, at the foot of a white tower in the restored world, a silver-haired woman was holding an old prophecy in her hands, reading the faint words at the top, almost rubbed away.

Please don't hurt her.

"Who wrote this?" she said out loud, to nobody. "Who wrote this, and why did they say please?"

And on a hill at the edge of time, an old man in a copyist's coat smiled, and closed his eyes, and did not answer.

He knew who had written it. He had only ever copied it. And he had copied it carefully, word for word, letter for letter, so that it would be remembered.

That had always been enough.
