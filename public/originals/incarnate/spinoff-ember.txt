Chapter One: The Monument Has One Name

They built the monument after the war.

It stood at the center of the restored world, where the broken world had finally been sealed shut, a tower of white stone so tall that clouds snagged on it. At the top burned a flame that never went out. At the bottom, cut into the stone in letters taller than a person, was a single word.

MODIFY.

Rui came to look at it on the first anniversary of the end of everything. She stood in the crowd with her hood up, because people still stared at the scar. It ran from her left temple down her jaw and across her collarbone, a raised shining seam of melted skin in the shape of a hand. Galactix's hand. The master had touched her for less than a second, at the very end, and it had been enough.

The crowd cheered. A choir sang. Children threw flowers at the base of the tower, and the flowers caught in the cracks of the letters and hung there, red and gold.

Rui read the monument three times, walking all the way around it, looking for the other names.

There weren't any.

Not Corrupt, who had flown into Galactix with wings of fire. Not Famine, who had starved a master of her power until her own veins went black. Not Cosmi, who had folded space like paper. Not Formality, or Positron, or Esme, who had died for it, or the mage, who had held reality together with his bare hands while it tried to fly apart.

And not Rui.

Not Rui, the anti-god of fire, who had stood in the storm beside her sister and poured out flame until there was nothing left in her but smoke. Who had been burned by a master and kept fighting. Who had held the line on the western edge of the battlefield for nine minutes, alone, while the others regrouped, nine minutes nobody had seen because everybody had been watching Modify at the center, glowing blue, mending the world.

She did not hate him for it. That was the worst part. He had earned the tower. He had earned all of it. She had watched him do it.

She only wanted one small line of stone, somewhere near the bottom, where the flowers caught. One line that said she had been there.

"You're Rui, aren't you?" said a voice at her elbow.

It was a little girl, maybe eight, holding a bunch of marigolds and staring up at Rui's scar with frank fascination.

"Yeah," said Rui. "That's me."

"My mom said you were in the war."

"I was."

"What did you do?"

Rui opened her mouth. Then she looked up at the tower, at the one name, at the flame that never went out, and she closed it again.

"Fire," she said at last. "I did fire."

"Oh," said the girl, losing interest. "Like the thing on top?"

"Yeah," said Rui. "Like the thing on top."

The girl ran off to throw her marigolds at the word MODIFY, and Rui stood alone in the cheering crowd, and felt, for the first time in a year, the old, cold, familiar feeling creep up the back of her neck. The feeling she had told Tsui about once, sitting on the edge of a broken dimension with the multiverse falling apart around them.

I'm just worried about being forgotten. That my sister will forget me. That I'm worthless.

She had not said it out loud since. She had thought, after the war, after they won, that it would go away.

It hadn't. It had only gotten quieter, the way a fire gets quieter when it goes down into the coals.


Chapter Two: The Sister Who Doesn't Remember

Tsui lived by the sea.

Of course she did. She was the anti-god of water. After the war she had found a white house on a cliff at the edge of the restored world, where the waves came in grey and green and broke on black rocks, and she had stayed there ever since. Rui visited every week. Every week she walked up the cliff path with a bag of oranges, because Tsui had always loved oranges, and every week Tsui opened the door and looked at her, pleasantly, politely, with no recognition at all.

"Hello," said Tsui. "Can I help you?"

"It's me," said Rui. "Rui. Your sister."

And every week something flickered in Tsui's eyes. Blue, for half a second, the way they used to glow when she called the tides. Then gone.

"Of course," Tsui would say. "Of course. Come in. I'm sorry. I'm terrible with faces."

It was the residual corruption. That was what the healers said. When Tsui had been aligned with Galactix, when the master had wrapped her in a vessel's chains and used her water against her own side, something had been torn out of her. Not her power. Not her name. Just the places in her where her sister had been.

She remembered the war. She remembered Modify and Corrupt and the battle, and the storm of fire and water that had finally broken Galactix's defenses. But when she remembered the fire in that storm, she remembered it coming from nowhere. As if it had simply been there, part of the weather.

She did not remember who had been holding it.

"I brought oranges," said Rui, this week, holding up the bag.

"Oh, how kind," said Tsui. "I love oranges. How did you know?"

Rui sat in the white kitchen and peeled an orange and listened to her sister talk about the tide, and the weather, and a seal she had seen on the rocks that morning, and every few minutes Tsui stopped, mid-sentence, and looked at her with that pleasant, polite, empty face, and said, "I'm sorry, what did you say your name was?"

"Rui."

"Rui. That's pretty. Like fire."

"Yeah," said Rui, and pressed her thumbnail into the orange peel so hard it split. "Like fire."

When she left, Tsui walked her to the door. On the step, she paused, and frowned, and put one cool hand on Rui's scarred cheek.

"That looks like it hurt," she said.

"It did."

"How did you get it?"

Rui looked at her sister for a long moment.

"Standing next to you," she said.

Tsui's eyes flickered blue. Just for a second. Just for half a breath, something moved behind them, like a fish under ice.

Then it was gone.

"Well," said Tsui kindly, "I hope it heals."

Rui walked down the cliff path in the dark with the empty orange bag in her fist, and when she reached the bottom she sat down on the black rocks at the edge of the sea and set her hands on fire, both of them, all the way to the wrists, and watched them burn until the tide came in and put them out.


Chapter Three: Famine Is Freezing

The news came from the nexus.

Everyone in the restored world knew about the nexus point, the way people know about the foundations of their house without ever going down into the basement. Every dimension had one: a single person, or place, or thing, that held it together among the millions of others in the multiverse. For their old world, it had been Tombstone. When Modify killed him, the world had begun to fall apart. And so, after the war, Famine had taken his place.

She had volunteered. The Horseman who had never wanted the apocalypse, who had tried to cleanse hell instead of ending the world, had walked into the center of the restored world and stood there and let the nexus take her. She became its anchor. She stopped aging. She stopped moving. She stood in a hollow of black glass at the bottom of a canyon, eyes closed, arms crossed over her chest, and the whole world hung from her like a coat from a hook.

And now, a messenger said, standing breathless in the door of Rui's little apartment, she was freezing.

"Freezing how?" said Rui.

"Frost," said the messenger. "On her skin. On the glass around her. It started a week ago. It's spreading. The healers say she's been the nexus too long. She's running out of whatever keeps her going." He swallowed. "If she stops, the world stops. Like before. The healers want every anti-god who's left. They're asking for you."

Rui stared at him.

"They're asking for me," she said. "By name?"

"By—well." The messenger looked at the scar. "They said the fire one."

Of course they did.

Rui went anyway.

The canyon was cold. That was the first wrong thing. It had always been hot, before, a deep dry hollow in red rock that soaked up the sun all day and breathed it out all night. Now there was frost on the rocks. Frost on the stunted trees. Frost on the ground in long white fingers, all pointing inward, toward the black glass hollow at the canyon's heart.

Famine stood in the center of it, exactly as she had stood for a year. But her skin had gone pale blue. Ice furred her eyelashes. Her crossed arms were glazed with a thin, shining crust, and around her feet the black glass had gone milky and cracked, like a frozen lake.

There were others there. The healers in their grey robes. Cosmi, thinner than Rui remembered, leaning on a staff. A tall woman with silver hair that Rui did not recognize at first, until the woman turned, and her eyes shimmered red, and Rui saw the faint pale lines on her arms where wings had once burned.

Corrupt.

Ena.

"Rui," said Ena. She did not smile; Ena did not smile much, anymore. But her face changed, a little, the way a face changes when it sees someone it is glad to see. "You came."

"They asked for the fire one," said Rui.

Something passed over Ena's face. She glanced, very briefly, toward the far end of the canyon, where on a clear day you could see the white tip of the monument rising over the hills.

"I know," she said quietly. "I'm sorry. I asked them to put the others on it. All of us. They said there wasn't room."

"There's room," said Rui. "It's the tallest thing in the world."

"I know," said Ena again. And then, more quietly still, so the healers couldn't hear: "He would have hated it. You know that, right? Mods. If he'd lived to see it. He'd have hated being the only one."

Rui didn't trust herself to answer. She turned and looked at Famine instead, at the frost spreading slowly over her shoulders, like a shawl.

"What's wrong with her?"

"She's cold," said Ena. "That's all. The nexus runs on her. Her hunger, her life, everything she has. A Horseman's not meant to stand still for a year. Famine's whole power is about consuming. Taking. Starving things. She's been standing here giving, giving, giving, holding up the world, and there's nothing left in her to give." Ena reached out, and touched the frozen glass at Famine's feet, and snatched her hand back. "She's starving, Rui. The Horseman of Famine is starving to death. And she's taking the world with her."


Chapter Four: What Fire Is For

The healers tried everything.

They tried blankets and hot stones and healing songs. They tried Cosmi's power, folding a pocket of summer air out of some other dimension and wrapping it around Famine like a cloak; the frost crept through it in an hour. They tried Ena's residual corruption, the strange red energy that still lived in her, that could displace her across realities, but it slid off Famine's skin like oil off water. Famine was the nexus. Nothing from outside could get in.

Rui waited until they had all tried, and all failed, and all gone to sit in the healers' tent at the canyon's mouth with their heads in their hands.

Then she walked down into the black glass hollow alone.

The cold hit her like a wall. It wasn't ordinary cold. It was the cold of the space between dimensions, the cold of the Gray Abyss, the cold that comes when something has been used up and there is nothing left behind it. Her breath turned white. The scar on her face went tight and aching.

She stopped in front of the frozen Horseman, close enough to touch.

"Hey," said Rui. "Famine. It's me. Rui. You probably don't remember me either. Nobody does. Doesn't matter." She swallowed. "You're cold. I'm going to try something. If it doesn't work, well. Nobody'll be surprised."

She put her hands on Famine's frozen arms, and called up her fire.

It came slowly. It always came slowly now, since Galactix. As if some part of her had been burned too, deeper than the scar, and was still afraid of the heat. But it came. Warmth flowed out of her palms and into the ice on Famine's skin, and for a moment—one moment—the frost under her hands went soft and wet.

Then the cold pulled.

It pulled the fire out of her. Not the way a fire is put out. The way a fire is eaten. Rui felt it go out of her hands and up her arms and out of her chest, all of it, all at once, the heat of her whole body draining down through her palms into the frozen Horseman, and she understood, too late, what Ena had meant.

Famine's whole power is about consuming.

The Horseman's eyes opened.

They were black. Black all the way across, black and hollow and endless, a hunger with nothing at the bottom of it. They looked at Rui, and Rui looked into them, and felt herself start to fall.

"Hungry," whispered Famine, though her lips did not move. "So hungry. So cold. Give me—"

Rui tried to pull her hands away. They would not come.

"Give me more," whispered Famine.

And then, deep in those black eyes, something changed. A flicker. The faintest flicker of recognition, like the flicker Rui saw every week in Tsui's eyes. And Famine's voice, still without her lips moving, said something different.

"You. You're the fire. From the western edge. The nine minutes."

Rui stopped pulling.

"You saw that?"

"I was starving Galactix," said Famine. "I was inside her hunger. I could feel everything she was trying to eat. And for nine minutes, on the western edge, something was burning so hot she couldn't swallow it. Couldn't get close. Couldn't get past. She hated it. I remember how much she hated it." The black eyes held hers. "I always wondered who it was."

The cold was still pulling. Rui's knees were shaking. She could feel her heartbeat slowing, the fire in her chest going down to coals.

"It was me," she whispered.

"Then why," said Famine, "are you giving it to me like it's worth nothing?"


Chapter Five: The Gray Abyss, Again

Ena pulled her out.

Rui did not remember it. She remembered falling, and the black eyes, and the cold, and then nothing, and then she was lying on a cot in the healers' tent with three blankets on her and Ena sitting beside her with red light still flickering around her fingertips.

"Displacement," said Ena, before Rui could ask. "I displaced you about a foot to the left. Out of her grip. It's the only thing residual corruption's actually good for." She looked tired. "You almost died."

"She saw me," said Rui. Her voice came out rough. "Famine. In the war. She saw what I did on the western edge. She remembered."

Ena was quiet for a long time.

"I saw it too," she said at last.

Rui turned her head.

"I was in the air," said Ena. "Over the battlefield. I had wings, then. I could see everything. I saw Galactix trying to break through on the west. And I saw something burning there, holding her back. I thought it was you. I knew it was you. And I didn't say anything after. Not once." Her hands tightened in her lap. "Because by then they were building the tower. And everyone was saying his name. And I thought, if I say anything, if I say, wait, it wasn't just him, it was all of us, they'll think I'm trying to put my name up there. Mine. The one who caused it all. The one who killed half the world." Her voice broke. "So I said nothing. And I let them forget you. I'm sorry."

Rui stared at the canvas ceiling of the tent.

"Famine said I was giving it away like it was worth nothing," she said.

"Were you?"

"I don't know." Rui closed her eyes. "Maybe. I don't know what it's worth. Nobody remembers. Tsui doesn't remember. The monument doesn't. How am I supposed to know what it's worth if nobody remembers?"

Ena didn't answer that. Instead she said, "The healers have a theory. About Famine. It's not good."

"Tell me."

"She doesn't need heat. She needs food. Her power is hunger, so what keeps her alive is eating. And there's nothing at the bottom of that canyon to eat. Nothing but the nexus, and she's holding it up, not eating it." Ena rubbed her eyes. "There's one place in all the dimensions where there's enough to feed a Horseman. Enough raw energy, enough leftover power, enough of everything, lying around in heaps. Nobody uses it because nobody can survive there."

Rui knew before she said it.

"The Gray Abyss," said Ena. "The ruins from the first war. Where corruption was born. Where everything that got killed in the anti-god wars ended up. It's full of it, Rui. Dead power. Thousands of anti-gods' worth. If someone could go in, and bring some out—"

"Then why haven't you?"

"Because I'm corruption," said Ena quietly. "The Abyss is where it was made. If I go back in, I don't think I come out as myself. The last time I was there—" She stopped. "The last time I was there, it showed me statues. Of everything I ever did. And Mods—" She stopped again, and this time she did not start.

Rui sat up. Her chest ached. Her hands were cold, colder than she had ever felt them.

"Then I'll go," she said.

"Rui—"

"I'll go. You can displace me in. I'll get what Famine needs. You can displace me out." She swung her legs off the cot. "Nobody'll miss me if it goes wrong."

Ena grabbed her wrist. Hard.

"Stop saying that," she said. "Stop it. I miss Mods every single day, and they built him a tower, and it doesn't help even a little. Do you understand? A name on a wall is nothing. It's stone. People forget who they were walking past while they're still looking at it. You think if they'd carved your name, you'd feel like you mattered?" Her eyes were wet and red and furious. "You'd still feel exactly like this. Because it was never about the wall."

Rui looked down at Ena's hand on her wrist. At the faint pale burn lines on Ena's arm, where her wings used to be.

"Then what's it about?" she said.

Ena let go.

"I don't know," she said. "I've been alive a very long time, and I still don't know. Maybe you'll find out down there." She stood up. "I'll take you to the edge. I can't go in. But I'll hold the door open as long as I can."


Chapter Six: Statues

The Gray Abyss was exactly as Rui remembered it from the stories, and nothing like it at all.

It was grey. Everything was grey. A grey sky with no sun, a grey plain stretching away forever under it, grey rubble in grey heaps, the wreckage of ten thousand years of war between beings who could unmake worlds with a thought. Broken towers jutted from the ground like snapped bones. Shattered weapons lay half-buried in the dust, still faintly humming with power. The air tasted like ash and old metal.

And everywhere, standing in the grey, were the statues.

They were exactly what Ena had said. Statues of every death that had sent something here. They stood in clusters and lines and lonely ones out on the plain: grey stone figures frozen in the act of killing, or dying, or both. An anti-god with a spear through another's chest. A dragon with a man in its jaws. A woman with her hands around a child's—

Rui stopped looking at that one.

"Everything that's killed gets sent here," she whispered to herself, remembering Eri's voice from Ena's stories. "Everything."

She walked. She did not know what she was looking for, only that it would be bright, and big, and full of power that Famine could eat. Her fire was low, so low she could barely light the tip of one finger, and she held that small flame up in front of her like a lantern and walked through the grey, past statue after statue after statue.

And then she found one she knew.

It stood on a rise of grey rubble, alone. A young woman with fire pouring from both hands, legs braced, head thrown back, burning. Burning so hot that the stone itself seemed to ripple with heat. And in front of her, reaching through the fire with one enormous hand, a shape with no face: a vast, blank, shining shape that Rui knew in her bones.

Galactix.

It was the western edge. It was the nine minutes. Some part of the Abyss had seen it, and made it in stone, and set it here among the dead.

"No," Rui said. "No. I didn't die. Nobody died there. Why is this—"

"Because something did die," said a voice behind her.

Rui spun around.

There was a figure standing on the rubble. Small and thin, wrapped in a ragged cloak the color of ash. Its face was hidden in its hood. But its voice was familiar in a way that made the scar on Rui's face throb.

"What died?" said Rui. "Tell me. What died there?"

The figure reached up and pulled back its hood.

It was Rui.

Not quite. Younger. Unscarred. Her face whole and smooth, her eyes bright orange, the way Rui's eyes used to glow before the war, when her fire came fast and hot and easy and never, ever hesitated.

"I did," said the other Rui. "The one who wasn't afraid of her own heat. You left me here when Galactix touched you. You've been walking around without me for a year." She smiled. It was a crooked smile, a little mean, the way Rui used to smile at Tsui when they were children and she had just won a race. "Did you even notice?"


Chapter Seven: The Part of You That Burned

"That's not possible," said Rui.

"You're the anti-god of fire standing in a graveyard for gods, having this conversation with yourself," said the other Rui. "I think possible left the building a while ago." She hopped down off the rubble and walked around Rui in a slow circle, looking her up and down. "Look at you. Look at the scar. Look at your hands. You can barely light a candle. You used to be able to set a river on fire."

"I got burned," said Rui. "A master burned me."

"No. A master touched you. You burned yourself." The other Rui stopped in front of her. "That's what happened on the western edge. You know it. You were holding her back, and it was too much, and you knew it was too much, and you didn't stop. You poured out more than you had. You poured out me. Every bit of the fire that made you you. That's why Famine couldn't swallow it. That's why it was so hot. It wasn't power. It was you, burning down to nothing, on purpose, so the others could get away." The other Rui's eyes flickered. "And when it was over, there wasn't enough of you left to come back. Just the part that was scared. That's who you've been all year. The part that was scared."

Rui couldn't speak.

"And then," the other Rui went on, more quietly, "you went home, and your sister didn't know you, and they built a tower with one name on it, and nobody said thank you. And the scared part of you thought: that's proof. That's proof it didn't matter. Nobody remembers it, so it must not have counted." She shook her head. "But it counted. It's right there." She pointed at the statue. "The Abyss doesn't make statues of things that don't count. It made one of you. Because you killed something that day. You killed the part of yourself that would have run."

The grey wind moved over the plain. Somewhere out among the statues, very faintly, Rui heard Ena's voice, calling her name across the edge of the door.

"I need power," Rui said. "For Famine. She's dying. The world's dying. I came to find something she can eat."

The other Rui laughed.

"Oh, Rui," she said. "You already know what she can eat. You almost let her eat it in the canyon. You just gave it to her wrong."

"What do you mean?"

"Famine's power is consuming. She eats what's given to her. She's been eating nothing for a year, because there's nothing in the nexus but the weight of the world. And then you came down and put your hands on her and gave her your fire, all of it, the way you always do, like it's worth nothing. Like it's garbage you're throwing away. And she tried to eat it. And it wasn't enough. Because garbage isn't food." The other Rui stepped closer. "You have to give it to her like it's worth something. Like it's the most valuable thing you have. Like it's a meal you cooked for someone you love."

"How do I do that?"

The other Rui held out her hand. Her palm was full of fire. Bright, hot, orange fire, the old fire, the fire that had never been afraid.

"Take me back," she said. "And then remember."


Chapter Eight: What Tsui Remembered

Ena was waiting at the edge of the door when Rui came out.

She had been holding it open for nine hours. Red light poured off her in waves; her arms were shaking; her nose was bleeding, a thin dark line down her lip. When she saw Rui stumble out of the grey, she let the door slam shut behind her and caught her as she fell.

"Did you find it?" Ena gasped. "Did you find what she can eat?"

"Yeah," said Rui. Her eyes were orange. Both of them. Glowing. "I found it."

They displaced back to the canyon. The frost had spread while they were gone. It covered the whole floor of the hollow now, and climbed the red cliffs in long white sheets, and the healers had abandoned their tent at the canyon's mouth and retreated to the rim, where they stood huddled together, watching the cold creep upward toward them.

And among them, wrapped in a blue shawl, looking down into the canyon with an expression of polite and anxious confusion, was Tsui.

"I sent for her," said Ena quietly, as they came up onto the rim. "Water and fire. I thought, if the two of you together broke Galactix's defenses—"

"She doesn't remember," said Rui.

"I know."

Tsui turned. She saw Rui coming toward her through the crowd, and her face went pleasant and polite and blank.

"Hello," she said. "Can I help you? Oh. You're the one with the oranges."

Rui stopped in front of her.

"Yeah," she said. "I'm the one with the oranges. And I'm going down there to save the world, and I might not come back. So I need to tell you something, and I need you to listen, even though you don't know who I am."

Tsui blinked.

"When we were kids," said Rui, "we lived in a house with a red door. You were three years older than me, and you were so much better at everything. Better at swimming, better at reading, better at talking to grown-ups. You could call the rain when you were five. I couldn't even light a match till I was nine. And everybody always said, Tsui's the special one, Tsui's the powerful one, and I hated you. I hated you so much. I used to wait till you were asleep and set the end of your braid on fire, just a little, just enough to smell it in the morning."

Tsui's eyes flickered blue.

"And you knew," said Rui. "You always knew it was me. And you never told. Not once. You'd come down to breakfast with your hair smelling like smoke, and Mom would say, what happened, and you'd say, I think I slept too close to the candle. And then you'd look at me across the table and just—smile. Like it was our secret. Like you thought it was funny that your little sister was so mad at you she set your hair on fire. And then one day, when I was ten, I finally made a real flame. A big one. In the backyard. And I was so scared I started screaming, and you ran out of the house and put it out with a wave, and then you sat down next to me in the burned grass and said, that was amazing. You said, Rui, that was the most amazing thing I ever saw. You're going to be so strong." Rui's voice cracked. "And I believed you. You were the first person who ever told me I mattered. And I believed you. And then Galactix took it out of your head."

Tsui was staring at her. Her eyes were blue. Blue and blue and blue, not flickering now, but steady, glowing, deep as the sea.

"You don't have to remember," said Rui. "That's okay. You don't have to. I remember. That's enough. I just needed you to know, before I go down there, that the most important thing I ever did wasn't the war. It was believing you, when you told me I mattered. And I'm going to go down there now and try to believe it again."

She turned to go.

A cool hand caught her wrist.

"Your braid," said Tsui slowly.

Rui stopped.

"You don't have a braid," said Tsui. "You had one. You cut it off. After the first time you burned mine by accident, really by accident, too much, when you were eleven and I had to cut mine short. You cut yours off too. So we'd match." Her voice was shaking. Her eyes were spilling over, tears running down her cheeks like rain. "You came down to breakfast with your hair chopped off with kitchen scissors and Mom screamed. And you looked at me across the table. And you smiled."

"Tsui," whispered Rui.

"I remember," said Tsui. "Oh, Rui. Rui. Rui. I remember."


Chapter Nine: A Meal Made With Love

They went down together.

The cold had grown so deep in the hollow that the air itself seemed to crack. The frost on the black glass was inches thick. Famine stood at the heart of it like a statue carved from ice, her skin gone white, her eyes closed again, and from her, in every direction, the cold flowed out like a tide.

Rui and Tsui stopped in front of her.

"How do we do it?" said Tsui. Her eyes were still glowing blue. She had not stopped holding Rui's hand since the rim. "What do we give her?"

"Everything," said Rui. "But not like it's nothing. Like it's everything."

She took a breath. She reached inside herself, past the scared part, past the part that had walked around for a year waiting to be forgotten, down to where the old fire lived, the fire she had carried back out of the Gray Abyss in the palm of her own unscarred hand.

And she remembered.

She remembered the backyard, and the burned grass, and her sister saying that was amazing. She remembered the western edge, and the nine minutes, and the vast blank shape of Galactix recoiling from her heat. She remembered Ena saying stop saying that. She remembered the little girl with the marigolds, and the way she'd asked what did you do, and the way Rui hadn't answered, and she thought: I'll answer now. I'll answer with this.

She put her hands on Famine's frozen arms. And beside her, Tsui put hers there too.

The fire came.

It came the way it used to, fast and bright and hot and unafraid, pouring out of Rui's palms in a wave of orange light. And beside it, braided through it, came Tsui's water. Not to put it out. To carry it. Warm water, steaming, rushing, tidal, curling around the fire the way the sea curls around a sunset, so that what flowed into Famine was not heat and not cold but something in between, something like the first spoonful of soup on a winter night, something like a meal made in a kitchen by someone who wanted you to have it.

Famine's eyes opened.

They were black. Endless. Hungry. But this time, when Rui looked into them, she did not fall.

"Oh," said the Horseman of Famine. Her lips moved this time. Her voice came out cracked and stiff with cold. "Oh. That's—"

"Eat," said Rui. "It's for you. I made it for you. It's the best thing I've got."

And Famine ate.

The frost broke. It broke all at once, with a sound like a frozen lake cracking in spring, every sheet and finger and crust of it shattering at the same instant and falling away into steam. The black glass of the hollow went clear and hot. The red cliffs of the canyon shrugged off their ice and stood bare and glowing in the last light of the day. And Famine, still standing in the center of the nexus, still holding up the world, uncrossed her arms and opened her hands and laughed, a deep, rough, hungry laugh, the laugh of someone who has just eaten for the first time in a year.

High up on the rim of the canyon, the healers were cheering. Ena was standing apart from them, at the very edge, with her silver hair blowing in the warm wind, and her face wet, and her hand pressed over her mouth.

"That," said Famine, looking down at the two sisters with her black eyes shining, "was the best meal I have ever had. And I've eaten a master." She tilted her head. "What was it?"

Rui thought about it.

"It was me," she said. "And my sister. Remembering each other."


Chapter Ten: The Second Line

Famine did not freeze again.

The healers said she would not need to be fed for a hundred years, maybe more. But Rui went down to the canyon every month anyway, and sat with her, and lit a small fire at her feet, and talked. Sometimes Tsui came too, and they told Famine stories about the house with the red door, and the braid, and the burned grass. Famine said she did not need the fire. She said she liked the stories better. She said they were more filling.

The monument at the center of the restored world still has one name cut into the bottom of it in letters taller than a person.

But a year after the canyon thawed, on the second anniversary of the end of everything, somebody went to the monument in the night with a chisel. Nobody ever found out who. Maybe it was Ena, who had been alive a very long time and knew how to move through a sleeping city without being seen. Maybe it was the little girl with the marigolds, grown up a little, with a chisel stolen from her father's workshop. Maybe it was neither.

But in the morning, when the crowds came, there was a second line of stone at the bottom of the tower. Small. Uneven. Cut by a hand that was not very good with a chisel.

It did not say RUI.

It said:

AND EVERYONE WHO STOOD BESIDE HIM.

Rui came to look at it that afternoon, with her hood down, and Tsui beside her, and an orange in each of her coat pockets.

She did not need her name there anymore. She understood that now. Ena had been right; it was never about the wall. People forget who they were walking past while they're still looking at it. Names wear off stone. The flame on top of the tower would go out one day, a thousand years from now, when the restored world was just as old and forgotten as the ruins of the Gray Abyss.

But somewhere in a white house on a cliff by the sea, there was a woman who remembered a braid. And somewhere at the bottom of a red canyon, there was a Horseman who remembered nine minutes on the western edge, and a meal. And somewhere in Rui herself, burning bright and orange and unafraid, there was a girl who had made her first real flame in a backyard and been told it was the most amazing thing anyone had ever seen.

That was enough.

That was more than enough.

"Do you want to go home?" said Tsui, taking her hand.

"Yeah," said Rui. "Let's go home."

And they walked away from the monument together, the anti-god of fire and the anti-god of water, two sisters who had forgotten each other and remembered again. Behind them the crowd threw flowers at the tower, red and gold, and some of them caught in the cracks of the new line of letters, the small uneven ones at the very bottom.

They hung there, bright in the sun, where anyone who looked down could see them.
