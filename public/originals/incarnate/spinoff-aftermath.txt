INCARNATE: AFTERMATH
(Early Act One Loose Ends — Uncut)

1.
The Seam didn't close cleanly. Everyone in the upper wards told themselves it did because the sirens stopped at four in the morning and the streetlamps came back on, yellow and buzzing, like nothing had been torn open above the reservoir.

Down by the spillway, the concrete was still hot enough to blister boots.

Mira stood at the edge of the retaining wall with a stolen service pistol tucked into the waistband of her coat and watched two Compliance sweepers drag a tarp over what was left of the night watchman. Not all of him fit under the canvas. One boot stayed sticking out into the runoff, filling slowly with black water.

"You're not supposed to be down here," the taller sweeper said. His visor was cracked down the left side. Through the split, his eye was bloodshot and fixed on her throat.

"Neither are you," Mira said. "The manifest said three bodies. That's two."

"Third one walked."

"People don't walk after Corruption-027 takes their spine."

"This one did," the sweeper muttered, spitting into the channel. "Left half his ribs on the grate and kept going toward the old transit tunnel. If you're looking for closure, kid, go home and lock your door. Nobody's paying us to chase meat."

2.
She didn't go home. She went down the maintenance stairs into the transit tunnel, where the air smelled like copper, burnt hair, and wet wool.

Early Act One had left too many threads hanging—names crossed out of hospital ledgers, a half-burned satchel from Kaina's first firing line, and three witnesses who had sworn they saw the blonde sister before the sky split. Two of them were dead by Tuesday. The third was sitting against a rusted junction box fifty yards past the floodgate, pressing a wadded-up jacket against his stomach.

It was Garrett—the dispatcher who had signed off on the false quarantine order in Chapter Two, the one everyone assumed had burned in the barracks.

"You look like shit," Mira said, stopping six feet away. She kept her hand on the grip of the pistol.

Garrett laughed, a wet, rattling sound that brought dark blood to the corners of his mouth. "Yeah. Well. Turns out when you sell out half a district for a clean transfer, the paperwork doesn't actually stop the bullets." He shifted, and the jacket slipped. Beneath it, his shirt was soaked black, the edges of the wound puckered with faint violet veins—the unmistakable rot of the Seam.

"Who shot you?"

"Does it fucking matter?" Garrett leaned his head back against the tile. "Kaina's people. Or the ones cleaning up after her. They're the same bastards by Thursday. They told me the early-act files were sealed. Said nobody would ever trace the first three bodies back to the reservoir."

"I traced them."

"Good for you." His eyes focused on her, sharp and bitter. "You want the rest of it? The girl they pulled out of the dorms wasn't the first vessel. There was a courier before her—someone who carried the original cipher out of the Hermitage and died in a stairwell off Fourth Street. Your precious heroes stepped right over his body on their way to the plaza and never even checked his pockets."

Mira's jaw tightened. "Where are the pockets now?"

Garrett reached inside his coat with shaking, blood-slick fingers and tossed a drenched leather pouch onto the concrete between them. It landed with a heavy, wet slap.

"Take it," he whispered. "And shoot me before the rot hits my brain. I'm not turning into one of those things."

She looked at him for five long seconds. The violet veins were already creeping up his neck, pulsing faintly under the skin like worms under parchment.

She drew the pistol, aimed once, and pulled the trigger. The shot echoed down the tunnel until it sounded like thunder.

3.
Back on the street, rain had started to fall—cold, gray, and tasting faintly of ash. Mira opened the leather pouch under a flickering bus-stop light. Inside were three waterlogged dog tags, a brass key stamped with the Hermitage seal, and a folded page torn from the original ledger.

Every name from the first night was written there in neat, clerical ink. None of them were accidents.

She pocketed the key, dropped the empty casing into a storm drain, and walked toward the city center. Act One was over, whether anyone wanted to admit it or not.
