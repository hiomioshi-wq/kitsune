THE SOUND SALVAGE COMPANY

A Kitsune Original. Text released under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0.


PART ONE

The Sound Salvage Company has an office on the Moonwake waterfront, between a chandler's and a shop that sells rope by the arm's length, and a yard full of glass floats that catch the light like a field of soap bubbles. The sign over the door says NO SOUND TOO SMALL. Under that, in smaller letters, somebody has painted: WE DO NOT DO WEDDING SPEECHES. Under that, in smaller letters still: ANYMORE.

I went to work there in the spring, because I could not hear out of my left ear and I had heard that the sound salvagers did not care.

The truth is that they cared very much. They cared about sound the way a lighthouse keeper cares about weather. But they did not care that half of mine was missing. Gault, who ran the place, said it was an advantage.

"You'll listen with the other ear harder," he said. "Most divers listen with both and hear neither."

Gault was seventy and built like a bollard, with hands that had been in cold water so long they had gone the color and texture of driftwood. He had been a soundman for fifty years. He had brought up the last song of the Moonwake jukebox before the fire, the toast at the Halversen wedding, a dog barking at a ferry in 1969, and the exact sound of the harbor bell on the morning the Meridian went down. He kept them all in the vault, in glass globes on shelves, each with a paper label in his small square hand. The vault was the coldest room in the building and the quietest. You could hear the globes hum if you stood still, a low sound like a cat asleep.

The way it works—the way Gault explained it to me on my first day, with a mug of tea and the patience of a man who had explained it many times to people who did not believe him—is that sound sinks.

Not all of it. Most sound goes up and out and is gone. But a sound that is held onto, that is listened to hard enough by enough people, becomes heavy. It settles. It goes down through the water the way a stone goes down, and it comes to rest in the cold layer at the bottom of the bay, where the water does not move, and there it stays.

"You dive," Gault said. "You go down slow. You do not chase it. You open the float and you hold it out and you wait, and if the sound is there, it will come into the glass because the glass is empty and the sound is tired of being alone."

"That's not how sound works," I said.

"No," Gault agreed. "But it's how this bay works. There's a difference between the way a thing works and the way it works here."

I learned the dive that spring. The suit is heavy and the float is heavier. You go down on a line with the float on a short tether, and you stop at the cold layer, which is about nine fathoms down and the color of old tea, and you hold the float out in front of you and you do not move. You wait. The cold comes through the suit and into your bones and makes a sound in your own head like a distant train. And then, sometimes, the float warms, and you close it, and you come up, and you have a sound.

The first sound I brought up was a church bell. It had been lost in 1911, when the old church fell into the sea, and it had been ringing underwater ever since, very faint, at the hours nobody kept. I brought it up on a Tuesday afternoon and Gault put it in a globe and labeled it and set it on the shelf, and we played it that evening in the yard, and the whole waterfront stopped to listen. The bell rang once, clear and cold, and then it was gone, and the gulls went up off the roofs as if they had been waiting fifty years for an excuse.

I cried a little. Gault pretended not to notice. He said the first one always gets you.

The clients came in the mornings. They came with requests the way people come to a lost-property office: a father's laugh, a song from a car radio, the sound of a screen door. Gault would take down the particulars—what, when, where, who else was there—and then he would look at me and say, "Perrin, the books," and I would go and look in the ledgers to see if we already had it. We had a surprising amount. We had four screen doors, eleven car radios, and a laugh that had been claimed three times by three different families, which Gault said was a common problem with laughs.

The one we did not have was the ordinary Tuesday. That was Mrs. Ilse.

PART TWO

Mrs. Ilse was eighty-three and came in on a Thursday with a handbag and a folded piece of paper. She sat down in the chair by the window and put the paper on the desk and smoothed it flat with the side of her hand.

"I want the sound of a Tuesday," she said.

Gault nodded as though this were a perfectly ordinary request. "Which Tuesday?"

"An ordinary one. In the kitchen. My husband was fixing the chair." She looked out at the harbor. "He hummed. He always hummed when he was fixing something. It wasn't a tune. It was just—a sound he made. And the chair had a squeak in the leg, and he would press it and listen, and press it and listen. And I was at the stove. It was raining. It was the most ordinary Tuesday of my life and I cannot for the life of me remember what he sounded like."

"When did he pass?" Gault asked, gently.

"Four years ago. I have his voice on the telephone machine. I have two videos from the wedding. But I do not have the humming. I have looked everywhere." She tapped the paper. "I wrote down everything I remember. The chair was oak. The kitchen was small. There was a pot of tea. It was raining, but only a little."

Gault read the paper. Then he looked at me.

"Perrin," he said, "the books."

I looked in the ledgers. There was no entry for an ordinary Tuesday in a small kitchen with an oak chair. There was no entry for humming. We had a lot of sounds, but not that one.

"We'll dive for it," Gault said.

We went out that afternoon. Mrs. Ilse came with us in the boat, wrapped in a blanket, because Gault said the client should be there. The bay was flat and gray. The rain had come in, a fine drizzle, and the town was a smudge of roofs along the shore. I put on the suit and took the float and went down the line.

At nine fathoms the water went cold and brown. I held the float out and waited. I thought about the kitchen: the oak chair, the pot of tea, the small rain. I thought about humming. I thought about my own mother, who used to hum while she did the dishes, and whose voice I had not been able to remember since the winter she died. I thought about how strange it is that the things we lose first are the ordinary ones, the hums and the screen doors and the way somebody pressed a chair leg to hear the squeak.

The float stayed cold.

I stayed down until my air ran low, and then I came up. Gault was waiting at the rail. Mrs. Ilse was sitting in the stern with her hands in her lap.

"Nothing," I said.

Gault did not look disappointed. He looked as though he had expected it.

"Then we do it the other way," he said.

PART THREE

The other way was not in the ledgers. Gault told me about it that night, in the office, with the lamps low and the rain on the window.

"Sound doesn't sink," he said. "I told you it did. That's the story we tell the clients, and it's mostly true, and it helps them. But it's not the whole of it. A sound sinks because somebody is holding it down. When the last person who listened to it lets go, it doesn't sink. It goes up and out and it's gone. The only sounds in the cold layer are the ones somebody is still holding."

"So when we dive—"

"We dive to show the client we're serious. We dive to give them time. We dive because the sea is a good place to remember in, and because a person who has paid for a dive will let themselves remember harder than a person who has just been asked." He tapped the side of his head. "The float doesn't catch the sound. The float catches the client. They hear it while you're down there, and they remember it, and then they can't put it back. That's the salvage. We don't bring the sound up. We bring the memory up. The glass just holds it so they can carry it home."

"That's a lie," I said.

"It's a kindness," said Gault. "And it's not a lie. The sound is real. It's in the person. We just help them find it. The glass is a place to put it down."

The next morning we took Mrs. Ilse out again. This time Gault did not send me down. He sat with her in the stern of the boat while I rowed slowly along the shore, and he asked her questions. What was the chair like? What was the pot of tea like? What was the rain like? What did he hum? Not the tune. The sound. Was it low or high? Was it steady? Did it stop when he found the squeak?

At first she said she didn't know. Then she said it was low. Then she said it was the sound of a man who was not thinking about anything. Then she laughed and said, "He used to hum the same four notes. Over and over. It drove me mad." And then she went quiet, and her hands came up to her mouth, and she said, "Oh."

And she hummed it.

Four notes, low, a little off-key, the sound of a man pressing a chair leg and listening. She hummed it twice. The second time I could hear the squeak underneath it, a tiny oak creak, and the rain on the roof, and the pot of tea ticking on the stove.

Gault took a glass globe from the locker and held it out to her. She looked at it and then at him and then at the globe, and she hummed the four notes into the glass. He sealed it and wrote on the label in his small square hand: *An ordinary Tuesday. Humming, oak chair, rain. Mrs. Ilse.*

She held the globe in her lap all the way back to the harbor. She did not cry. She looked as though she had set down something heavy she had been carrying for four years and was not yet used to the lightness.

That was the day I understood the company.

PART FOUR

I brought up my own sound in the autumn.

I had not told Gault about my mother. I had told him about my ear, because you have to, but not about her. She had died when I was sixteen, and for years I had been able to hear her voice in my head, saying my name, telling me to put a coat on, humming while she did the dishes. Then one day I could not. It had gone the way a word goes when you say it too many times: the shape was there but the sound had come out of it. I had tried to get it back with recordings and photographs and the smell of her soap. Nothing worked. That was why I had come to the sound salvagers. I had thought, in the way you think things when you are grieving, that they might have it in a drawer.

Gault found out the way he found out everything: by listening.

We were in the vault one night, playing the unclaimed sounds, which was something he did on storm nights so that the sounds would not be alone. He had a globe in his hand—a small one, older than the rest—and he was telling me about a woman who had brought in a sound and never come back for it.

"She said it was the sound of her mother at the market," he said. "She couldn't keep it. Said it made her too sad. So she left it with us. We keep the ones nobody claims. We play them on storm nights."

He held out the globe. "Do you want to hear it?"

I took it. It was warm, the way the floats go warm when they have caught something. I held it to my good ear and opened the seal.

A woman said my name.

Not a stranger's version of it. Her version. With the little rise at the end and the softness on the second syllable and the breath she always took before it, the way she used to when she was calling me in from the garden. She said it twice. Then she said, "Put a coat on." Then the globe went quiet.

I sat down on the floor of the vault.

Gault sat down beside me. He did not say anything for a long time. The globes hummed. The storm went over the roof.

"The woman who brought it in," I said, when I could talk. "What did she look like?"

"Small," said Gault. "Gray coat. Bought a glass float and didn't want it filled. Said she'd heard something at the market and wanted it kept somewhere safe, because she wasn't the one it belonged to."

"Did she leave a name?"

He turned the globe over. On the bottom, in old ink, there was a name. It was my aunt's.

I had not known she had come here. I had not known you could do this. I had not known that a sound you had lost could be kept by somebody else, like a coat left at a restaurant, until you were ready to come and get it.

I played the globe three more times that night. Then I put it on the shelf with the other unclaimed sounds, because Gault said it was not lost anymore, and a sound that is not lost should not be kept in a vault. I could hear it whenever I wanted. It had not gone anywhere. It had been waiting.

I am a soundman now. I have my own floats and my own labels and my own small square hand. I dive in the cold layer and I hold the glass out and I wait, and sometimes I bring up a bell, and sometimes I bring up nothing, and sometimes I bring up a person's whole ordinary Tuesday.

The sign over the door still says NO SOUND TOO SMALL. I have not painted the part about wedding speeches. Gault says it's part of the company's history, and that a company should keep its old almosts where people can see them.

The vault is cold and quiet and full of sounds. On storm nights I play them. A dog barks at a ferry. A bell rings once. A woman at a market says a name. And in the yard the empty floats catch the light, row after row of them, waiting to be filled.
