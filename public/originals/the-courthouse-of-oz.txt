PART ONE

In the Emerald City, where everything is green, the courthouse is green too. It stands at the end of a quiet street behind the palace, with green marble steps and a green brass door, and on top a green dome with a green weathercock that points, faithfully, toward the weather.

The courthouse tries things. People are tried across the street, in the ordinary way, by juries of other people; but things—teapots, doorknobs, one very haughty billiard table, and once a gust of wind with opinions, tried in its absence—are tried here. In Oz very little is truly inanimate, and the Emerald City decided long ago that nothing which can make up its mind ought to be above the law.

Princess Ozma established the court in the first year of her reign, after the palace umbrella leaked, quite deliberately, on a Winkie senator who had said something unkind about the Scarecrow's hat, and was sentenced to stand open until it was sorry, which took a fortnight. Everyone agreed such cases deserved a permanent court.

Presiding over it all is old Judge Bahomet, who is mostly awake.

That is not an unkind description; it is the official one. He is a very large, very old gentleman with eyebrows like haystacks and a voice like the closing of a good heavy door, and in his younger days he was a country lawyer for furniture, no one else in Oz having troubled to learn the law as it applies to things.

On the morning this story is about, the docket held one case, and the courthouse steps held a line of travelers that ran down into the street, past the shop that sold lamp wicks.

The case was the signpost of the Winkie road.


PART TWO

The signpost had stood at the fork where the Winkie road leaves the Emerald City road for a hundred years, pointing to four countries of Oz with four long yellow arms, and it had never said one word. Signposts in Oz can talk, as everything can, but this one never had, and travelers had stopped expecting it to, the way you stop expecting a mountain to say good morning.

It was arrested on the first fine morning of spring. The road-warden, a Winkie called Mr. Tagg, who walked the road once a season with a bucket of yellow paint and an official notebook, came over the hill at sunrise and found all four arms curled inward and around, each painted finger on the post, as though the signpost were hugging itself.

"Pointing," wrote Mr. Tagg, after a long think, because the notebook had forms for other offenses but none for that one, "at itself." And underneath: "Sleeping on duty."

Under Section Twelve of the Road Laws of Oz, no guide upon the Princess's roads—no post, no sparrow, no gust of wind with opinions—shall sleep at its duty; for a traveler who trusts a sleeping guide may be carried anywhere at all.

So the signpost was dug up, gently, with a circle of road-earth around its roots—the bailiffs were Winkies, and Winkies are careful with wood—and brought to court in a wheelbarrow, where it stood in the dock looking exactly like a signpost, which is the great difficulty of trying things: they never look worried.

The prosecution was a gavel, a very new gavel, carved in the spring from Winkie oak and polished daily since, appointed last Tuesday. It had never lost a case, chiefly because it had never had one, and it intended to keep both records.

The defense was a sofa: a green plush sofa from the corridor outside the throne room, where it had spent thirty years listening to petitioners explain themselves, and which had taken up the defense of things the way other sofas take up arthritis. It practiced on two principles: first, that most things are innocent if you sit with them long enough; and second, that the ones that are not are usually guilty of something smaller than the charge. It was, everyone agreed, slightly opinionated.

Because a signpost cannot take an oath and cannot be kept from testifying, its arms had been tied to its post with pink ribbon from the courthouse supply, tied in bows, so that it could not point at anybody.

"How does the defendant plead?" asked Judge Bahomet.

Nobody said anything.

"It doesn't," said the sofa. "It has never said anything to anybody. I ask the court to enter a plea of Not Speaking, which is not the same as Not Guilty, but is very rarely the same as guilty."

"So entered," said Judge Bahomet, mostly awake.


PART THREE

The prosecution began with the line of travelers—every one of whom had followed the signpost somewhere truer than where they meant to go, and every one of whom had come to make sure it was well treated, which took the prosecution entirely by surprise.

The first witness was a Munchkin laundress called Mrs. Poole, in a blue bonnet washed so often it had gone the color of the sky on a Tuesday.

"I set out in June for my sister's, in Baker's Bend," she said. "You ask the signpost the way, the way you do, and it pointed me off across country. In the evening I came over a hill, and there was the lake I have been saying I'd see since I was a girl. I'd always said I'd see it before I died, in the way you say things you've decided not to do. I was three days late for my sister, and she forgave me before I finished the sentence, because she has a lake of her own she's been not going to see."

"AND THE DEFENDANT POINTED WRONG," said the gavel, and struck itself once in indignation, so that the whole room stood up by reflex.

"Sit down," said Judge Bahomet. "It's only the prosecutor."

"It pointed truer," said Mrs. Poole.

The second witness was a Winkie called Coggle, a tinsmith's apprentice with the look of a young man who has recently had a door opened in him. He had set out to sell kettles at the market, been pointed down a lane he had never once taken, and arrived at a little yellow house where a woman sat on the step mending clocks. "She looked up," said Coggle, "and said, You're late. I said, I know. We were engaged by Thursday."

The third witness was a Gillikin boy called Fen, about thirteen, arms crossed, chin out, who had set out to find the man who sold his father a goat that turned out to be a frog in a coat. The fork pointed him into a back lane, the lane into a hollow, and in the hollow was a pond full of frogs, singing in parts.

"I sat down angry," said Fen, "and got up calm. I've been calm since June. Nobody in my family has ever been calm. It has caused a great deal of talk."

"Do you press the charge?" asked Judge Bahomet.

"I demand justice," said Fen.

"For what?"

"I'll know it when I see it. That's how it works in my family."

Then the gavel summed up. "WRONG IS WRONG," it said. "A traveler told nothing will stop and think. A traveler told wrong will trust. The law does not ask where they arrived. The law asks what the sign said. A sign that points where it likes is not a guide. It is a wish."

The defense called the Scarecrow, who had come down from his corncob house in the Winkie Country to watch. He came forward loosely and bowed.

"I began my career on a pole," he said. "I stood in a Munchkin cornfield a good many years, and everyone who passed knew exactly what I was for, and not one of them was right. So I can tell the court the difference between a post and a signpost, having been one and lived beside the other. A post holds up what it is given. A signpost chooses. And a sign's business is not the truth; that is the road's business, and the road attends to it without help. A sign's business is the traveler. That is why signs are given arms and not feet: so that they may reach for us, and cannot follow."


PART FOUR

The sofa rose to its cross-examination—that is, it did not rise, being a sofa, but it made itself felt.

"Now, Mr. Tagg," it said. "You found the defendant pointing at itself."

"I did."

"Was it asleep?"

"A signpost pointing at itself," said Mr. Tagg, with the dignity of a man reading his own handwriting aloud, "is either asleep or up to something, and Up To Something is not a printed form."

"Had you ever seen it curled like that before, in eleven years of walking the road?"

"Never once. I only write down what's irregular. That was the most irregular thing I ever saw. It's in the notebook twice, the second one crossed out."

The defense called one last witness: a carter who passed the fork at dusk, the evening before the arrest. "There was an old tinker sitting under the signpost," said the carter. "Pack at his feet, hat over his face, fast asleep, and it starting to drizzle. The signpost had all four arms curled down around him, close as a hen, and not one drop was getting in. I remember thinking, that's a well-trained signpost. Then I thought, no. That's the other way round."

"Was the tinker there in the morning?" asked the sofa.

"Gone before light. He left nothing behind him but the shine. He'd polished every arm on that post in the night, before he went. That was his thank-you. He was a tinker. It's what they have."

"The charge is sleeping on duty," said the sofa. "I suggest what the evidence has shown. On that night there stood at the fork a man with no true place left to be pointed to—no sister, no market, no man who sold the frog—and the defendant pointed at the only honest direction it had left, which is here. And then, so as not to wake him, it held still, curled, through a drizzle, until you came over the hill, Mr. Tagg, and found it in the very posture of its kindness and wrote it down as sleeping."

"KINDNESS IS NOT A DEFENSE," said the gavel. "THE STATUTE ASKS ONE QUESTION, WHICH IS WHETHER IT SLEPT."

"Then," said Judge Bahomet, and stopped. The judge's attention went briefly to the window, but came back at once, and his voice was quieter than the gavel had ever heard it.

"The statute asks whether it slept," he said. "The signpost is the only one who knows. Has anyone asked it?"

Nobody had. The travelers looked at one another. A hundred years of travelers had asked that signpost the way to everywhere in Oz, and not one of them had thought to ask it anything.

"Untie the ribbon," said Judge Bahomet.


PART FIVE

The clerk untied the four pink bows, the whole courtroom leaned forward, and the two bailiffs turned the sofa, since sofas, whatever else you may say of them, do not pivot.

The signpost stood still a moment. Then it began to turn, the way weather does, too slowly to catch at it and slowly enough to watch, creaking gently on its old root. It found Mrs. Poole first. One yellow arm rose and held her bonnet in its painted finger; then the whole post swung until it pointed past the green window, east, and Mrs. Poole, staring at the wall, nodded once, as you nod at the confirmation of a rumor.

Then Coggle: the arm found him and swung south, and Coggle went pink to the ears. Then Fen: the arm held him longest, and the swing ended pointing low, at somewhere still and frog-colored, and Fen said, "Hm," in a small voice.

"Would the defendant," said the sofa, very quietly, "show the court the night before last?"

A signpost cannot point at a time. Everyone knew that. But the four arms curled slowly in and down and around, over the place where a man might sit with a pack at his feet and a hat over his face, and stayed there, and the courtroom was so silent you could hear the weathercock on the dome above, pointing faithfully at the weather.

The gavel sat on its cushion without striking. Judge Bahomet was entirely awake.

Then the arms uncurled, and one pointed at the gavel. "I—that is—STRIKING IS ALL I KNOW," said the gavel, striking twice in confusion, so that the room stood up and sat down twice. But the arm had already swung on, toward the tall doors, and through them, everyone understood, at the west, where the oak grows that a gavel is carved from and has never once gone back to see.

And then the post turned to the bench, rose up as far as its circle of dirt allowed, and pointed at Judge Bahomet.

The clerk dropped the ribbon. The signpost held the judge a moment; and then the whole post swung slowly around the compass of the room—past the benches, past the sofa, past the window with the west light—and settled, pointing at the tall doors, and through them, at the fork, and past it, at whatever lies beyond that a mostly-awake judge has not gone out to see in forty years.

Judge Bahomet took out his watch, which has been stopped since before the clerk was born, and put it away.

"The court," he said, "will take an hour."


PART SIX

When the court came back it was fuller than before, word having brought the whole line of travelers indoors. Judge Bahomet was more awake than anyone had seen him in a season.

"The charge is sleeping on duty," he said. "I find the following. A signpost's arms are its own, and its business is the traveler, not the truth; I have that on good authority from a former post." The Scarecrow, in the public benches, bowed.

"This signpost has told the truth to travelers who asked truly, and something better than the truth to travelers who asked for something they did not need. And on that night it held still through a drizzle, all night, so as not to wake a sleeping tinker. If that is sleeping on duty, then every pillow in Oz is an accomplice, and I decline to try them all before supper."

"Not guilty," said Judge Bahomet. "Furthermore, it will be replanted at the fork with a fifth arm, painted white, to point wherever it pleases, wrong or right, with the blessing of this court. Being wrong on purpose for somebody else's sake is the most expensive kindness there is, because you cannot explain it. A signpost cannot explain anything. It can only point. We ought to be careful whom we call wrong."

He nodded to the gavel, which struck the verdict—once, very softly, the softest strike ever heard in that courthouse, which is a gavel's way of applauding.

Then Judge Bahomet took the afternoon off, his first in forty years, and pushed the wheelbarrow home himself, with the travelers behind him, singing, unevenly, the only song all four countries know, which is not much of a song but nobody has ever minded. They replanted the signpost with its circle of road-earth, and the white arm went up, and by evening the judge was still there, sitting on the grass with his haystack eyebrows in the sun. The Magic Picture showed it all to Princess Ozma, who watched every minute and laughed twice; but that was her privilege.

If you pass the fork today, ask it the way to the Emerald City and it will tell you truly. Ask it the way to somewhere you only think you ought to go, and watch: the five arms will curl slowly in, and point at the only direction on the road that has never once been wrong. It means the grass here is good. It means here will do, for a while.

And it always does.
