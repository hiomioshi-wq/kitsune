THE MOTH LIBRARIAN

A Kitsune Original. Text released under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0.


PART ONE

The Library Under the Lake was two buildings. There was the long wooden reading room, which is the part that drowned, with its brass-handled catalogue and its stove and its nine thousand, four hundred and twelve books; and there was the wing that climbed the east shoulder of the old valley, four storeys of grey stone under a slate roof, which the water climbed but never finished. The flood stopped on the wing's second stair landing, and everything above it stayed dry. The carp calls this part the dry wing. He says it in the voice of somebody declining to be held responsible for it.

The carp is the librarian. He is orange and white and old in a way that makes counting seem rude, and he wears half-moon spectacles that have no business staying on and stay on anyway, and he keeps the drowned floors, the fines and the payment in kind. Borrowers come down the path from the new village with their books held over their heads, and he rises out of the green water to meet them, ledger held in a fin, and everyone agrees he is very good at it, and he agrees.

What the carp does not keep, has never kept, cannot keep, being a fish, is the dry wing. "Nothing up there but shelving," he says, when anybody asks. "And the shelving keeps itself."

For eighty years, nobody asked a third question.

The third question was asked by Sorrel. She was nineteen and small and from the new village below the dam, where she had spent her whole life being the person who notices where things have been put. She answered the advertisement the carp had pinned to the returns box, which read, in full: NIGHT HANDS WANTED. APPLY AFTER DARK. The interview was conducted at moonrise on the water's edge. The carp asked her three questions: whether she was afraid of moths; whether she could keep a secret from herself, which is not the same as keeping one from other people, and is much harder; and whether her hands were patient. She said no, yes, and yes, in that order, and the carp looked at her over his spectacles.

"The moths will want to see the hands," he said. "Hold them out over the water."

She did. A moth landed on her knuckles before she had finished being surprised, and sat there, grey as the light and about as heavy, and the carp said, "Good enough," and that was the whole of the interview.


PART TWO

The library keeps more than books. Everyone in the valley knows the library keeps books, and everyone who has ever borrowed one in kind knows that the carp keeps promises. Only the carp and the moths knew the third thing, which is this: the library keeps what the valley forgets.

It is a matter of plumbing. Everything forgotten in the valley drains downhill, the way rain does. A name let go of on a Tuesday ends up, eventually, in the reservoir, which is the lowest thing for thirty miles and, not coincidentally, the library's roof. Forgettings arrive the way borrowers arrive: without announcement, in ones and twos, a little embarrassed. They come as thin paper slips, faint as breath, and they turn up tucked under the returns box, dried flat on the heron's post, and folded into the dam sluices.

The moths index them.

They come at moonrise, under the eaves of the dry wing, forty-one of them in ordinary weather, in grey and dust-gold, in businesslike silence. Moths, as anyone who has ever been up to the carnival above Seven Bends could tell you, have always kept things; the valley simply never knew where the rest of it was kept. The head of them all is a moth the width of Sorrel's two palms, wings grey as slate, whose wing-dust comes off on your fingers like a rebuke. The carp calls this one the Chief Cataloguer. The Chief Cataloguer has never once acknowledged the title, which is how you can tell it is correct.

The work goes like this. A moth lands on a slip and stands on it, sometimes for an hour; moths read with their feet. Then it takes the slip up, folded like a small banner, and files it.

The shelves of the dry wing are labelled in no alphabet Sorrel has ever seen; the labels are only for looking at. The real arrangement is by how a thing was lost. There is a case of LOST SLOWLY, and a case of LOST AT ONCE, and a locked case of LOST ON PURPOSE, which the Chief Cataloguer opens for no one; and there is one enormous case of LOST BY ACCIDENT, which takes up a whole wall and most of the traffic. There is a case of smells, a drawer of names, and a drawer of songs with the seventh verses worn soft from being stood on. There is one long bay of summers, smelling, when you walk it at night, of every summer in the valley stacked standing on end. And at the bay's far end stand the dream cases, and the first says, on a card in grey letters: DREAMS, LOVED AND LOST ON WAKING.

Which is where the hands come in. A moth can carry a slip anywhere in the world, but it cannot open a swollen drawer, or shift the brass steps, or lift the long case of summers, or turn the key in the case of LOST BY ACCIDENT, which sticks, the carp maintains, because the wood remembers being a tree and takes against hurry. The moths had managed alone for eighty years by being patient. But patience, the Chief Cataloguer had decided at last, was no substitute for thumbs.

So Sorrel's post, the whole of it, is to be at the top of the dry wing stairs at moonrise with a lamp, and to be hands.


PART THREE

She had held the post for one full spring by the night the flying dream came in. The spring had filed, among other things, the miller's Tuesday afternoon, entire, which slipped his mind in March and took four moths to carry; and the summer of the long rain, which three brothers had lost together, coming home as one slip smelling of wet wool. The moths filed it all in silence, and Sorrel opened what wanted opening, and the work was nothing but quiet and glad.

The flying dream arrived on a night wind in early June. It was silver-grey and smelled of height, which is a smell like cold bread and rope, and the slip was still warm at one corner, because dreams stay warm longest where the dreamer held on to them.

The dream belonged to Tam, the cooper's son, who was eight, and it was famous. In it he flew. Not in any complicated way; he went up off the hillside and out over the reservoir, slow as a beetle crossing a table, and because the water was his, it let him see through it, and he could count the chimneys of the old drowned village, all nine, which is more than anyone awake has ever managed. Tam had lost the dream three winters back, the way dreams are lost: all at once, and without witnesses. His mother said he stood at the window a long time that morning, like somebody whose train had left.

The moth who carried the dream was new that season, gold-dusted still, keen as a Monday. It had already misfiled the miller's Tuesday under MONDAY, which is how it had come to be called Tuesday, moths being named, in the dry wing, for their first mistake. Tuesday took the flying dream up into the moonlight, along the bay of summers, to where the dream cases stand side by side, in the order they have always stood in: FLYING, and one case lower, FALLING.

The cases look alike in the moon. That is the only excuse there has ever been, and the Chief Cataloguer does not consider it a good one.


PART FOUR

A misfiled thing does not sit still. That is the trouble with misfiled things; it is nearly the only trouble with them. A dream filed under the wrong feeling keeps on having the wrong feeling, and it leaks, slowly, downhill, back the way it came, the way everything in this valley eventually leaks down to the library.

The flying dream, filed under FALLING, leaked falling.

It took the valley three nights to feel it. On the third night Tam woke shouting, with cold water in his mouth, having dreamed all night of going down instead of up, into dark water full of chimneys. On the fourth night the kites at the spring fair would not stay up, though the wind was doing nothing worth mentioning. On the fifth night the swallows came out at midnight and skimmed the green water with their wingtips, which swallows never do, on account of the midges being asleep. The heron who keeps the shallows stood on one leg and looked at the sky as though it owed him an explanation, and did not get one.

The retired engineer from the water company, who pays his late fees in apologies on Thursdays, said the swallows had it wrong. He said it at the returns box, and Sorrel heard it, and filed it under SOMETHING IS WRONG, which is not a case in the dry wing, but is exactly how the moths file too: a moth will stand on a fact all night until you notice what shelf it wants.

When Tuesday came in that night, it came in low, under the eaves slower than the others, and landed on the dream cases, and stood very still, which is how a moth looks ashamed, and also how a moth looks pleased, and also how a moth looks asleep; and only the smell of height going cold off the slip under FALLING told Sorrel which one it was.


PART FIVE

The carp was consulted at moonrise, at the water's edge, and adjusted his spectacles with a fin.

"A misfile is not a fine," he said. "A fine is a debt. A misfile is a shelf's opinion. Shelves are wrong once a decade." He paused. "So am I."

He explained the rest without being asked; he is a librarian, and explaining comes with the ledger. A misfiled thing must be refiled before the valley re-forgets it the wrong way round. A flying dream left under FALLING long enough stops leaking falling and starts belonging there, the way a coat left in a house long enough belongs to the house; and then Tam would never fly again, not even asleep, and the valley would go on being the kind of valley where a boy who had counted nine chimneys once can no longer get up off the hillside in his sleep.

"Refile it tonight," said the carp. "Moth custom is that the filer files its own errors. Yours is to carry the lamp, hold the ladder, and open the drawer. And, Sorrel." He rose an inch out of the water, which for a carp is the height of urgency. "Do not read it aloud. A dream read aloud in the wrong voice becomes the reader's. You are not fitted for flying. You are fitted for drawers."

The correction walk began at one in the morning, with the moon-coloured lamp, and Tuesday riding the slip's corner like a very small ferryman. They went through the case of names, where the name of the dog dead thirty years lies filed next to an old word for the colour of the lake; past the locked case of LOST ON PURPOSE, behind whose grille Sorrel had seen a folded letter standing on end, facing out, and had asked no question, because she keeps secrets from herself, as advertised; along the bay of summers; and so to the dream cases, where the slip lay under FALLING, cold now, smelling of deep water.

She took it up with both hands, and it was heavier than a dream has any right to be, and the first word on it, faint in the lamp, was up, and her mouth, without consulting her, had begun to shape the next one when the Chief Cataloguer landed on her knuckles, grey as a rebuke, and stayed there, heavy as a coin, until her mouth unhappened.

FLYING, drawer seventeen, sticks. It has stuck since the damp summer, and it will stick, most likely, forever. She set the lamp down and opened it with both hands, and Tuesday walked the slip in over the sill of the drawer, and stood on it a while, reading it once more with its feet, gently, the way you would pat something that had been out in the rain.

A misfiled thing takes three nights to feel the wrong shelf, and one morning to forgive the right one. On the morning after the refile, the swallows went up high over the dam and stayed there, where the midges were. The kites at the fair rose into a sky with nothing in it worth mentioning and sat there anyway, out of respect. And in the new village, Tam woke on the hillside with his arms out, and had not the dream, yet, but the gladness of it, which is how dreams come back when they come back at all: not all at once, but like the tide, checking first whether anybody is still waiting.

He flew again on the Friday, and counted the chimneys, and reported five, because you cannot take everything a dream says at face value, even a good dream; the other four, the carp points out, are still filed.


PART SIX

The staff ledger of the library is kept, of course, at the bottom of the reservoir, because the carp is not going to climb anybody's stairs. Sorrel asked about her post the day she was confirmed, and the carp rose with the ledger in his fins and let her read over the water. The establishment has four lines. Librarian. Keeper of the Returns. Heron, part-time. And the fourth line, which has been in the ledger since the year the water came, always empty:

MOTH LIBRARIAN.

"We assumed a moth would apply eventually," said the carp. "Moths being expected to make their own arrangements. You are the first applicant in eighty years. The moths appear satisfied."

She is still there. The drawer of flying dreams still sticks on damp nights, and she is always at the top of the stairs with the lamp before the queue forms. And when the children of the new village found out, as children find everything out, that the library above the lake will keep a thing for you if you lose it on purpose and address it properly, the slips began to arrive addressed in crayon, TO THE MOTH LIBRARIAN, CARE OF THE LAKE, KEEP THIS UNTIL I AM BIGGER, and she filed every one, in the case that has no lock, because there is nothing on this earth more deliberately lost on purpose than a thing you mean to come back for.

Libraries, the carp says, are not about the books. They are about the coming back. He says it about the drowned floors, but he has never once objected to being quoted one storey up.
