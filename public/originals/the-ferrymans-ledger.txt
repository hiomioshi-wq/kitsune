THE FERRYMAN'S LEDGER

A Kitsune Original. Text released under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0.


PART ONE

The ferry to Moonwake Bay is small, green, and older than anyone on the coast will admit to a birthday, and since the day the service began it has kept a ledger.

The ledger is a book the size of a door, and it lives on the shelf under the ferry office bench, and it is written entirely in pencil, because ink will not dry at sea and Mott does not hold with pretending otherwise. Every crossing has a line: the date, the tide, the fare, the weather, and a remark. The remarks are Mott's. There is a remark for every crossing, even the dull ones, because, Mott says, a crossing without a remark is a crossing that didn't happen all the way through, and he will not be party to half-happenings. A page of the ledger holds a decade. The remarks say things like QUIET, or ONE GULL, or KETTLE BOILED TWICE, and once, memorably, PROPOSAL; SHE SAID ASK AGAIN IN SPRING, which cost the ferry three weeks of not looking at the couple.

The ferryman is a sea turtle called Mott, who has kept the crossing since his mother retired to the warm shallows, and whose mother kept it before him, and whose grandmother opened the service with one boat, one bell, and a spare pencil in case the first was swept away. The bell is brass and hangs at the stern, and the custom of it is fixed as the tide: one ring when the ferry goes out, one ring when it comes home. Mott's flippers have worn a groove in the shelf under the ledger where the pencil rests, and his mother's flippers wore it before him, and the groove is the only part of the service that has never once been late.

The ferry leaves on the afternoon tide, every day the tide allows, because Mott is a great believer in the tide's judgment and nobody else's. The crossing is two hours out and two hours back, bar weather. Bar weather is not custom, it is arithmetic: when the wind is on the bar, the ferry stays tied to the quay, and the bell doesn't ring at all, which is the loudest thing a bell can do.

The apprentice is a sandpiper called Pim, who is nineteen and small, and who was sent to the landing by his mother to learn a patient trade, sandpipers being famously short of patience and her son being famous, among sandpipers, for it. Pim rings the out-bell, rules the fresh pages, sharpens the pencils, keeps the fair tide notes, and reads the water's edge all day, which is a sandpiper's idea of reading, and is, on this coast, usually more accurate than the almanac.


PART TWO

In the slow season, Mott set Pim to checking the old pages against the tide tables, which is work that has never once found anything, the tide being the only punctual thing on this coast, and Mott believing firmly in checking on principle.

It was Pim who found the entry.

He found it on the page for the current decade, which is only half ruled, the decade being young, and he found it because a sandpiper's eye goes along a line the way a sandpiper's feet go along the shore: noticing everything, pausing at what doesn't belong. What didn't belong was this: a passenger with two lines.

The first line was three years old. The seventh of June, small tide, fare paid both ways, one carpetbag, remark: AWAY.

The second line was in the same pencil, in the same unhurried slope, and it was dated the seventh of June, this very month, four days from now: following wind from four, one gull, remark: HOME.

Same page. Same hand. Three years apart, to the day.

Pim read both lines nine times, which is what the tide tables get, and then carried the ledger up the beach to where Mott was warming his back against the ferry office, and set it down open, and said: "Which of these is the memory?"

Mott read the lines once, which is what he does to everything.

"She came back," he said.

"Mott," said Pim. "That crossing hasn't happened yet."

"Yes," said Mott, in the voice of somebody being handed the obvious and finding it in good condition. "That's why it's in pencil."


PART THREE

The passenger is a hare called Aster, and the valley tells her story in three sentences, the way valleys do. She crossed to Moonwake Bay three years ago, to settle her sister's house on the far shore, the sister having died in the spring and the house having nobody left in it who knew where anything was. She stood on the quay with one carpetbag, and paid her fare at the box, and then, without being asked, paid it again, which no passenger in sixty-one years had done before her or since, and she said: "The other one's my coming home. Same tide, three years on. Keep it for me, and don't lose the boat in between."

Mott wrote the second crossing that same evening. That is the practice, and it is the practice for a reason: a fare paid both ways is a crossing owed, and the ledger does not hold with owing things off the books. And an owed crossing is entered whole. Not just the day, but the day's weather; not just the tide, but the tide's wind. You write a crossing owed as a crossing certain, Mott says, down to the gull, or you don't write it at all, and you give the lady back her coin.

Pim, being a bird of the shore, went looking for the hole in this the way his species hunts: in short runs, at an angle, all day. "You wrote FOLLOWING WIND FROM FOUR," he said. "Three years in advance. You wrote ONE GULL. Three years in advance."

"I wrote what I was sure of," said Mott.

"And the gull?"

"I was sure of the gull."

The valley, meanwhile, keeps its own ledger of Aster, and its arithmetic is shorter. Her grand-niece Bracken has been at the landing on the seventh of June every year for three years, with tea in a flask and her good coat on, because Aster said same tide, three years on, and Bracken was brought up to be at the water when a thing is due at the water. This year, Bracken had told the tea-seller on the quay, was the last year. Three is a fair number of years to stand on a cold landing being punctual for somebody else's promise, and Bracken is a hare, and even a hare who keeps her word keeps it best with an end in sight.

Pim took his half-day on the fifth and walked the coast road to where the weather cartographer keeps his maps, and asked an otter called Odo what the seventh of June was doing, in weather, at this end of the coast. Odo unrolled the map for the seventh with both paws and held its corners down with two mugs.

"Fog till mid-afternoon," said Odo. "Then it lifts. Following wind from four o'clock, right along this stretch." He tapped the bar with one claw. "You can have a gull with it, if you're taking one; there's a gull that works this coast most evenings. It's my apprentice's," he added. "She notices it. It's a bit of a show-off."

"Your map agrees with the ferry's ledger," said Pim.

"Of course it does," said Odo. "We're both describing the same sea. It's the sea that can't be trusted to make a fuss about it."


PART FOUR

The seventh of June arrived wrapped in fog, exactly as mapped and as pencilled, and the fog lay on the bar like a wool blanket on a job it resents.

The afternoon tide came and went without the bell, because this was arithmetic: nobody crosses to a landing they cannot see, for a reunion that would be fogged at both ends, and of Mott's judgment on that there has never once been an appeal. But the ledger's line for the day said HOME, with a following wind from four, and it did not say when the crossing left; and Pim, who had been reading the water's edge all day from the office bench, noticed at half past three that the ripple line along the bar had changed its mind.

"The fog's had enough," he said. "From the edge outward, it's going soft. It'll be gone by four."

"The ferry leaves on the afternoon tide," said Mott, who believed it even as he said it, the way you believe an old coat.

"The afternoon tide was this morning," said Pim. "The landing in the ledger is owed at dusk. That leaves now."

Mott looked at the bar for the length of time a turtle takes to be sure of something, which is the length of time other people take to be sure of six things, all of them wrong. "The landing is owed," he agreed at last. "The leaving is ours." And he went down the beach like a slow green decision, and Pim rang the bell once, out, into the fog that was already letting go of it.

The crossing was quiet and strange and shorter-feeling than two hours, the way things feel that have been promised a long time. The fog lifted off the bar in one grey piece, like a curtain taken up by its own corner; and the four-o'clock wind arrived at four o'clock exactly, following; and the gull arrived with the wind, out of nowhere, at the height of the mast, which for a gull is a manner of arriving, and it did not leave.

The landing at Moonwake Bay was gold with evening. On the mooring post at the end of it sat a hare with one carpetbag and her good coat on, sitting the way you sit when you have been told an hour by somebody who has never once been late. Aster looked down the ladder at Mott's face coming up out of the evening water, and said, "You're punctual," and then, before Mott could answer, in the voice of somebody paying the rest of a fare three years old: "I told you the tide."

The bell rang once, home, into the last of the gold. And at the near landing, on her last year of being punctual, Bracken already had the flask open and two cups poured, because she had heard the out-bell at four and done in one afternoon the arithmetic she had had three years of practice at: fog; a bell; a landing owed; the four-o'clock wind coming; and the family habit of keeping a thing once you have promised it, on either side of the water.

Aster stepped ashore with the carpetbag, and held Bracken by both shoulders at arm's length, looking, and then longer, not looking; and she looked at the two cups, already steaming, and said the thing every arrival says on this coast when the arrival has been owed longest, which is: "You didn't have to wait."

And Bracken, who had waited three years, and would have stood a fourth with her good coat on, said what the whole landing was thinking, which is the other half of the custom: "I know. I liked it."


PART FIVE

That evening the fair copy was written up, which is Pim's work: the ruled line, the date, the tide, the fare, the weather, the remark, in the small even hand that Mott can no longer be bothered to be envious of. FOLLOWING WIND FROM FOUR, ONE GULL, HOME. He wrote it exactly as it had been written three years in advance, which is to say exactly, and it fitted the day the way a key fits a lock it was cut for, and the pencil behaved like a pencil that had been holding its breath since the seventh of June, three years gone.

He ruled the next line, empty, for the next crossing. And then he asked the question he had been carrying all day, the way you carry a stone you keep picking up to see whether it is still a stone.

"Mott. The two lines. The one from three years ago, and the one you wrote the night she paid. Which of them is the memory?"

Mott was quiet for the length of time a turtle takes. Behind them, in the office, the kettle came to the boil and nobody turned it off, it being that kind of evening, and the kettle boiling twice went in the fair copy, because a remark is owed for every crossing and this crossing had earned two.

"Neither," said Mott at last. "A crossing isn't a thing to remember. It's a thing to keep. The pencil line and the water line keep the same crossing twice: once on the page, so that it can be true even when it isn't happening; and once between the landings, so that it can be true to the people in it. The memory isn't in the ledger, boy." He settled deeper into his shell, the way he does when a thing has been said all the way to the end of it. "The memory is whoever comes along afterward, and reads the page, and waits at the water. That's why we write it down. Paper is just the bell's other half."

Pim thought about that while he sharpened the pencil, and set it back in the groove worn by three generations of flippers, sharp end toward the sea, the way it has always faced.

Then he opened the ledger to the decade's page, and under the line with the wind and the gull and the home, in his small even hand, because the day had earned a second remark and the ledger is nothing if not honest, he wrote:

WAITED. GLAD.
