THE MUSEUM OF ALMOST

A Kitsune Original. Text released under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0.


PART ONE

The Museum of Almost is on Gannet Street, between a locksmith and a shop that sells nothing but buttons, and it is open from six in the evening until two in the morning, which are the hours when people are most likely to have almost done something.

I have been the night guard there for eleven years. My name is Nell Orr. I carry a torch that does not work very well and a ring of keys that does not open very much, because the museum does not really need guarding. Nobody steals from the Museum of Almost. What would you take? A bus ticket someone nearly used? A ring somebody almost gave? The objects are worthless. It is the almosts that are valuable, and you cannot put an almost in your pocket.

The collection is arranged by kind. The Hall of Unsent Letters is the longest room: three hundred and forty-one letters, all written, all sealed, none posted. The Shelf of Trains Not Taken holds a row of little paper stubs, each one a journey somebody stood on a platform and did not make. The Drawer of Names Almost Called is the smallest and the heaviest, a single drawer that takes two hands to pull and smells of every telephone in the world. There are other rooms. There is a room of apologies that were rehearsed in the shower and never spoken. There is a room of careers that were almost applied for, which is mostly hats. There is a jar of the things people almost said at weddings.

Each object sits under a little lamp with a card: the object, the date, and the almost. *An umbrella. Left on the train. The day she almost followed him.* *A corkscrew. The evening he almost told his brother.* The cards never use names. The museum does not collect names; it collects the moment of turning, and a name is not needed for a turning.

The curator is Mrs. Oviatt. She is ninety-two and has the kind of white hair that looks deliberate. She sits at a desk by the door and takes the admission, which is one thing you almost did. You do not have to prove it. She can tell. She has been able to tell since 1958, when she founded the museum with a shoebox of her own almosts and a borrowed room above the button shop, and she has never once been wrong.

I like the quiet. At midnight the lamps hum. At one o'clock the building settles, and the almosts settle with it, and for a little while the whole museum feels like a held breath. I walk the rooms and I do not touch anything. That is the first rule. The second rule is that you do not read the cards out loud. I broke the second rule once, in my first month, and for three days afterward every telephone in the building rang at once.

I have my own almost, of course. Everyone does. I do not know what it is. That is another thing about the museum: it will not tell you. You have to find it.

On the night this story begins, I came on shift at six and found a door where the wall had been.

It was a good door, oak, with a brass handle worn smooth. It stood between the Hall of Unsent Letters and the room of rehearsed apologies, where I would have sworn there had only ever been wall. There was no dust around it. The wall did not look as though it had been cut. It looked as though the door had always been there and I had simply failed to notice it, which is a feeling I have had about several things in my life.

Mrs. Oviatt was at her desk with her glasses on and a cup of tea that had stopped steaming a week ago. She did not look surprised.

"New room," she said.

"New room?"

"The museum grows," she said. "It doesn't have a choice. People keep almost doing things." She nodded at the door. "That one's yours, I think."

I did not ask what she meant. I have learned not to ask Mrs. Oviatt questions before midnight. But I went to the door. The brass handle was warm. I opened it.

The room was small: a single case, a single lamp, a chair. The case held a mug. It was blue, chipped on the rim, the kind of mug that comes free with a garage and outlives the car. I knew it at once. I had held it in my hands every morning for four years. I still had one like it in my kitchen, unless I had thrown it out, unless I had been unable not to throw it out.

The card under the mug said: *A blue mug. The morning you almost said yes.*

I stood very still.

I would like to tell you that I had forgotten. People say that about the turning points of their lives, that they forgot, that it slipped their mind. I had not forgotten. I had spent eleven years walking up and down these rooms at night precisely because I had not forgotten, and because the museum was the one place in the world where a person could be surrounded by near-misses and not have to explain her own.

The almost had happened a long time ago. There was a woman. Her name was June. She was going to the coast for the summer, to work on a boat, and she asked me to come with her. She asked me in her kitchen on a Tuesday morning, with the blue mug in my hands and the bus already idling at the end of the road. She said, *Come. It'll be an adventure. We can be poor together.* And I almost said yes. I felt the yes rise in me like a tide. It reached my mouth.

And then I said, "I've got work."

She nodded. She was not angry. That was the worst part, that she was not angry. She finished her coffee and picked up her bag and kissed my cheek, and the door closed, and the bus went, and I sat at her kitchen table for a long time holding the blue mug.

The card under the mug did not say any of that. It only said the almost. That is the museum's way. It gives you the shape and lets you bring the rest.

I heard the door close behind me. Mrs. Oviatt was standing in it with her hands folded.

"You've found it," she said.

"I don't want it."

"That's usually a sign it's yours." She came in and stood beside me. The lamp hummed. "You know what the museum is, Nell. I've never told you straight, but you've worked here long enough to guess."

"It's a collection of things people didn't do."

"It's a collection of the moment before the thing," said Mrs. Oviatt. "The leaning. The object is only where the moment leaned. Your mug is here because one Tuesday morning the whole rest of your life leaned over a kitchen table and then stood back up."

I looked at the mug. In the lamplight the chip on the rim looked like a tiny mouth.

"What happens if I take it?"

"Then the almost becomes a real past. You'll remember it the way you remember real things: as something that happened, and ended, and cannot be changed. It will stop being a possibility. It will start being a door you closed."

"And if I leave it?"

"Then it stays open. Forever. The room stays. The cup stays. You get to keep wondering."

I said, "That sounds like the same thing."

"No," said Mrs. Oviatt. "It's the opposite thing. One is grief. The other is a kind of haunting." She smiled. "Most people leave theirs. The museum is mostly full of things people left."

She went back to her desk. I stayed in the room for a long time.

PART TWO

I did my rounds at midnight with the door open behind me. The museum was quieter than usual, as though it were listening. In the Hall of Unsent Letters somebody's old lamp flickered. The trains on their shelf seemed to lean toward the little paper stubs. Even the jar of things almost said at weddings had a kind of glow to it, a soft amber like honey held up to a window.

At one o'clock I came back to the room. The chair had been moved. There was somebody sitting in it.

She looked like me. That is the only way to say it. She looked like the photograph on my own identification card, if the photograph had been taken on a coast, in the sun, by somebody who loved the photographer. Her hair was longer. She had a tan. She was holding the blue mug, and the chip was on the other side.

"Nell," she said.

"You're not real."

"I'm the almost," she said. "I'm what you would have been if you'd got on the bus." She turned the mug in her hands. "I'm not a ghost. I'm a direction. The museum keeps directions."

I sat down on the floor, because there was only one chair.

"Is it good?" I asked. "The coast?"

She thought about it. "It's hard," she said. "The boat leaks. June is terrible at cooking. We argued about money for three years. There's a child." She smiled. "There's a dog that hates me. It's not a better life, Nell. It's just the other one."

"But it's a life."

"It's the life that almost was. It doesn't get to be real unless you make it real." She held out the mug. "That's the choice. You can take this, and I'll be a memory, and you'll have to grieve me, and then you'll be free of the wondering. Or you can leave it, and I'll stay here, and you can visit, and neither of us will ever know how it ends."

I looked at the mug. It was a very ordinary mug. It was the most important object I had ever been offered.

"What did you do?" I said. "The real you. The one who said yes. What did she do when the almost came for her?"

The almost-Nell laughed. "I don't know. I'm only the version that said yes. I don't get to see the museum. I'm the exhibit." She set the mug down on the case. "But I can tell you one thing. Your June is still on the coast. She writes to the button shop every Christmas. Mrs. Oviatt knows the address."

I sat on the floor for a long time. Then I stood up and picked up the blue mug.

The room did not vanish. I had expected it to vanish. The almost-Nell did not vanish either. She began to fade, slowly, from the edges, like a photograph left in the sun. She did not look sad. She looked like somebody setting down a heavy bag at the end of a long walk.

"Thank you," she said.

"For what?"

"For looking at me," she said. "An almost nobody looks at is just a regret. An almost somebody looks at is a life you didn't live." She was almost gone. "Say yes to something, Nell. It doesn't have to be the coast."

Then the chair was empty.

PART THREE

Mrs. Oviatt was at her desk with her coat on, which she never did before two o'clock.

"Well?" she said.

I put the blue mug on her desk.

She looked at it for a moment, then at me. "You took it."

"I took it."

"And?"

"I don't know yet. I think I'm going to be sad for a while. But I'm also going to write to June." I looked at the mug. "I'm not going to ask for the life back. I'm just going to say hello."

Mrs. Oviatt nodded slowly. Then she did something I had never seen her do. She opened the bottom drawer of her desk and took out a small paper bag and set it on the desk between us. Inside was a single pale blue button.

"That's mine," she said. "The thing I almost did. I've been the curator of this museum for sixty-four years and I have never once taken my own almost off the shelf." She looked at the button. "I was going to be a dressmaker. Did you know that? I had a shop, a little one, on a street with a plane tree. I almost opened it. Then my father died and the money went to the funeral and I got a job taking admissions at a museum, and that was that." She smiled. "I've never regretted it. But I've never been able to let go of the shop either. So I built a museum where everybody else could have the choice I couldn't make."

"What would you do," I said, "if you took the button?"

She was quiet for a long moment. Then she put the button back in the bag and put the bag back in the drawer. "I'd have to close the shop," she said. "And I'd have to open it. And I'm ninety-two." She patted the drawer. "Some almosts are for keeping. That's what makes them almosts." She nodded at the blue mug. "Yours wasn't."

I took the mug home. I washed it and put it in the cupboard with the others. It was just a mug again. That was the strange thing: the whole museum had held it up as a relic, and now it was just a mug with a chip in it, and I could drink tea out of it, and the tea tasted like tea.

At six the next evening I came on shift and went straight to the space between the Hall of Unsent Letters and the room of rehearsed apologies. The door was gone. The wall was a wall. There was a small brass plaque where the handle had been, and the plaque said: *The Almost of the Night Guard. Closed.*

I stood there for a while. Then I went to Mrs. Oviatt's desk and asked for June's address.

She wrote it on the back of an old admission ticket, in a careful hand, and she did not say anything, and her eyes were wet, and she pretended they were not.

That is how the Museum of Almost works. It is not a museum of failure, whatever people think. It is a museum of the moment when the whole rest of a life leans, and then either falls or stands back up. Both are allowed. The museum keeps the ones that stood back up so that somebody can look at them, and learn the shape of their own leaning, and decide what to do the next time the bus is idling at the end of the road.

I wrote to June that night. I said hello. I said I had kept the mug. I said the coast sounded hard and I was glad she had gone.

She wrote back in the spring. She is a grandmother now. She is terrible at cooking, and the dog still hates her, and she says the boat leaks, and she says if I am ever on the coast there is a spare room.

I have not gone. Not yet. But I have started saying yes to small things, and the museum has started a new wing, and the plaque on the wall now says:

*The Almost of the Night Guard. Open.*

Mrs. Oviatt says that is the best kind.
