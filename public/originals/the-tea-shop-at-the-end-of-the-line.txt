THE TEA SHOP AT THE END OF THE LINE

A Kitsune Original. Text released under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0.


ONE

The little train ran from the city to the sea, and where the rails stopped, in the last of the sand dunes, there was a tea shop.

It had no sign. It had a red door, a window full of steam, and a bell above the door that rang a half-note flat, as if it had once been dropped and never forgiven anyone. Inside there were six stools, one long counter made from the hull of a rowing boat, and a tanuki called Ponzu, who was round and slow and wore an apron with a burn on it in the exact shape of Australia.

Ponzu opened the shop at the first train and closed it after the last. In between he made tea. He made it badly, by most standards. He let it steep too long and he never measured anything and sometimes he forgot it altogether and it went black and bitter in the pot, and then he would drink it himself, wincing, and say that it built character.

Nobody minded. Nobody came for the tea.

They came because of the shelf.

The shelf ran along the back wall, from the door to the stove, and on it were all the things that people had left on the little train. Umbrellas. Gloves, in every colour, never in pairs. A trumpet. A jar of pickled plums with a name on the lid in handwriting so small it could only belong to a grandmother. A teddy bear with one ear sewn on backwards. Three hundred and twelve paperback books. A wedding ring.

Ponzu kept them all. The railway had a lost property office in the city, but it was on the fourth floor and had a lift that only worked on Tuesdays, and Ponzu had decided long ago that people who lose things deserve better than a lift that only works on Tuesdays.

So he kept the shelf. And people came to the end of the line, and sat on a stool, and drank his terrible tea, and looked.


TWO

The girl came on the last train on a wet evening in October.

She was ten, perhaps, a hedgehog with a raincoat buttoned up wrong and a ticket held so tightly in her paw that it had gone soft. She stood in the doorway with rain dripping off her quills and looked at the shelf with the expression of someone reading a very long list of names, looking for one.

"Shut the door," said Ponzu, "or the steam will escape and then it's just a room."

She shut the door. The bell rang its sour half-note.

"I'm looking for something," she said.

"Most people are," said Ponzu. "Sit down. The tea's dreadful."

She sat. He poured her a cup of something that had been brewing since lunchtime and was now the colour of an old boot. She drank it without complaint, which told him a great deal.

"What did you lose?"

The girl looked at her cup.

"A button," she said.

Ponzu nodded slowly, as if buttons were the most serious matter in the world. In his experience, they often were.

"What sort of button?"

"Green. Wooden. With four holes. It was off my grandfather's coat." She turned the cup in her paws. "He gave it to me on the train. He said, hold this, it keeps coming off. And then we got to the hospital and I put it in my pocket and…" She stopped. "And now it's not in my pocket."

"When was this?"

"March."

Ponzu did not say anything for a while. Outside, the sea made its long, patient noise against the sand.

"Is he better now?" he asked, though he could already see the answer, in the way she held the cup.

"No," said the girl. "He's not anything now."

Ponzu put down his teapot.


THREE

There were, he estimated, four thousand buttons on the shelf.

They lived in a biscuit tin, a big blue one with a picture of a lighthouse on the lid, and they had accumulated over the years in the way that buttons do: one at a time, quietly, from nowhere. People did not usually come back for buttons. People did not usually notice a button was gone until weeks later, standing in front of a mirror with a coat that wouldn't close.

Ponzu brought down the tin and set it on the counter between them. He lifted the lid. The buttons gleamed in the lamplight like a small, untidy treasure.

"Well," he said. "Let's look."

The girl stared at the tin. "There are so many."

"There are," Ponzu agreed. "That's what makes it a search."

He did not tip them out. You never tip buttons out; they roll under things and become myths. Instead he took them one at a time, held each up to the lamp, and put it in one of two saucers. Green, and not green. The girl watched him for a while, and then, without being asked, she began to do the same.

The rain went on. The last train had already left. Neither of them mentioned it.

Brass buttons from soldiers' coats. Pearl ones from cardigans. A plastic one shaped like a strawberry. A tiny gold one that might have come from a doll's dress, or a very small duke. A button with a face on it that Ponzu turned over quickly and put at the bottom of the not-green saucer and did not discuss.

"Why do you keep all of them?" the girl asked, at about the five-hundredth button.

Ponzu thought about it.

"Because when you lose something," he said, "the worst part isn't that it's gone. The worst part is that it's gone and nobody noticed. Nobody said, oh, where did that go? Nobody kept a place for it." He held up a button, examined it, put it in not-green. "I keep a place for it. That's all. It's not much."

"It's a lot of buttons," said the girl.

"It is a great many buttons," said Ponzu gravely, "for not much."

At the thousandth button she laughed, very briefly, at a button shaped like a tiny fish. At the two-thousandth she fell asleep on the counter with her cheek on her raincoat sleeve, and Ponzu put a blanket over her and kept going alone, and the stove ticked, and the sea breathed, and the green saucer slowly filled.


FOUR

There were forty-one green buttons.

When she woke, in the grey early light, with the blanket around her and the window full of fog, she found them laid out in rows on the boat-hull counter like a small, solemn army.

She looked at them for a long time.

"Wooden," Ponzu said quietly, from the stove, where he was making the first pot of the day, badly. "Four holes. That leaves eleven."

She went down the rows. She picked each one up and turned it over and held it against her palm, the way you would hold something that might still be warm. One, two, three. A shade too dark. A shade too light. Five, six. Too round. Too flat.

At the ninth, her paw stopped.

It was an ordinary button. Green wood, slightly worn at the edge where it had rubbed against a coat for thirty years. Four holes. In one of the holes, a single thread, frayed, the colour of brown wool.

She did not say anything. She did not need to. She closed her paw around it and put it against her chest and stayed very still, and Ponzu turned back to the stove and was very busy for some time with a teapot that did not need his attention.

When he turned around again, she had put the button in the inside pocket of her raincoat and buttoned the pocket shut, and then she had buttoned the whole raincoat, properly this time, every button in its right hole.

"Thank you," she said.

"You found it," said Ponzu. "I just kept the place."

She looked at the shelf: the umbrellas, the trumpet, the teddy bear with its backwards ear, the ring. "Do they all come back? The people?"

"Some," said Ponzu. "Not many. Most things stay."

"That's sad."

"Is it?" Ponzu poured two cups. "I don't think so. Somebody loved every one of them enough to carry it. That's a lot of love on one shelf. It keeps the shop warm."

The girl considered this with the full seriousness of ten.

"Can I come back?" she said. "Not for a button. Just to help."

Ponzu put down the teapot. His apron, with its burn the shape of Australia, rose and fell once.

"The first train's at seven," he said. "I'll leave the door open."


FIVE

She came on Saturdays, after that.

She learned to sort the umbrellas by colour and the gloves by hand, and to write the date on a label for every new thing, and to never, ever tip out the buttons. She learned that people who come to the end of the line looking for something are not really looking for an umbrella. She learned to make the tea, too, and she made it rather well, which Ponzu found deeply suspicious.

Sometimes someone would come in and stand in the doorway with that particular look, reading the shelf like a list of names, and she would say, "Shut the door, or the steam escapes and it's just a room," and pour them a cup, and fetch the right tin.

And on the inside of her raincoat, in a pocket buttoned shut, she always carried a green wooden button with four holes and a single brown thread, so that it could never be lost again.

The little train still runs from the city to the sea. If you take it to the end of the line, past the last of the dunes, you'll find a red door, a window full of steam, and a bell that rings a half-note flat.

Shut the door when you go in.

The tea is dreadful.

Nobody minds.


THE END
