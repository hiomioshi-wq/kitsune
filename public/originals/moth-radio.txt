MOTH RADIO

A Kitsune Original. Text released under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0.


ONE

At two in the morning, when the town was asleep, Wren went on air.

The studio was the top of the old water tower: a round tin room with one window, one microphone held together with tape, and a radio transmitter the size of a wardrobe that her great-aunt had built from a washing machine, three toasters and something she always referred to only as "the Belgian part." It hummed. It smelled of hot dust. When it was working properly, a small orange light came on at the top, and a moth usually came to sit on it.

Wren was a mouse. She was fourteen, and she did not sleep.

That was not a complaint; it was a fact, like her ears, or the colour of her fur. Some people are tall. Wren lay in bed with her eyes open for hours, listening to the house click and settle, and at two in the morning she gave up, every night, climbed the ladder to the top of the water tower, and turned on the light.

"Good morning," she said into the microphone. "Or good night. Whichever one you're having. This is Moth Radio, on a frequency nobody remembers, from the top of the tower on Hill Street. It's two o'clock. It's cold. The moon looks like someone's taken a bite out of it. If you're awake, you're not the only one."

Nobody had ever answered. She had been doing it for a year.


TWO

It was a strange show. It had no music, because the Belgian part didn't care for music; when she played a record it made a sound like a cat being folded. So instead she talked.

She read out the weather for places nobody lived: "Out over the sea, it's dark and enormous, with a light chance of whales." She read the shipping forecast from an almanac from 1953. She described the stars she could see from the window, and, when it was cloudy, the stars she imagined behind the cloud, which were usually better. She read letters from listeners, which she wrote herself, because none ever came.

"Dear Moth Radio," she read. "I am a lamp-post on Ferry Road and I am very tired of moths. Please can you ask them to go somewhere else. Yours, Lamp-post." She paused. "Dear Lamp-post. I'm sorry. They can't help it. You're very bright and they're very lonely. Maybe think of it as a compliment."

The orange light glowed. The moth on top of the transmitter, a big furry brown one she had named Mrs. Patel, opened and closed its wings.

"Mrs. Patel agrees," said Wren.

At four o'clock, she said goodnight, turned off the light, and went home, and lay in bed watching the window go grey.


THREE

The first letter came in November.

It was pushed under the door at the bottom of the tower, in a small brown envelope, addressed in pencil: TO MOTH RADIO, THE TOWER.

Wren stood in the cold stairwell for a long time holding it, as if it might bite.

Inside, in large shaky capitals:

DEAR MOTH RADIO,
I AM 81. I DON'T SLEEP SINCE MY WIFE DIED. I HEARD YOU ON MY OLD WIRELESS BY ACCIDENT, LOOKING FOR THE CRICKET. YOU SAID "IF YOU'RE AWAKE YOU'RE NOT THE ONLY ONE." I WROTE IT DOWN.
WHAT IS THE WEATHER LIKE OVER THE SEA TONIGHT.
YOURS,
ALBERT (BADGER), FERRY ROAD.

That night, at two o'clock, Wren sat down at the taped-up microphone and found her paws were shaking.

"Good morning," she said. "Or good night. This is Moth Radio. It's two o'clock. And tonight… tonight we have a letter. A real one."

She read it out. She read it twice. Then she looked out of the window at the black line of the sea.

"Albert," she said. "Out over the sea tonight it's very dark and very calm. The tide's going out. There's one boat, way out, with a single green light on it, going somewhere slowly. There's a light chance of whales. If I were you, I'd make a cup of something and sit by the window, and I'll sit by mine, and we'll watch the green light until it's gone."

She did. For eleven minutes, she said nothing at all into the microphone, and the transmitter hummed, and the green light crept across the sea and went over the edge of the world.

"There," she said softly. "Gone. Safe, probably. Goodnight, Albert."


FOUR

After that, the letters came.

Not many, at first. One a week. Then two. Then a handful every morning, pushed under the tower door: brown envelopes and lined paper and the backs of shopping lists and once a paper napkin from the all-night garage.

DEAR MOTH RADIO, I'M A NURSE ON NIGHTS AT THE COTTAGE HOSPITAL. THE PATIENTS LIKE THE STARS. COULD YOU DO MORE STARS.

DEAR MOTH RADIO, I'M A BABY'S FATHER. SHE WAKES AT THREE. I PUT YOU ON. SHE GOES QUIET WHEN YOU DO THE WHALES.

DEAR MOTH RADIO, I'M THE LAMP-POST ON FERRY ROAD. NOT REALLY. I'M ALBERT'S NEIGHBOUR. HE TOLD ME. I WORK AT THE BAKERY, START AT FOUR. I LISTEN WHILE THE DOUGH RISES.

DEAR MOTH RADIO, I'M SEVENTEEN AND I CAN'T TELL ANYONE WHY I CAN'T SLEEP. PLEASE JUST KEEP TALKING.

She read every one. She read them all on air, without names when they asked, and she answered them, and she did more stars, and more whales, and she began each night by saying, "If you're awake, you're not the only one," and now, for the first time, she knew it was true.

She still didn't sleep.

But the nights had changed shape. They weren't a hole any more. They were a room, and it had people in it.


FIVE

In February, the transmitter broke.

It happened in the middle of the whales. There was a smell like burning toast, the orange light flickered and went out, and the Belgian part made a long, low, final noise, like a sigh at the end of a very long life.

Wren sat in the dark, in the cold, with the dead microphone in her paws.

She thought about Albert, sitting by his window, and the nurse, and the baby, and the bakery dough rising in the dark, and the seventeen-year-old who couldn't tell anyone. All of them, at this exact minute, turning a dial and finding only hiss.

She did not cry. She was too busy being afraid.

She climbed down the ladder and walked through the frozen town in her dressing gown, and she did not really know where she was going until she got there: Ferry Road, a small house with one light on in the downstairs window.

She knocked.

Albert opened the door in a cardigan with a teapot in his paw. He was very old and very large and he looked at her for a long moment, and then his face did something slow and astonished.

"You're the radio," he said.

"It broke," said Wren, and then, to her horror, she did cry.


SIX

Albert had been an electrician for fifty-one years.

He did not say so. He just put his coat on over his pyjamas, and found a torch, and a toolbox that weighed more than Wren, and walked with her back through the frozen streets to the tower; and on the way, the bakery's back door opened and a hare in a floury apron came out with a box of hot rolls and said, "Is that her?" and fell into step; and outside the cottage hospital, a nurse on her break looked up from her flask and said, "Is the show off? Oh. Oh, is that her?" and came too.

By the time they reached the water tower, there were eleven of them.

They could not all fit in the tin room. So they stood on the ladder, and in the stairwell, and outside in the frost, passing the hot rolls up and down, while Albert lay on his back under the transmitter with a torch in his mouth and said things like "Hm," and "Ah," and, once, with great feeling, "Belgium."

At half past four, the orange light came on.

A cheer went down the ladder and out into the street, a cheer from eleven people who did not sleep, at half past four in the morning, in February.

Mrs. Patel, the moth, landed on the light as if nothing had happened.

Wren sat down at the microphone. Her paws were black with grease. Her face was wet. Behind her, in the doorway, Albert leaned on his toolbox, breathing hard, smiling with his whole old face.

"Good morning," she said, into the night. "Or good night. Sorry about that. We had a little trouble. This is Moth Radio."

She looked at the faces in the doorway, and on the ladder, and out of the window, in the street below, looking up.

"If you're awake," she said, "you're really, really not the only one."


SEVEN

Moth Radio still goes out, most nights, at two o'clock.

It has a proper aerial now, which the bakery paid for, and a microphone that isn't taped together, which the cottage hospital donated, and the Belgian part, which nobody has ever dared to replace. It has a guest on Thursdays: an old badger from Ferry Road who explains how to fix things, slowly, in a voice like a warm blanket.

Wren still doesn't sleep much.

But sometimes, at the very end of the show, at four o'clock, with the sky going grey and the bakery lights coming on down the hill and a moth on the orange light, she finds that her eyes have closed by themselves, just for a moment, in the middle of the whales.

When that happens, the listeners don't mind. They just leave the radio on, and listen to the hum.


THE END
