BLACKWOOD UNIVERSITY

EPISODE TWENTY-TWO: “A CHAIR FOR YOU”

Previously: The last hundred and forty-four humans voted to answer Yuki’s question. Simone, who locked the door on her own family at the end of the universe, wrote four words on the glass: please, come in, sit. Yuki put one foot on a staircase that wasn’t there, and it held.

Content warning: Rated R. Strong language, grief, a negotiation with the end of the world, a very small girl in a very dark room, and a woman who finally says sorry.

TEASER

INT. THE STAIRCASE — NO TIME

A staircase of white stone climbing through a sky that is not a sky. No railing. No wall. On either side, nothing: not dark, not light, just an absence politely arranged around the steps.

Yuki climbs.

Grey cardigan, scuffed shoes, the scythe in her right hand like a walking stick. She has the file under her arm and Imogen’s letter folded inside it. She has been climbing for what feels like an hour and might be a minute.

On each step, a figure sits in a robe. They do not speak. They do not look at her. They sit with their hands in their laps and their faces turned slightly away, like people at a funeral who have decided to let a stranger pass.

YUKI
(to the first, quietly)
Sorry. Excuse me.

The figure does not answer. Its face, beneath the hood, is a thumbprint of light.

YUKI (CONT’D)
(climbing on)
Sorry. Sorry. Excuse me.

SCYTHE
(muttering, from her grip)
You apologise to furniture.

YUKI
They’re not furniture.

SCYTHE
They’re a hundred and forty-three people who put a child in a coat.

YUKI
(climbing)
They’re also a hundred and forty-three people who wrote please.

SCYTHE
(grudging)
That’s a point.

YUKI
(a dry breath)
Don’t spoil it.

She climbs. The air thins. Somewhere far below, a sound like surf, which is the sound of a hundred and four thousand people sleeping.

She stops.

YUKI (CONT’D)
(looking at her hands)
I’m shaking.

SCYTHE
You’re dead. You don’t shake.

YUKI
I’m shaking.

SCYTHE
(after a pause, gently)
Yes. You are.

She takes a breath. She climbs on. At the top, a door: plain, white, wooden, with a brass knocker in the shape of a small hand. Under it, a card in a neat, careful, official hand.

PLEASE KNOCK.

Yuki looks at the card for a long time.

She knocks.

ACT ONE

INT. THE ARK — THE ROUND ROOM — “MORNING”

The door opens inward on a circular room with a domed ceiling, a hundred and forty-four mismatched chairs in rings, and a plain table in the middle. The light is gold and a little too warm, the way a room is when someone has turned the heating up for a guest.

Simone is standing at the table, in her sister’s grey coat, with her hands clasped in front of her like a woman at a bus stop.

SIMONE
(rather stiffly)
Hello.

YUKI
(in the doorway)
Hi.

A silence. They look at each other. Behind Simone, a hundred and forty-three people sit very straight in their chairs and try to look as though they have not been practising their expressions in a mirror.

SIMONE
You came.

YUKI
You said there was a chair.

SIMONE
(a little helplessly)
There is.

She gestures. On the inner ring, one chair stands empty, with a cushion. Her own grey coat has been folded neatly on the seat. Simone crosses, lifts the coat, and holds it for a second against her chest before she lays it over her arm.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(quietly)
It’s been empty for a while.

YUKI
(carefully)
Whose is it?

SIMONE
(not looking at her)
Yours. If you’d like it.

Yuki walks in. Her shoes make a small sound on the floor. A hundred and forty-three people breathe in at once.

She stops beside the chair. She looks at it. She looks at Simone.

YUKI
(quietly)
Imogen’s?

Simone’s face does something quick and terrible.

SIMONE
(barely audible)
You read the letter.

YUKI
Yes.

SIMONE
(a whisper)
Then you know what it says.

YUKI
(a nod)
It says ask them.

She sits.

It is a very ordinary chair. It creaks a little. The cushion is thin and slightly lumpy. Yuki settles into it with her shoulders up around her ears, holding the scythe across her lap like a handbag.

SIMONE
(oddly formal)
Would you like tea?

YUKI
(startled)
What?

SIMONE
We have tea. It’s not very good.

A woman in a faded pink cardigan, from the third ring, cries out in a high, bright, wavering voice:

PEONY
It’s dreadful!

Somebody shushes her. Somebody else starts, helplessly, to laugh. And in that laugh, small and cracked and unfair, Yuki feels the room unclench a quarter of an inch.

YUKI
(a faint smile)
I’d love some.

Marisol hurries out, and comes back with a battered enamel pot and a tray of mismatched mugs. She pours a mug for Yuki with elaborate care, hands trembling, and sets it in front of her. Yuki takes it. It is hot. It is, genuinely, undrinkable.

She drinks it anyway.

YUKI (CONT’D)
(swallowing)
Thank you.

MARISOL
(a squeak)
You’re welcome.

Yuki sets down the mug. She puts both hands flat on the table. She looks around the rings: at the surgeon with her arms folded, at the priest with his head bowed, at the very old woman asleep in a wheelchair, at the boy in the huge tracksuit who is staring at her like she is the first thing he has ever seen.

YUKI
(quietly)
You asked me in.

SIMONE
(sitting, finally, in the chair beside hers)
We did.

YUKI
You want something.

SIMONE
(a long breath)
We want a great many things.

YUKI
(dryly)
That’s what I said to the Assignor. It said you’d tell me.

SIMONE
(a thin smile)
It says that about a lot of things.

YUKI
(gently, not smiling)
Tell me.

Simone folds her hands.

SIMONE
(carefully)
I’d like to start with the part you won’t like.

YUKI
(steady)
All right.

SIMONE
We’re going to tell you everything. We’re going to tell you what we are, what we did, why. We’re going to tell you the number. We’re not going to leave anything out to make ourselves look better.

YUKI
(quietly)
Okay.

SIMONE
(her voice low)
And then we’re going to ask you for something we have no right to ask.

YUKI
(after a moment)
You’d better start.

SIMONE
(nodding)
Kwabena.

Kwabena, from the second ring, stands. He straightens his dark suit, adjusts his cuffs, and speaks in the grave, clear, exact voice of a man giving evidence.

KWABENA
Miss Tanaka. There is a warmth in the dead.

INT. THE ARK — THE ROUND ROOM — A LITTLE LATER

A tight shot on Yuki’s face. The telling goes on over the picture, in several voices, passed round the room like a collection plate. We see her listening. We see her hands, flat on the table, whiten at the knuckles.

KWABENA (V.O.)
The warmth is grief. The larger the pain, the greater the yield.

HALVORSEN (V.O.)
(clipped)
We built the school to keep the dead hopeful, so they would linger.

ANSELM (V.O.)
(hoarse)
And we taught them to be afraid of losing it.

TWINS (V.O.)
(together)
And we picked the ones who’d suffered most to carry the scythe.

OLD RUTH (V.O.)
(cheerfully)
Because the ones who’ve been there know how to talk to the ones who are going.

SIMONE (V.O.)
(very quietly)
And because they would never be able to leave.

Yuki’s eyes close.

Her lips move, very slightly. We cannot hear what she says.

KWABENA (V.O.)
There are a hundred and four thousand of them now. We have thirty-eight days.

Yuki opens her eyes.

YUKI
(a whisper)
What happens in thirty-eight days?

A pause. All around the rings, faces turn to Simone.

SIMONE
(carefully)
We go out.

YUKI
(not understanding)
Out?

SIMONE
(steadily)
The Ark goes dark. All of us die. Properly. Not as I did, not as Imogen did, with the door waiting. There is nothing after us. The door’s only open because of the warmth.

Yuki stares at her.

YUKI
(slowly)
So you can’t stop.

SIMONE
No.

YUKI
(a whisper)
And you can’t keep going.

SIMONE
(very softly)
No.

YUKI
(her voice rising, a girl’s voice, furious and frightened)
Then what the hell do you want me to do?

A very long silence. The room holds its breath.

Simone leans forward.

SIMONE
(quietly)
Ask them.

YUKI
(blinking)
What?

SIMONE
The hundred and four thousand. Not us. Them. We’ve never asked them. We built them a world, we filled it with parties and cupcakes and friendship and a cheerful old woman with a coat, and we never once told them what it was for.

She spreads her hands.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(very low)
We’d like you to tell them.

YUKI
(stunned)
Tell them what?

SIMONE
(a thin, ruined smile)
Everything. Tell them they’re dead, tell them what the warmth is, tell them what we took. And then ask them, exactly as you asked us, what they want.

YUKI
(a whisper)
They’ll run.

SIMONE
(steadily)
Some of them.

YUKI
They’ll panic.

SIMONE
Yes.

YUKI
(her voice cracking)
They’ll hate you.

SIMONE
(softly)
I hope so.

Yuki stares at her.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(gently)
It would mean they were still people.

Behind Simone, Dr. Halvorsen has risen to her feet.

DR. HALVORSEN
(sharply)
This is not what we agreed.

SIMONE
(without turning)
It’s exactly what we agreed. Three hundred and sixty-two assemblies. I’ve read the minutes.

DR. HALVORSEN
(through her teeth)
We agreed to answer the Reaper’s question. We did not agree to hand a hundred and four thousand souls the keys to the engine.

SIMONE
(a flicker of the old steel)
It isn’t our engine, Helen. It never was. It’s theirs.

DR. HALVORSEN
(furious)
They’ll turn it off!

SIMONE
(quietly)
Possibly.

DR. HALVORSEN
(a snarl)
And we’ll die!

SIMONE
(very gently, finally turning to look at her)
Helen. We’re already dead.

The room goes entirely silent.

Dr. Halvorsen opens her mouth. Shuts it. Her eyes are wild. Her hands, at her sides, are shaking.

DR. HALVORSEN
(a whisper)
I’m not ready.

SIMONE
(stepping toward her)
I know.

DR. HALVORSEN
(cracking)
I’m not ready to be nothing.

SIMONE
(her own voice trembling)
I know.

DR. HALVORSEN
(a harsh, wounded breath)
I have a daughter.

SIMONE
(very quietly)
That’s why.

She holds out her hand.

For a long moment Dr. Halvorsen does not take it. Then, as if lifting a great weight, she does.

END OF ACT ONE

ACT TWO

INT. THE ARK — THE LATTICE — LATER

The cathedral of lamps. A hundred and four thousand points of light on walls that rise out of sight. The pillar of the heart at the centre, glowing, pulsing, very slightly dimmer than it was yesterday.

Yuki stands at the foot of the pillar with Simone and Dr. Halvorsen. Behind them, a respectful distance back, the rest of the Ark has gathered in the great doorway, in their patched coats, watching.

YUKI
(looking up, her voice small)
That’s all of them?

SIMONE
That’s all of them.

YUKI
(a whisper)
Which one is Lux?

Simone points.

A lamp near the top of the vault, white and gold, burning very strong. Beside it, close, a second lamp, black-edged and silver. They burn so close they have begun to merge.

YUKI (CONT’D)
(quietly)
Nine lives?

SIMONE
Nine.

YUKI
They never remembered.

SIMONE
They never remembered. They just kept… finding each other.

YUKI
(an unsteady smile)
That sounds like her.

SIMONE
(softly)
It sounds like both of them.

Yuki looks at the pair for a long time. Then she lowers her eyes along the walls, searching, row on row, down through the great cold constellation.

YUKI
(a whisper)
Is Madison there?

SIMONE
(a pause)
Yes.

Her finger moves. A small, tremulous lamp, a pale pink, far down the wall, almost out.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(gently)
She’s very bright. She’s been very bright.

YUKI
(her eyes stinging)
She’s almost gone.

SIMONE
(very quietly)
Ena killed her.

Silence.

YUKI
(a whisper)
I know.

SIMONE
(carefully)
We didn’t ask her to. We never ask anyone to. But a very frightened girl who’s been told she’s going to lose the only thing she loves will do the worst thing a person can, and the Ark burns very bright on the worst things.

YUKI
(her voice breaking)
So you let her.

SIMONE
(without flinching)
We let her.

YUKI
(a sob)
You just let it happen.

SIMONE
(her voice low)
Yes. And I would like, before this is over, for every single person in this room to say that sentence out loud, in front of you, with their own mouth. Because I don’t believe any of us has said it yet.

She turns.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(to the doorway, clearly)
Come in.

They come in. A hundred and forty-two of them, a little at a time, shuffling, shy, ashamed. They cluster around the foot of the pillar in a ragged half-circle, looking at their shoes. At the front: the twins, hand in hand, and Marisol, and Kwabena with his hat in his hands, and Anselm, and Old Ruth in her chair, and Tobias in his huge tracksuit.

Simone steps to the centre.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(quietly)
We let it happen.

Silence.

Then, behind her, a voice: Kwabena, dry, upright.

KWABENA
We let it happen.

The twins, together, in a whisper:

TWINS
We let it happen.

MARISOL
(a squeak)
We let it happen.

ANSELM
(hoarse)
We let it happen.

OLD RUTH
(very clearly)
We let it happen.

Round the circle it goes, one voice, then another, then twenty at a time, then all of them: a great sad, murmured, ragged liturgy, a hundred and forty-three people at the foot of the engine, saying the sentence one at a time, each in their own words, each with their own face.

We let it happen. I let it happen. I knew, and I let it happen. I looked away. I was warm.

Yuki stands in the middle of it with her arms wrapped round herself, shaking, and listens.

When it dies away, she is crying.

YUKI
(very low)
I can’t forgive you.

SIMONE
(nodding)
No.

YUKI
(wiping her face on her sleeve)
I don’t think anybody can.

SIMONE
(softly)
I don’t think anybody should.

YUKI
(a wet, shaky laugh)
That’s a terrible thing to say.

SIMONE
It’s an honest one.

YUKI
(after a long moment)
But I can listen.

Simone closes her eyes.

SIMONE
(whispering)
Thank you.

YUKI
(wiping her nose)
I mean it. I’m not saying yes. I’m saying I’ll listen. And then I’ll go and ask them.

SIMONE
(eyes still closed)
That’s all we’d ever have asked.

YUKI
(a small, raw smile)
That’s not all. You said you’d want something you’ve no right to ask.

Simone opens her eyes.

SIMONE
(quietly)
Yes.

YUKI
What is it?

Simone turns to Dr. Halvorsen. Helen, beside her, is very pale. She is holding something in her hands: a small thing wrapped in cloth.

DR. HALVORSEN
(hoarsely)
I would like you to take this.

She unwraps it.

A child’s drawing in crayon. A house, a yellow sun, a dog with too many legs. The paper has gone soft and brown. At the bottom, in a seven-year-old’s capitals: SOFIA H.

YUKI
(a whisper)
Oh.

DR. HALVORSEN
(a rough, flat voice)
She is in the first room. The one I made. I’d like you to bring her to the field. I’d like you to open the door, and let her out, and let her have the dog.

YUKI
(stunned)
Can I?

DR. HALVORSEN
(carefully)
You have the scythe.

YUKI
(looking down at it)
I thought that was for cutting.

DR. HALVORSEN
(a dry, awful breath)
It’s for opening. Everyone has always forgotten that.

The scythe, in Yuki’s hand, gives a small, nervous cough.

SCYTHE
(embarrassed)
It’s true. It’s the oldest function. The harvest was a later improvisation.

YUKI
(blinking)
You never said.

SCYTHE
You never asked.

YUKI
(a laugh like a sob)
I’m going to murder you.

SCYTHE
(with dignity)
I am a scythe.

Yuki takes the drawing. She looks at it. She looks at the doctor.

YUKI
(gently)
Where is she?

DR. HALVORSEN
(a whisper)
Down.

INT. THE FIRST ROOM — NO TIME

A very small, very dark room. Four walls. No door. No window. A mattress on the floor. A tin plate. A stuffed rabbit with one ear.

On the mattress, in the dark, a child.

She is seven. She wears a faded pyjama top, much too big. Her knees are drawn up to her chin and her arms are wrapped round them, and she is not crying. She stopped crying some time in the second century. She is simply sitting, very straight, staring at the wall, in the grey, flat, absolute calm of someone who has learned that nobody is going to come.

In the wall, a crack. A thin vertical line of gold.

The child does not look at it. She has seen it before. It has been a game it plays, every few years: a sliver of light, a rumour of a door. She knows better.

The crack widens.

A hand comes through, a girl’s hand, small, dark, trembling, holding a scythe by the shaft. Then an arm. Then a face: a girl in a grey cardigan, wet-eyed, breathless, looking round the tiny room as though she has walked into the worst thing she has ever seen.

The child turns her head.

SOFIA
(in a very small, level voice)
Are you the next one?

Yuki can’t speak.

SOFIA (CONT’D)
(patiently, like an old woman talking to a young one)
The next one, who comes to look. They come sometimes. They look through the door. They never come in.

YUKI
(finding her voice)
I’m coming in.

SOFIA
(flatly)
That’s what the last one said.

YUKI
(crossing the room on her knees, slowly, as to a frightened animal)
Sofia.

Sofia’s eyes move to her face.

SOFIA
(suspicious)
How do you know my name?

YUKI
(very gently)
Your mum told me.

Sofia’s composure cracks at the corners. It is as though a very fine glaze has been scored down the middle.

SOFIA
(a whisper)
She’s not here.

YUKI
She’s upstairs.

SOFIA
(very small)
She’s a long way away.

YUKI
(her voice shaking)
She sent this.

She holds out the drawing.

Sofia stares at it. A house. A sun. A dog with too many legs. The room is very quiet. The stuffed rabbit, on the mattress, with its one ear, looks on.

Sofia puts out one small finger and touches the dog.

SOFIA
(a whisper)
That’s Biscuit.

YUKI
(trying to smile)
Is it?

SOFIA
(her lip trembling)
He’s got eight legs, because I couldn’t do four.

YUKI
(her own face wrecked)
He looks very good.

SOFIA
(shakily, trying to be brave)
He was the best dog.

And then it comes: the whole seven-year-old, all of it, six hundred years, the crack running right through. She drops the drawing and throws herself at Yuki with her whole thin body, and Yuki catches her, scythe and all, and holds her.

Sofia sobs. She sobs like a little girl. She sobs as though she has been saving it for six hundred years.

SOFIA (CONT’D)
(into Yuki’s cardigan, in pieces)
I was very good. I was very good. I didn’t make any noise.

YUKI
(her face in the child’s hair, whispering)
I know.

SOFIA
(hiccuping, muffled)
I thought if I was good she’d come.

YUKI
(her eyes shut tight)
She’s coming. She’s coming right now.

SOFIA
(clinging)
Promise?

YUKI
(a whisper)
I promise.

And she lifts the scythe, one-handed, over the child’s head, and the wall behind them tears open from floor to ceiling in a long, bright, golden seam, and through it: a field. A real field, green, windy, with long grass and a big blue sky, and a small brown dog with eight rather unlikely legs, galloping toward them, barking, tail whipping, ears flying, with all the pent-up joy of five hundred and ninety-eight years.

Sofia turns.

SOFIA
(a shriek)
BISCUIT!

She is gone, out of Yuki’s arms, across the grass, a small girl in a very large pyjama top running with her arms spread wide, and the dog meets her halfway and knocks her flat in the sun, and she is laughing, and weeping, and laughing, rolling on the grass with her arms round his neck.

Yuki kneels in the doorway, on the cold floor of the dark room, in the light from the field, with her hand over her mouth, and weeps.

INT. THE ARK — THE LATTICE — MOMENTS LATER

At the foot of the pillar, Dr. Halvorsen has dropped to her knees.

On the wall, the first lamp, an amber one, small, steady, five hundred and ninety-eight years old, goes out.

In its place, very faint, a new light: not a flame. A glow, a faint warm gleam of an entirely different colour, a soft pale green like sun through leaves.

Helen looks up at it. Her mouth is open. She does not seem to be breathing.

DR. HALVORSEN
(barely audible)
Oh.

Simone, beside her, has taken her hand again.

SIMONE
(a whisper)
She’s all right.

DR. HALVORSEN
(a harsh, shaking inhale)
She’s with the dog.

SIMONE
(her own eyes full)
She’s with the dog.

Helen bows her head over her knees. For a long time she does not move. She does not make a sound. She just kneels there at the foot of the engine of the dead, with a child’s drawing crushed in her hand, and trembles.

Then she says, in a voice so small and clear that it carries to the edge of the vault:

DR. HALVORSEN
(whispering)
I’m sorry.

It echoes. It goes up through the lamps. A hundred and forty-two people hear it, and not one of them moves.

DR. HALVORSEN (CONT’D)
(a sob)
I’m so sorry. I’m so sorry. I’m so sorry.

Simone puts both arms round her and holds her while she says it, a hundred times, a thousand, like a prayer, like a heartbeat, and does not tell her to stop.

END OF ACT TWO

ACT THREE

INT. BLACKWOOD — THE COMMON ROOM — “DAY”

The great hall, full of the sleeping dead. A hundred and four thousand people on mattresses and sofas and floor, in pyjamas and prom dresses and cardigans, with the grey light going gold across their faces.

The door opens.

Yuki comes in. Her eyes are red and her cardigan is wet and there is a streak of grass on one knee. In her left hand the scythe, humming softly; in her right, a very old, very small, faded drawing of a dog.

Behind her, in the doorway, a hundred and forty-three people in ragged coats, who have not stood in a living room in six hundred years, stop dead and gaze at the sleepers as though at a nativity.

Yuki crosses the floor between the sleeping bodies, stepping over arms and legs, past a very small sleeping Sasha, to a pale girl curled up on a sofa under a blanket.

Lux.

She is sleeping on her side, one hand under her cheek, hair in her eyes. Beside her, on the floor, wrapped round her like a guard dog, in black, boots still on, Velvette sleeps with her head on Lux’s hip and one hand clamped round her wrist.

Yuki kneels.

YUKI
(very quietly)
Lux.

Nothing.

YUKI (CONT’D)
(softer)
Lux. It’s me.

Lux stirs. One eye opens. It is the pale, perfect, sceptical blue of a woman who has been asleep for a hundred years and resents being woken by anyone who isn’t at least a head of state.

LUX
(a croak)
Darling.

YUKI
(a wet laugh)
Hi.

LUX
(her eyes drifting closed again)
You look dreadful.

YUKI
(a sob that is also a laugh)
I know.

LUX
(sleepily)
Have you been crying?

YUKI
(wiping her nose)
A bit.

LUX
(a breath)
Good. It’s very flattering on you. Come here.

She lifts one arm from under the blanket, and Yuki falls into it, face first, and Lux holds her with a slowness that is almost shy.

LUX (CONT’D)
(into her hair, drowsy)
You’re going to tell me something awful.

YUKI
(muffled)
Yes.

LUX
(fondly)
I knew it. It’s always something awful. I could tell by the way you came in. You walked like a person carrying a very large lasagne.

YUKI
(a hiccup)
It’s about the school.

LUX
(a yawn)
It’s always about the school.

YUKI
It’s about all of us.

LUX
(a long pause)
Ah.

She opens her eyes.

LUX (CONT’D)
(very quietly, entirely awake)
Then you’d better say it properly.

At the foot of the sofa, Velvette has lifted her head, boots and all, and is looking at Yuki over the arm. Her eyes are sharp and black and very awake.

VELVETTE
(a low, flat voice)
Is it the thing with the sky?

YUKI
(startled)
You knew?

VELVETTE
(a shrug)
I’ve been watching it for a week. It cracks every time someone cries.

YUKI
(a breath)
You didn’t say.

VELVETTE
(dry)
Nobody asked.

Yuki closes her eyes. Opens them. Looks at the two of them, at the pale head on the pillow and the black one at her hip.

YUKI
(a whisper)
It’s a very long story.

LUX
(gently)
We’ve got time.

Yuki looks at her.

YUKI
(a ragged laugh)
Thirty-eight days.

LUX
(blinking)
Is that a lot?

YUKI
(her voice cracking)
I don’t know.

LUX
(very softly)
Then we’d better start.

She sits up, Velvette’s hand still round her wrist. She looks across the great hall, at the hundred and four thousand sleepers, and then, at last, at the doorway, at the hundred and forty-three silent strangers in their ragged coats.

LUX (CONT’D)
(her eyes narrowing)
And who are they?

Yuki looks round.

At the front of the group, in the doorway, in a grey coat with a missing button, an old, tired woman is standing with her hands clasped. She is looking at Lux with the terrible naked longing of someone who has watched a candle for six hundred years and is finally in the same room as the flame.

YUKI
(carefully)
They’re… the ones who’ve been looking after us.

LUX
(coolly)
Are they.

YUKI
(a breath)
Sort of.

LUX
(taking in the coat, the face, the missing button, every detail, with a glance that could strip varnish)
Mm.

Simone steps forward, one pace. She does not seem to know what to do with her hands.

SIMONE
(very low)
Hello, Lux.

LUX
(coolly, tilting her head)
We’ve met.

SIMONE
(a whisper)
Have we?

LUX
(a little smile that does not reach her eyes)
Nine times, apparently. I keep dreaming of a grey coat.

Simone’s face breaks.

SIMONE
(barely audible)
Oh.

LUX
(softly, merciless, curious)
Tell me, darling. Is it true that I’ve been loved by the same woman for nine hundred years?

SIMONE
(her mouth working)
I… yes.

LUX
(very pleasantly)
Wonderful. Then you can tell me, since you’re here and you’ve clearly been very interested in my private life.

She swings her feet off the sofa.

LUX (CONT’D)
(clear, cold, exact)
Who the hell are you, and what have you done to my school?

A silence you could stand a spoon in.

Simone opens her mouth. Closes it. And then, to the astonishment of a hundred and forty-two other people, the oldest woman in the universe laughs, helplessly, with her whole face, tears running down it.

SIMONE
(through the laugh)
Oh, you’re going to be a nightmare.

LUX
(coolly)
I’m a delight.

VELVETTE
(from the floor, deadpan)
She’s a nightmare.

LUX
(not looking down)
Thank you, darling.

SIMONE
(wiping her eyes)
I’m so sorry. I’ve waited a very long time to meet you.

LUX
(with immense hauteur)
Then you won’t mind if I put some shoes on first.

END OF EPISODE TWENTY-TWO.

EXT. A FIELD — NO TIME

Wind in tall grass. A very large blue sky. A little girl in an enormous pyjama top is lying on her back on the grass with her arms flung out, laughing at the clouds, while a small brown dog with eight rather unlikely legs sits on her chest and licks her chin.

Beside them, a woman in a faded pink cardigan has appeared, from nowhere, cautiously, with a tray.

PEONY
(brightly, nervously)
I brought you a sandwich.

Sofia looks up.

SOFIA
(suspicious)
Who are you?

PEONY
(trying to smile, her eyes filling)
I used to hear you in the pipes.

Sofia considers this. Then, with great solemnity, she holds out one small hand for the sandwich.

SOFIA
(chewing, seriously)
I knew somebody was out there.

Peony sits down on the grass. She is shaking. The dog nudges her hand. She puts her other hand over her mouth.

PEONY
(a whisper)
Oh, you’re so brave.

SOFIA
(with her mouth full)
It was fine.

PEONY
(softly)
Was it?

Sofia stops chewing.

She looks at the sky.

SOFIA
(finally, very small, very honest)
No.

PEONY
(gently)
No.

SOFIA
(a long breath)
But it’s better now.

PEONY
(a sob that is also a laugh)
Yes.

The dog flops down between them with a groan. Sofia rests her head against him, one small arm round his neck. Above them, the sky goes on, very large, very blue, and in the corner of it, almost too faint to see, in the daylight, for the first time in six hundred years, a single pale, patient, impossible point of white.

SLOW FADE.

END.
