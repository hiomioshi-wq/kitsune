KEVIN SLIDES
A BLACKWOOD UNIVERSITY SPINOFF

EPISODE FOUR: “FORWARDING ADDRESS”

TEASER

INT. MORTALITY SERVICES — THE LOBBY — NIGHT

The building is exactly the way Kevin left it, which is the first thing that is wrong.

The same beige carpet. The same stained ceiling tile shaped like Ohio. The same motivational poster of a kitten hanging off a branch, caption: HANG IN THERE, THEY’LL RESTRUCTURE EVENTUALLY. The same dead ficus in the corner, which Kevin once watered for six months before learning it was plastic.

The fluorescent lights flicker. Somewhere, a photocopier is dying with great dignity.

Kevin and Kevina land on the carpet in a heap. The Parcel bounces once and rolls to a stop against the reception desk.

KEVIN
(face in the carpet)
Ow.

KEVINA
(floating up, mop first)
I’m already dead. I don’t even get to say ow.

KEVIN
(rolling over)
Say it anyway.

KEVINA
(flatly)
Ow.

They lie there a moment, staring up at the ceiling tile shaped like Ohio.

KEVIN
(quietly)
It’s the same.

KEVINA
(looking around)
It’s very beige.

KEVIN
(sitting up)
No, I mean it’s exactly the same. Nobody’s moved anything. The coffee ring is still on the desk.

He points. On the reception desk, a brown ring, shaped like a sad little planet.

KEVIN (CONT’D)
That was mine.

KEVINA
(carefully)
How long were you gone?

KEVIN
(slowly)
I don’t know. Weeks. Months. Time’s been doing a lot of jazz lately.

KEVINA
(gently)
And nobody cleaned it.

KEVIN
(a beat)
Nobody noticed I was gone.

A silence. The photocopier coughs. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe knocks twice, as if somebody on the other side were politely asking to be let in.

BOX
(small, from the floor)
Kevin?

KEVIN
(not looking)
Yeah.

BOX
(carefully)
The counter’s stopped.

Kevin looks down. The glowing numbers on the Parcel’s side have frozen at 00:09:41:12. The colour has drained from them to a dim, resigned grey.

KEVIN
(quietly)
It’s not a slide.

BOX
(whispering)
No.

KEVIN
(slowly)
It’s a stop.

BOX
(very softly)
This is where I get delivered.

Kevina floats closer, her translucent face unusually gentle.

KEVINA
(softly)
Who’s it for?

KEVIN
(taking a breath)
The Reaper.

KEVINA
(carefully)
Is she here?

KEVIN
(looking at the dark corridor beyond the reception desk, at the one door at the end with a strip of light underneath it)
I think she’s always here.

MAIN TITLES

ACT ONE

INT. MORTALITY SERVICES — THE LOBBY — CONTINUOUS

Kevin gets to his feet. His knees crack. He is wearing the same cardigan he left in, and it has acquired, over seven universes, a charming collection of stains: wine, ash, cupcake, something blue that he refuses to identify.

He picks up the Parcel. It is heavier than it was. Or he is lighter.

KEVINA
(floating beside him)
Do you want me to come?

KEVIN
(carefully)
Do you want to?

KEVINA
(shrugging, mop over her shoulder)
I’ve been following you across the multiverse for what feels like a very long time. I’d like to see how it ends.

KEVIN
(small smile)
It might end badly.

KEVINA
(matter-of-fact)
Everything ends badly. It’s the quality of the casserole that matters.

KEVIN
(laughing, startled)
You’ve been hanging around the Reaper’s mother too long.

KEVINA
(gravely)
She is a very formidable woman.

They start down the corridor. The carpet muffles their steps. The fluorescent tubes tick overhead like tiny, tired clocks.

Along the walls hang the same laminated notices Kevin used to ignore: FIRE EXITS ARE FOR EMERGENCIES ONLY. DEATH IS NOT AN EMERGENCY. PLEASE DO NOT MICROWAVE FISH. A new one, handwritten, in red marker, pinned crookedly to the corkboard: IF YOU ARE READING THIS AND KEVIN IS STILL MISSING, PLEASE RING THE NUMBER BELOW.

Underneath, a phone number. Underneath that, in a different handwriting, small and careful: PLEASE.

Kevin stops.

KEVIN
(very quietly)
Somebody put that up.

KEVINA
(reading)
“Please ring the number below.”

KEVIN
(touching the paper)
It’s Yuki’s handwriting.

KEVINA
(carefully)
Your Yuki.

KEVIN
(nodding)
My Yuki.

He peels the paper from the corkboard, folds it very carefully, and tucks it into the Parcel’s side pocket, which, he realises, the box has just grown for the purpose.

BOX
(muffled)
Thanks.

KEVIN
(patting it)
You’re welcome.

They continue down the corridor. The light at the end grows brighter. The Reaper’s door is slightly ajar. A thin line of gold spills across the carpet. From within, a sound: very faint, very rhythmic. A knife on a chopping board.

Chop. Chop. Chop.

KEVINA
(whispering)
Is that…

KEVIN
(listening)
Onions.

KEVINA
(whispering)
She’s cooking.

KEVIN
(slowly)
She doesn’t cook.

KEVINA
(whispering)
Somebody does.

Kevin puts a hand on the door. It is warm.

He pushes.

INT. THE REAPER’S OFFICE — CONTINUOUS

The office is not an office.

It is a kitchen.

A small, warm, impossibly tidy kitchen, with yellow wallpaper and copper pans and a window over the sink looking out onto a garden that is, for once, alive. Green. Honest-to-god green. A tomato plant. A cat asleep on the sill.

At the stove, her back to the door, stands an elderly woman in a floral apron, stirring a pot. She is small, round, with silver hair in a bun and the particular comfortable bulk of somebody who has fed a great many people. Beside her, on a chopping board, sits a heap of diced onion.

At the table, in a rocking chair, with her hood down and her scythe leaning against the wall, sits the Reaper.

She looks small. She looks tired. She looks, for the first time in Kevin’s experience, like somebody’s daughter.

She is holding a mug of tea in both hands.

She looks up.

REAPER
(very quietly)
Hello, Kevin.

KEVIN
(frozen in the doorway)
Hi.

REAPER
(carefully)
You’re late.

KEVIN
(helplessly)
I got held up.

REAPER
(a ghost of a smile)
By what?

KEVIN
(counting on fingers)
A graveyard of Kevins, a megachurch, the Hereafter Revenue Service, a suburban cul-de-sac with a flamethrower—

REAPER
(waving a hand)
Yes, yes. I’ve been reading your reviews.

KEVIN
(blinking)
There are reviews?

REAPER
(dryly)
One star. “Delivery guy keeps saying please. Very irritating. Would not die with again.”

KEVIN
(small)
That’s unfair.

REAPER
(mildly)
It’s Mother’s.

The old woman at the stove, without turning around, calls out in a warm, brisk, slightly Yorkshire voice.

MOTHER
Don’t stand in the door, love, you’re letting the heat out.

KEVIN
(automatically)
Sorry.

MOTHER
(turning, with a spoon, beaming)
And wipe your feet.

KEVIN
(obeying instantly)
Yes, ma’am.

MOTHER
(to Kevina, kindly)
And you must be the one with the mop.

KEVINA
(startled)
Yes?

MOTHER
(approving)
Good. Somebody ought to be.

She wipes her hands on her apron. She looks at Kevin. Her pale grey eyes are very bright, very sharp, and very, very old.

MOTHER (CONT’D)
Well. Go on. You’ve brought the post.

Kevin steps forward. The Parcel in his arms is trembling slightly.

KEVIN
(carefully)
It’s addressed to the Reaper.

MOTHER
(nodding)
I know. I wrote the address.

KEVIN
(slowly)
It’s been in the post since 1347.

MOTHER
(sighing)
There was a plague. They had a backlog.

KEVIN
(a faint, strangled laugh)
Of course there was.

REAPER
(quietly)
Mother.

MOTHER
(looking over)
Yes, love.

REAPER
(not meeting her eyes)
I don’t have to open it.

MOTHER
(gently)
No. You don’t.

REAPER
(rubbing her thumb along the rim of the mug)
I could just… leave it. On the shelf. With the others.

MOTHER
(a long beat)
Yes, love. You could.

A silence. Outside, the cat on the windowsill stretches, yawns, and settles again.

KEVIN
(carefully)
Others?

The Reaper points, without looking. On the far wall, a long pine shelf, crowded with boxes. Cardboard boxes. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Each wrapped in brown paper and tied with string, each stamped with a faded postmark, each addressed in the same small, rounded hand.

KEVIN (CONT’D)
(slowly)
She’s been sending them.

REAPER
(quietly)
For about seven hundred years.

KEVIN
(horrified)
And you haven’t opened any of them.

REAPER
(sharply)
They’re all casseroles.

MOTHER
(mildly)
They are not all casseroles.

REAPER
(a beat)
…Some of them are casseroles.

MOTHER
(patiently)
I put a note in each one.

REAPER
(flatly)
I know.

MOTHER
(gently)
You didn’t read them.

REAPER
(staring into the mug)
I didn’t want to know what they said.

A very long silence. Even the stove stops hissing.

Kevin sets the Parcel on the table between them, with an almost ceremonial care. The counter on its side flickers once, then settles to a quiet, steady gold.

BOX
(in a tiny voice)
I’m very sorry I took so long.

REAPER
(looking at it for the first time, startled)
You talk.

BOX
(embarrassed)
Only when people say please.

REAPER
(slowly)
Mother.

MOTHER
(innocently)
I did put some manners in the post.

ACT TWO

INT. THE REAPER’S KITCHEN — LATER

The pot has been moved to the table. It is a vast, bubbling, aromatic stew of onion and carrot and barley and something unidentifiable, and its smell has done something to the room, softened it. The yellow wallpaper seems warmer. The copper pans seem brighter.

Four chairs. Four bowls. Kevin, Kevina, the Reaper, and Mother, all seated around the old pine table, the Parcel in the centre like a centrepiece nobody has the courage to open.

Kevina is staring at her bowl.

KEVINA
(quietly)
I can’t eat this.

MOTHER
(kindly)
Of course you can, love.

KEVINA
(gesturing at herself, translucent)
I’m a ghost.

MOTHER
(spooning more stew into the bowl)
That’s no excuse.

KEVINA
(hesitating, lifting the spoon)
It’s very… I mean, I’ll fall through the table.

MOTHER
(firmly)
Not in this kitchen.

Kevina tries a spoonful. The spoon does not pass through her hand. The stew does not pass through her face. She swallows. Her translucent features go completely, wonderfully slack.

KEVINA
(whispering)
Oh my god.

MOTHER
(satisfied)
Told you.

KEVINA
(spooning more)
I haven’t eaten in nineteen years.

MOTHER
(patting her hand)
Eat, love. There’s plenty.

Kevin, watching, feels something in his throat give way.

KEVIN
(quietly, to the Reaper)
Where is this?

REAPER
(stirring her own bowl)
Home.

KEVIN
(carefully)
This isn’t your office.

REAPER
(shrugging)
It’s whatever Mother wants it to be.

KEVIN
(slowly)
She can do that?

REAPER
(with a thin, tired smile)
She’s the oldest mother in the world, Kevin. She can do whatever she likes.

MOTHER
(sharply)
Don’t exaggerate, dear.

REAPER
(dryly)
Yes, Mother.

MOTHER
(to Kevin, warmly)
Eat.

Kevin eats. It is, simply, the best thing he has ever tasted. It tastes of Sunday afternoons and childhood colds and every kind thing anyone has ever done for him that he was too tired to notice.

KEVIN
(quietly)
Oh.

MOTHER
(smiling)
There you go.

A silence, rich and warm. A few spoons clink. The cat on the sill opens one eye, evaluates the situation, and goes back to sleep.

KEVIN
(finally)
Can I ask something?

MOTHER
(nodding)
Go on.

KEVIN
(carefully)
Why did you send it by me?

MOTHER
(blinking)
Hm?

KEVIN
(gesturing at the Parcel)
There are hundreds on that shelf. Why was this one different?

The Reaper’s spoon stops halfway to her mouth.

MOTHER
(slowly, after a long breath)
Because it was the last.

REAPER
(sharply)
Mother.

MOTHER
(gently)
It was, love.

REAPER
(strained)
You can’t just—

MOTHER
(calmly)
I’ve been sending you a parcel every year since you took the job. I’ve been very patient. I’ve left you alone. I’ve let you hide in the office and the paperwork and the long dark hallways. I’ve let you be… efficient.

She says the word like a diagnosis.

MOTHER (CONT’D)
(softly)
But I’m an old woman. I’m not going to be here forever.

REAPER
(horrified)
You’re the Mother of Death.

MOTHER
(dryly)
And even I get a bad hip.

A silence. The Reaper has gone very white.

REAPER
(whispering)
You’re not… dying.

MOTHER
(very gently)
Everything does, love. Eventually. That’s rather the point of you.

REAPER
(her voice cracking)
I never wanted you to.

MOTHER
(patting her hand)
I know.

REAPER
(furious, helpless)
I stopped all of it. For you. I didn’t take the old. I didn’t take the sick. I let them pile up in the lobby. I let Kevin keep the paperwork so it wouldn’t reach you. I’ve been holding the line for seven hundred years.

KEVIN
(carefully)
Holding what line?

REAPER
(turning, bleak)
The one between you and the end.

MOTHER
(softly)
Oh, love.

REAPER
(fiercely)
Every parcel you sent, I thought: this is the one that says goodbye. This is the one with the note. So I didn’t open them. I put them on the shelf. And as long as they stayed shut—

She cannot finish. Her hand has gone to her mouth.

MOTHER
(quietly)
As long as they stayed shut, I couldn’t say it.

REAPER
(whispering)
Yes.

Kevin sits very still. He has just understood the entire multiverse, the Parcel, the slides, the six worlds, the mother with the tuna noodle bake, the wrong homes, the delivery, the long, bewildering, tender, unreasonable hunt.

KEVIN
(slowly)
She couldn’t open it.

REAPER
(not looking at him)
No.

KEVIN
(carefully)
So she sent it to me.

MOTHER
(gently)
I needed somebody who’d say please.

KEVIN
(small)
You could have said it yourself.

MOTHER
(a faint, rueful laugh)
I did, love. Seven hundred times. She didn’t answer.

The Reaper puts her face in her hands.

The kitchen is perfectly silent. The stew steams. The cat dreams. Outside, in the improbable garden, a single breeze moves the tomato plant, and the leaves whisper like a congregation.

KEVINA
(whispering, to Kevin)
Do something.

KEVIN
(whispering back)
What?

KEVINA
(urgent)
You’re the courier. Courier something.

Kevin takes a breath. He reaches across the table. He turns the Parcel so its label faces the Reaper. He says, as gently as he has ever said anything:

KEVIN
(softly)
Please.

The Reaper lowers her hands. Her eyes are red. Her face is wet. She looks at the Parcel, then at her mother, then at the Parcel again.

REAPER
(hoarse)
I’m scared.

MOTHER
(gently)
I know, love.

REAPER
(small, fierce)
I’m so fucking scared.

MOTHER
(smiling)
Language.

REAPER
(a wet, cracked laugh)
Sorry.

She wipes her face with the back of her wrist. She reaches out. Her long, pale fingers close around the string.

For a moment, nobody breathes.

She pulls.

The string comes loose.

The brown paper falls away.

The box, a plain, ordinary cardboard box, lifts its lid by itself.

Inside, nestled in a tea towel, is a casserole dish.

KEVINA
(whispering)
It is a casserole.

KEVIN
(whispering)
It’s tuna noodle bake.

REAPER
(staring)
It’s still warm.

MOTHER
(proudly)
Seven hundred years and not a degree lost.

The Reaper lifts the lid. Steam rises, rich and golden. And on top of the casserole, on a small square of paper, a note, written in the small, rounded hand she has avoided for seven centuries.

She picks it up. She unfolds it. Her lips move silently.

She stops. She reads it again.

REAPER
(in a very small voice)
It says…

She cannot finish. She hands it, shaking, to Kevin.

Kevin takes it. He reads.

On the paper, in blue ink, in a shaky, old, loving hand:

“Dear Child,

I’m not going anywhere. I only wanted you to eat.

Love, Mum.

P.S. There’s a spoon at the bottom.”

KEVIN
(laughing and crying at once)
That’s it?

MOTHER
(innocently)
What did you expect?

REAPER
(her face crumpling)
I thought it was goodbye.

MOTHER
(coming around the table, taking her in her arms)
Oh, you daft thing.

The Reaper, sixteen feet of cold dread in the minds of mortals everywhere, collapses into her mother’s apron and sobs like a child.

MOTHER (CONT’D)
(rocking her, patting her hair)
It was just dinner. It was only ever dinner.

Kevin and Kevina sit very still, trying not to look, looking anyway.

KEVINA
(whispering, wiping her eyes)
I think I’m going to cry.

KEVIN
(whispering back)
You’re translucent. You’ll leak.

KEVINA
(with great dignity)
I’ll mop it up.

ACT THREE

INT. THE REAPER’S KITCHEN — LATER

The stew is cold. The casserole has been cut. Four bowls, four spoons, one dish with a slightly burnt crust that the Reaper is eating directly with a fork, standing at the counter, like a teenager at midnight.

She has her hood down. Her hair, which Kevin has never seen, is long and dark and cut with the haphazard bluntness of somebody who has done it herself for several hundred years.

REAPER
(chewing)
This is excellent.

MOTHER
(proudly)
Of course it’s excellent. I made it.

REAPER
(swallowing)
I’m not going to apologise for the seven hundred years.

MOTHER
(mildly)
I didn’t ask you to.

REAPER
(pointing the fork)
But I’d like it noted that I was, in fact, being a martyr.

MOTHER
(dryly)
It was noted. About 1500.

KEVIN
(laughing quietly)
This is the nicest dinner I’ve had in months.

REAPER
(turning, a flicker of something like warmth)
Kevin.

KEVIN
(politely)
Yes?

REAPER
(carefully)
Thank you.

KEVIN
(a beat)
For what?

REAPER
(awkward, stiff)
For… the post.

KEVIN
(smiling)
It was very well packaged.

REAPER
(almost smiling)
It was. Even by Mother’s standards.

She puts down the fork. She looks at him for a long time.

REAPER (CONT’D)
(quietly)
I owe you a debt.

KEVIN
(shaking his head)
You don’t.

REAPER
(firmly)
I do. And I pay my debts.

KEVIN
(carefully)
What are you going to do?

REAPER
(a beat)
There’s one thing you want.

KEVIN
(guarded)
I want a lot of things.

REAPER
(dryly)
One thing in particular.

Kevin’s throat tightens. He knows. He has known the whole time.

KEVIN
(quietly)
Yuki.

REAPER
(nodding)
Yuki.

KEVIN
(slowly)
She’s not dead.

REAPER
(carefully)
No.

KEVIN
(an edge in his voice)
You told me she was.

REAPER
(evenly)
I told you she was gone.

KEVIN
(sharply)
That’s the same fucking thing.

REAPER
(mildly)
Is it?

Kevin opens his mouth. He closes it. He thinks of the Mortality Services Yuki in her coffee apron, the Revenue Service Yuki with her stamp, the megachurch Yuki with her priestly robes. Every one of them was kind. Every one of them was a little bit wrong.

KEVIN
(quietly)
She’s in the multiverse.

REAPER
(nodding)
She’s in every part of it.

KEVIN
(slowly)
She went through a slide.

REAPER
(softly)
She went through the Seam. When the Architect fell. She was holding it shut. And it took her.

KEVIN
(horrified)
She was—

REAPER
(gently)
She was scattered.

A silence. The cat on the sill has woken and is watching.

REAPER (CONT’D)
(carefully)
Every Yuki you’ve met. Every one. They’re her. They’re pieces of her. Not copies. Not echoes. Her.

KEVIN
(whispering)
All of them.

REAPER
(nodding)
She’s been trying to come home for as long as you’ve been trying to find her.

KEVIN
(faintly)
Why didn’t you tell me?

REAPER
(honestly)
Because I didn’t know how to fix it.

KEVIN
(bitter)
And now you do.

REAPER
(softly)
Now I have a courier.

She nods at the Parcel. The empty box has not gone dark. Its counter has restarted, slowly, with a new colour: not gold, not grey, but a warm, deep, rosy amber.

00:09:59:59

BOX
(whispering)
Hello again.

KEVIN
(slowly)
There’s another slide.

REAPER
(nodding)
There’s one more. The last. The Seam is calling it home.

KEVIN
(carefully)
Where does it go?

REAPER
(simply)
Everywhere at once.

KEVIN
(blinking)
That’s not a destination.

REAPER
(dryly)
It’s a delivery address. You’ll be fine.

KEVINA
(floating forward)
I’m coming.

KEVIN
(turning)
You don’t have to.

KEVINA
(firmly)
I’ve been a mop in the afterlife for nineteen years, Kevin. I’m not missing the finale.

KEVIN
(small, grateful)
Thank you.

KEVINA
(gruffly)
Don’t make it weird.

MOTHER
(from the stove, calling)
Take sandwiches!

KEVIN
(turning)
What?

MOTHER
(already wrapping them)
Cheese and pickle. You’ll want something in your stomach. Nobody ever slides on an empty stomach.

KEVIN
(taking the greaseproof packet)
Thank you, Mrs.…

MOTHER
(sharply)
Mum. You may call me Mum. Everyone does.

KEVIN
(small, warm)
Thank you, Mum.

MOTHER
(patting his cheek, with a hand like warm bread)
Go on, then. Go and fetch the girl.

ACT FOUR

INT. THE BEIGE VOID — NO TIME

Falling again. It is, Kevin reflects, becoming a lifestyle.

He is holding the Parcel in one arm and a cheese and pickle sandwich in the other. Kevina floats beside him, mop tucked under her elbow, chewing. Every few seconds a small, soft sound drifts up from the box: a tiny hum, a contented little purr.

KEVINA
(through a mouthful)
This is a great sandwich.

KEVIN
(also chewing)
It’s the pickle.

KEVINA
(solemnly)
I’m going to make a shrine to that pickle.

BOX
(sleepily)
Eat your crusts.

The beige void, for once, is not empty. Around them, drifting past like jellyfish in a slow current, are tall, glowing doorways. Hundreds of them. Each one open onto a different world. A coffee shop. A graveyard. A church. A desert road. A motel. A cul-de-sac. A lobby with a kitten poster.

KEVIN
(slowly)
That’s all of them.

KEVINA
(swallowing)
All what?

KEVIN
(pointing)
Every place we’ve been.

KEVINA
(looking)
Is that… do they all have somebody in them?

Kevin squints. In each doorway, far away, he can see a figure. Small. Distant. Dark hair, a long coat, a bag over one shoulder. A woman standing very still, looking out as if waiting for a train.

KEVIN
(hoarse)
Yuki.

BOX
(from his arm, gently)
That’s the address.

KEVIN
(not understanding)
What is?

BOX
(softly)
All of them. You don’t deliver to one place. You deliver to every place she is.

KEVINA
(frowning)
That’s a lot of doors.

BOX
(cheerfully)
It’s the biggest delivery in the history of the post.

KEVIN
(dryly)
Of course it is.

The counter on the Parcel starts to tick. 00:09:59:59. 00:09:59:58. Each tick makes a doorway brighten.

KEVIN (CONT’D)
(quietly)
Ten minutes.

KEVINA
(grimly)
Not much time to find hundreds of women.

KEVIN
(taking a breath)
I don’t have to find them.

KEVINA
(turning)
What do you mean?

KEVIN
(looking at the box, understanding)
I just have to ask.

He holds the Parcel out in front of him, like an offering. He closes his eyes. He thinks of the coffee shop in Blackwood, the quiet mornings, the sticky notes on the fridge, the way she used to wrinkle her nose when he said please, as if the word itself were slightly too sweet.

He says it.

KEVIN (CONT’D)
(quietly)
Please come home.

Nothing happens.

Then, very faintly, from every doorway at once, a sound like a distant bell.

ACT FIVE

INT. VARIOUS — THE YUKIS

A rapid, dreamlike sequence. Each doorway flashes, each Yuki turns her head.

INT. BLACKWOOD COFFEE — THE COUNTER

A Yuki in a barista’s apron, steaming milk, lifts her head. The milk overflows. She doesn’t notice.

YUKI (BARISTA)
(softly)
Did somebody say my name?

INT. THE MEGACHURCH — THE PULPIT

A Yuki in priestly vestments, halfway through a sermon on quarterly projections, stops. The congregation murmurs.

YUKI (PRIESTESS)
(slowly)
I think I’m being called.

INT. HEREAFTER REVENUE SERVICE — THE AUDIT FLOOR

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(whispering)

INT. MORTALITY SERVICES — THE WRONG HOME — NIGHT

INT. THE LAST STOP MOTEL — THE OFFICE

A Yuki in a bounty-hunter’s duster looks up from a stack of wanted posters. In every one, the face is Kevin’s.

YUKI (BOUNTY HUNTER)
(slowly)

A Yuki in a floral dress and gardening gloves, kneeling in a flowerbed, looks up. A bee lands on her wrist. She doesn’t flinch.

YUKI (GARDENER)

INT. THE BEIGE VOID — CONTINUOUS

Kevin opens his eyes.

KEVINA
(awed)

KEVIN
(barely breathing)
I didn’t know it would do that.

BOX
(proudly)
I’m a very good box.

Out of the white steps a figure.

She is not any of the Yukis. She is all of them. She wears a barista’s apron and a priestly stole and a bounty hunter’s duster and a gardening glove and the tattered remains of a Revenue Service badge. Her hair is dark and long and wild. Her face is a mosaic, every expression she has ever worn. Kind. Sharp. Tired. Fierce. Amused.

She walks across the nothing as if it were a pavement.

KEVIN
(whispering)
Yuki?

(in a voice of a hundred voices, all saying the same word)
Kevin.

KEVIN
(helplessly)
Hi.

YUKI
(a flicker of the old wry smile)
You look like shit.

KEVIN
(a wet, cracked laugh)

YUKI
(considering)
That’s fair.

You said please.

KEVIN
(nodding)
I did.

YUKI
(a rueful smile)
You always do.

KEVIN
(helplessly)

YUKI
(gently)
I don’t hate it.

KEVIN
(blinking)
You wrinkle your nose.

YUKI

KEVIN
(small)
Oh.

YUKI
(softly)
I’ve been hearing it for years. In every version of me. In every world. Somebody saying please at the end of a corridor. I didn’t know it was you. I just knew it was the only thing keeping me from coming apart.

KEVIN
(quietly)
I looked for you.

YUKI
(nodding)
I know.

KEVIN
(voice breaking)
I was so fucking angry at everyone. At the Reaper. At the HOA. At a cardboard box.

(small)
I forgive you.

KEVIN
(half laughing)
Thank you.

YUKI
(gently)
You found me.

KEVIN
(shaking his head)
I didn’t. You came.

YUKI
(a faint smile)

KEVIN
(quietly)

KEVIN

Three.

YUKI

YUKI

00:00:10:00

KEVINA
(sharply)
Kevin.

YUKI

KEVIN
(horrified)
It’s opening.

YUKI

Kevin.

YUKI

KEVINA
(whispering)

KEVIN
(whispering)

KEVIN
(horrified)

KEVINA
(softly)
That’s the worst thing I’ve ever heard.

YUKI
(not smiling)
It’s the worst thing there is.

KEVIN

(nodding)

YUKI
(gently)
Kevin.

I’m terrified.

ACT SEVEN

INT. THE BEIGE VOID — A LITTLE LATER

The void has changed. It is not beige anymore, not exactly. It is the soft pearl-grey of a quiet morning, and in the middle of it, as if a stagehand had wheeled it in, sits the old pine table from the Reaper’s kitchen. Four chairs. Four mugs. A pot of tea.
