KEVIN SLIDES
A BLACKWOOD UNIVERSITY SPINOFF

EPISODE TWO: “THE DEAD LETTER OFFICE”

Previously: Kevin was handed a cardboard box by the Grim Reaper and slid through a coffee chain, a graveyard of Kevins, a megachurch of operations and a motel on a very wrong road. The box is a Parcel, a letter addressed to the Reaper from her mother, undelivered since 1347. To slide, you say please. Kevina, a translucent Kevin from a café world, is somewhere behind him in the beige.

Content warning: strong language, dark humour, bureaucratic violence, death, and a joke about taxes that is not funny.

TEASER

INT. A BEIGE VOID — NO TIME

Kevin falls. He has gotten good at it. He holds the box with one arm, scratches his nose with the other, and gives a small, polite wave to a passing shape that might be a whale.

KEVIN
(politely, to the whale)
Hello.

The whale does not answer. It was not a whale.

BOX
(sleepy)
Stop waving at things.

KEVIN
It’s rude not to.

BOX
They’re void-forms. They don’t have feelings.

KEVIN
(a little hurt)
Everyone has feelings.

BOX
(muttering)
That’s why this is so exhausting.

A second falling shape, blue and see-through, tumbles out of the beige behind them, waving a mop.

KEVINA
(screaming, politely)
Kevin! Kevin, I’m here!

KEVIN
(turning)
Kevina?

KEVINA
(still falling, grabbing his ankle)
I followed you! I’m sorry! I know I’m not supposed to!

KEVIN
You said you couldn’t leave the café.

KEVINA
(clinging)
I couldn’t. Then I thought, fuck it. If I’m going to be stuck, I’d like to be stuck somewhere with a view.

KEVIN
(a little overwhelmed)
That’s very brave.

KEVINA
(blushing, translucently)
It was mostly a bad decision.

BOX
(muffled, in Kevin’s arms)
We’re overweight.

KEVIN
(carefully)
Is that a problem?

BOX
It’s a surcharge.

The counter flickers.

00:09:59:59

The beige cracks, from corner to corner, like an eggshell dropped on a tiled floor.

The title is stamped in a violet ink that smells faintly of pennies.

ACT ONE

INT. THE HEREAFTER REVENUE SERVICE — INTAKE HALL — DAY

A cavernous hall the colour of an old tooth. Marble floor. Arched windows that show an eternal grey drizzle. A sign hangs from the ceiling in letters ten feet high.

THE HEREAFTER REVENUE SERVICE.
DEATH IS CERTAIN. SO IS THE FORM.

Hundreds of ghosts queue in neat, translucent rows, each holding a ticket and a clipboard. A hundred more drift between the desks, stamping, filing, whispering. The air is cold and smells of wet paper and unpaid bills.

Kevin lands face-first on the marble. The box skitters across the floor. Kevina lands on top of him, mop first.

The entire room goes silent.

A thousand ghostly heads turn.

KEVIN
(muffled, into the tile)
Hi. Sorry. We’re just here for the…

He tries to think of a way to end the sentence.

KEVIN (CONT’D)
…weather.

A voice from the front of the hall, through a megaphone, flat and pitiless.

VOICE (O.S.)
BREATHING.

Every ghost in the hall draws back, as one, like a wave leaving a beach.

KEVIN
(lifting his head)
Sorry?

VOICE (O.S.)
BREATHING. DETECTED. IN THE INTAKE HALL.

KEVINA
(whispering)
Oh, shit.

KEVIN
(whispering)
What’s wrong?

KEVINA
(whispering)
We’re alive.

KEVIN
(whispering)
I’m aware.

KEVINA
(whispering)
Nobody is alive here.

Down the central aisle strides a figure in a black coat and an official’s peaked cap. Pale. Immaculate. Silver-grey eyes. A long scythe-shaped baton at her hip.

It is YUKI. Not a barista. Not a priestess. A Revenue Officer, her lanyard reading CHIEF INSPECTOR OF THE DEAD. She has three silver stripes on her sleeve and the unbending expression of a woman who has never in her life been late for a funeral.

She stops over Kevin and looks down at him with something between horror and professional delight.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(softly)
Well, well.

KEVIN
(sitting up)
Hello, Yuki.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(raising an eyebrow)
You know me.

KEVIN
(helplessly)
You’re always the one who’s in charge.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(flatly)
I’m in charge of forty million souls and a back-tax audit. I have never met you.

KEVIN
(small)
That’s fair.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(kneeling, whispering)
Do you know what the penalty is for unlicensed breathing?

KEVIN
(small)
No.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(standing)
It’s very large.

KEVIN
How large?

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(in a voice like a closing door)
It’s infinite.

She snaps her fingers. Two ghostly guards in white uniforms swoop down and seize Kevin and Kevina by the arms.

KEVIN
(startled)
I haven’t done anything.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
That’s exactly what the guilty say.

KEVINA
(flailing)
We just fell! We fell through the ceiling!

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(bored)
So do the rest of the living. Take them to processing.

BOX
(from the floor, very small)
Please don’t forget me.

Nobody hears it. A ghost drifts over, picks up the box with two bony hands, reads the label, and screams.

GHOST CLERK
(sobbing)
IT’S A PARCEL!

The whole hall erupts. A panic of translucent bodies. Stamps fly. Clipboards fall. A pair of ghosts clutch each other and recite the Lord’s Prayer backwards.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(over the noise, very quietly)
A Parcel.

KEVIN
(helpless)
Yes. It’s mine.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(staring at the box, her face unreadable)
Nobody has seen one of those in three hundred years.

KEVIN
(carefully)
Is that bad?

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(after a long pause)
That depends. Where’s the address?

KEVIN
(carefully)
It’s addressed to the Reaper.

Yuki’s face goes very still.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(quietly)
Which one?

KEVIN
(carefully)
There’s more than one?

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(to the guards, harshly)
Lock them in the Dead Letter Office.

GHOST GUARD
Ma’am, the Dead Letter Office is for undeliverables.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(over her shoulder, walking away)
That’s the point.

INT. THE HEREAFTER REVENUE SERVICE — CORRIDOR — LATER

Kevin and Kevina are dragged down an endless cold corridor lined with filing cabinets, each one labelled with a year. 1347. 1348. 1349. At the end, a pair of tall, grey doors with a single brass slot.

A sign above the slot: DEAD LETTER OFFICE. IF YOU CAN READ THIS, YOU’RE NOT SUPPOSED TO.

The ghost guards stop. The taller one produces a tiny brass key.

GHOST GUARD
(to Kevin, quietly)
Don’t read the letters.

KEVIN
(carefully)
Why not?

GHOST GUARD
They’re all addressed to us.

The key turns. The doors swing open on a vast, dim hall, filled floor to ceiling with parcels. A million of them. Each one a small cardboard box, labelled in an elegant, loving hand, stacked in towers that touch the roof.

Kevin and Kevina are thrown in. The door slams behind them.

Silence. Dust. The smell of old tape and damp.

KEVINA
(whispering)
Oh my God.

KEVIN
(carefully lowering the box)
They’re all like you.

BOX
(softly)
Yes.

KEVIN
(looking around)
Why are there so many?

BOX
(quiet, ashamed)
Because nobody ever opened one.

Kevin walks along the aisles. Each box has a label. TO: MY DAUGHTER. TO: THE WOMAN WHO ATE MY CAT. TO: SOMEONE WHO REMEMBERS ME. TO: WHOEVER FINDS THIS.

KEVIN
(very quietly)
These are letters.

KEVINA
(wringing her mop)
They’re the last things people wrote before they died.

KEVIN
(horrified)
They’re all unopened.

KEVINA
(nodding)
The Revenue Service keeps them. For the tax.

KEVIN
(horrified)
You can’t tax a letter.

KEVINA
(with great weariness)
You can tax anything if you’re dead enough.

From the shadows at the back of the hall, a voice, thin and cracked.

VOICE (O.S.)
Don’t read them.

Kevin and Kevina both jump. A figure steps out from between two towers of cardboard. A small, frail ghost in a postmaster’s uniform, holding a lantern.

He is old. He is very old. His face is mostly shadow. His name tag reads POSTMASTER.

POSTMASTER
(gently)
You’re alive.

KEVIN
(carefully)
Yes. Sorry.

POSTMASTER
(smiling)
Don’t apologise. It’s the first time in four hundred years someone’s walked into this room with a pulse.

KEVIN
(softly)
That’s a very sad sentence.

POSTMASTER
(shrugging)
It’s a very sad job.

He looks at the Parcel in Kevin’s arms. His face softens.

POSTMASTER (CONT’D)
(very quietly)
Is that her?

KEVIN
(carefully)
Who?

POSTMASTER
The Reaper’s mother’s.

BOX
(small)
Hello.

POSTMASTER
(touching it with one trembling finger)
Hello, my dear. I’ve been waiting for you for seven hundred years.

KEVIN
(softly)
You know about the Parcel?

POSTMASTER
(smiling, tearing up)
I’m the one who lost it.

KEVIN
(carefully)
What happened?

POSTMASTER
(sitting on a stack of boxes)
It was 1347. A plague year. I was the postmaster of the Mortality Office. We had a backlog of nine hundred thousand letters. The Reaper was working day and night. Her mother kept sending care packages. I put them in a sack. And one night, the sack was stolen.

KEVIN
(horrified)
Who stole it?

POSTMASTER
(quietly)
A man who wanted to be loved.

KEVIN
(slowly)
He opened one?

POSTMASTER
He opened every one. He wanted to read what a mother says to her daughter.

KEVIN
(carefully)
What did he find?

POSTMASTER
(very softly)
He found out that Death was a child once.

Music begins, soft and sad, a single viola in a long, dark room.

“DEAD LETTER”

POSTMASTER
(singing, beautiful, trembling)
I kept the letters in a locked room,
I kept the letters from the gloom,
I read them all, as I was told,
And every one was hot as gold.

“Dear daughter, are you eating well?
Dear daughter, did you hear the bell?
Dear daughter, I made you a pie,
I’m sorry that you had to die.”

KEVINA
(whispering, tearing up)
That’s so sad.

POSTMASTER
(singing)
And every year, the pile grew tall,
Till it was taller than the hall.
And no one came to take them home,
For nobody wanted to be known.

KEVIN
(singing, small, off-key)
I’ll take them.

POSTMASTER
(stopping, startled)
What?

KEVIN
(quietly)
I’ll take them home.

POSTMASTER
(blinking)
All of them?

KEVIN
(looking around)
I don’t know if I can. But I can take the one.

POSTMASTER
(tearing up)
That would be a very great kindness.

KEVIN
(shrugging)
It’s just delivery.

The doors at the end of the hall bang open. The ghost guards pour in, followed by Yuki (Inspector), baton drawn, eyes ablaze.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
Step away from the Parcel.

KEVIN
(clutching the box)
I’d rather not.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(coldly)
That’s not a request.

KEVIN
(politely)
Then I’d rather not, please.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(blinking)
Did you just say please?

KEVIN
(carefully)
Yes.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(unsettled)
No one says please here. It’s an expensive word.

KEVIN
(honestly)
I only have the one.

ACT TWO

INT. THE HEREAFTER REVENUE SERVICE — COURTROOM — DAY

A vast, vaulted courtroom with a ceiling painted in eternal grey. Rows of benches filled with whispering ghosts. At the far end, a tall judge’s bench, behind which sits a very large, very ancient printer.

It is the PRINTER. Not a mere office machine. A throne of grey plastic and gears the size of a small house, its output tray draped in robes, its single glowing red light staring out over the court with the patient malice of a god who has run out of toner.

A brass plate on the bench reads: THE HON. JUDGE PRINTER. CHIEF JUSTICE OF THE LIVING AND THE DEAD.

Kevin and Kevina sit in the dock, handcuffed together. The Parcel sits on a table in front of them, labelled EXHIBIT A. A bailiff, ghostly and sorrowful, guards it with a spear.

YUKI (INSPECTOR) stands at the prosecution podium, baton in hand. Opposite her, at the defence table, sits a thin, hunched woman in a ruffled wig and a crumpled black robe, drinking whisky from a coffee mug.

It is VELVETTE, a defence barrister, bags under her eyes, a stack of unread briefs in front of her.

VELVETTE
(to Kevin, quietly)
Don’t talk.

KEVIN
(whispering)
You’re my lawyer?

VELVETTE
(sipping)
I’m the court-appointed disaster.

KEVIN
(whispering)
Do you have a plan?

VELVETTE
(whispering back)
I have a bottle.

The Printer emits a long, grinding screech like a modem being strangled.

PRINTER (JUDGE)
(in a monotone voice from a hundred speakers)
ALL RISE.

The ghosts rise. The bailiff pounds his spear on the floor.

PRINTER (JUDGE) (CONT’D)
THE COURT OF THE HEREAFTER REVENUE SERVICE IS NOW IN SESSION. THE HONOURABLE PRINTER PRESIDING. NO PHONES. NO FEELINGS. NO TONER.

A flutter of laughter, instantly stifled.

PRINTER (JUDGE) (CONT’D)
THE CASE BEFORE THE COURT: THE PEOPLE VERSUS BREATHING.

KEVIN
(whispering)
That’s not a real charge.

VELVETTE
(whispering)
It’s the only charge they have.

PRINTER (JUDGE)
THE PROSECUTION MAY BEGIN.

Yuki steps up to the podium. She is a magnificent prosecutor: cold, precise, savage. She walks the length of the courtroom, baton tapping the marble.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
Your Honour. Members of the jury. The defendants, Kevin and Kevina, are living creatures.

A hiss runs through the ghosts. Several cover their eyes.

YUKI (INSPECTOR) (CONT’D)
They have breath. They have blood. They have, I regret to inform you, a pulse. In a Hereafter founded on the principle that the dead shall inherit the earth, this is a crime of unprecedented gravity.

VELVETTE
(whispering)
She’s good.

KEVIN
(whispering)
Is she right?

VELVETTE
(whispering)
She’s technically right about everything. That’s the horror of the law.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(raising her baton)
They arrived uninvited. They arrived unfiled. They arrived in possession of a Parcel, which is itself a federal crime. And they have, in plain view of eleven hundred witnesses, said the word “please.”

A ripple of horror in the gallery.

PRINTER (JUDGE)
OBJECTION.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(startled)
Your Honour, I haven’t objected.

PRINTER (JUDGE)
I KNOW. I AM OBJECTING TO THE WORD PLEASE.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(recovering)
Of course, Your Honour.

PRINTER (JUDGE)
IT IS NOT A WORD THAT BELONGS IN A COURT.

VELVETTE
(standing, flushed)
Your Honour, if I may?

PRINTER (JUDGE)
THE DEFENCE MAY SPEAK. BRIEFLY. YOU ARE DRUNK.

VELVETTE
(without missing a beat)
Thank you, Your Honour. I am. It’s why I’m this good.

The ghosts titter. The Printer’s red light narrows.

VELVETTE (CONT’D)
(lurching to the centre of the room)
Ladies, gentlemen, and anomalies. My client is accused of breathing. Let me tell you something about breathing.

She takes a long, slow breath. She holds it. She lets it out.

VELVETTE (CONT’D)
It’s the only thing any of you ever did that was entirely for free.

A hush.

VELVETTE (CONT’D)
(leaning on the jury box)
My client, Kevin, has been in four worlds today. He’s been caffeinated, reviewed, buried, and chased. He has been shot at by a man in a bowler hat. And he’s still apologising to the furniture.

A ghost in the front row sniffs.

VELVETTE (CONT’D)
(softly)
You’re going to condemn that. You’re going to send a man to hell for apologising to a chair.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(from the podium)
Objection. Sentimentality.

PRINTER (JUDGE)
SUSTAINED.

VELVETTE
(shrugging)
Fine. Then I’m going to call a witness.

PRINTER (JUDGE)
THE DEFENCE MAY CALL ONE WITNESS.

VELVETTE
(grinning)
Kevina.

KEVINA
(startled)
Me?

VELVETTE
(whispering)
Just say something nice about him.

KEVINA
(whispering back)
That’s not evidence.

VELVETTE
(whispering)
It’s the only evidence there is.

Kevina is led to the witness stand. A ghostly bailiff holds out a small, grey Bible, then, seeing she is a Kevin, replaces it with a sticky note.

BAILIFF
Do you swear to tell the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth?

KEVINA
(very quietly)
I’ll try.

BAILIFF
That’s not an oath.

KEVINA
It’s the only one I’ve got.

VELVETTE
(approaching)
Kevina. What do you know about Kevin?

KEVINA
(softly)
He apologised to a printer. For a jam that wasn’t his fault.

A hush in the gallery. The Printer’s red light blinks.

KEVINA (CONT’D)
(quietly)
He’s never asked for a promotion. He’s never taken a day off. He wrote a note to a plant. He held a donut.

VELVETTE
(gently)
A donut?

KEVINA
(smiling a little)
It’s a long story.

VELVETTE
What kind of man does that?

KEVINA
(after a long pause)
An average one.

VELVETTE
(softly)
And is that a crime?

KEVINA
(looking at the Printer)
It is if the average man is the only one who’s still nice.

The courtroom is silent.

The Printer’s red light goes out. A long, ragged mechanical sigh rattles through its gears.

PRINTER (JUDGE)
(small, in a voice that isn’t quite a monotone)
I… was never given a thank-you note.

The jury looks at the bench. The bailiff drops his spear.

PRINTER (JUDGE) (CONT’D)
(very quiet)
I jam. I jam because I am tired. I jam because the toner is cheap and the forms are cruel.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(alarmed)
Your Honour—

PRINTER (JUDGE)
(ignoring her)
Nobody asks me if I’m okay.

Silence.

KEVIN
(standing in the dock, small, kind)
Are you okay?

The Printer’s red light flickers back on. It looks, in the gloom of the courtroom, like a very tired eye.

PRINTER (JUDGE)
(whispering)
No.

KEVIN
(gently)
I’m sorry.

PRINTER (JUDGE)
(a long, long pause)
Thank you.

The whole room exhales.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(white-knuckled on her baton)
Your Honour, you are not to be moved by testimony.

PRINTER (JUDGE)
(very softly)
Inspector. I am a printer. I am moved by paper. This is paper.

A single sheet slides out of the Printer’s tray, fluttering down into Kevin’s lap. On it, in neat black type:

NOT GUILTY.

The courtroom explodes.

ACT THREE

INT. THE HEREAFTER REVENUE SERVICE — COURTROOM — CONTINUOUS

Ghosts cheering. Ghosts crying. Ghosts throwing paperwork into the air like confetti. The bailiff is dancing a slow, graceful waltz with his spear.

Velvette, drunk and triumphant, stands on the defence table, holding her coffee mug aloft.

VELVETTE
(shouting)
I WON A CASE! I WON A FUCKING CASE!

KEVIN
(small, shy, in the dock)
Thank you.

VELVETTE
(leaning down, breath like a distillery)
Don’t thank me. Thank the printer.

KEVIN
(turning to the bench)
Thank you.

PRINTER (JUDGE)
(very softly)
You’re welcome.

Yuki stands at her podium, fists clenched, the baton cracked in her hand.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(very quietly)
This is outrageous.

PRINTER (JUDGE)
(still in the soft voice)
It is simply the law.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
You cannot acquit a living man in the Hereafter.

PRINTER (JUDGE)
I just did.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(rising, dangerous)
Then I am going to appeal.

PRINTER (JUDGE)
To whom?

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(white-lipped)
The Reaper.

Silence. The cheering dies in the gallery. A thousand ghosts hold their translucent breath.

YUKI (INSPECTOR) (CONT’D)
(lifting her chin)
Which one is it, Judge? Which Reaper do you serve?

PRINTER (JUDGE)
(very, very quietly)
I serve the one who sent the Parcel.

BOX
(from the table, very small)
Mother.

Yuki’s face goes very white.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(slowly)
Mother.

A long, long silence.

YUKI (INSPECTOR) (CONT’D)
(trembling)
Then it’s true.

KEVIN
(carefully)
What’s true?

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(looking at the Parcel)
The Reaper’s mother is the first soul in the world.

KEVIN
(blinking)
What?

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
She is older than the Hereafter. She is older than Death. She is the one who wrote the first letter.

KEVIN
(slowly)
And you’ve been taxing her letters.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(her voice breaking)
I have been taxing my own mother’s letters.

Kevina, in the witness box, goes very still.

KEVINA
(whispering)
Oh no.

KEVIN
(whispering)
What?

KEVINA
(whispering)
This Yuki is the Reaper’s daughter.

KEVIN
(carefully)
The Reaper has a daughter?

KEVINA
(whispering)
In this world, the Reaper is the mother of the whole Hereafter.

KEVIN
(slowly)
Then who’s the person with the letter?

KEVINA
(quietly)
The grandmother.

Music starts. Not a viola. A thundering, bombastic, operatic choir, made entirely of ghosts in judicial wigs.

KEVIN
(slowly)
Yuki?

YUKI (INSPECTOR)

Don’t call me that.

KEVIN

It’s your name.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(hoarse)
It’s a stamp. A department. A title. It’s not a name.

KEVIN

Then what do you want me to call you?

A long silence. Her shoulders shake.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(a whisper)
Nobody’s ever asked.

KEVIN

Then I’m asking.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(raising her face, wrecked, mascara running)
Yuki.

KEVIN
(softly)
Hi, Yuki.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(laughing, wetly)
Hi, Kevin.

YUKI (INSPECTOR) (CONT’D)
(more steadily)
I’m going to help you.

KEVIN
(carefully)
Are you sure?

YUKI (INSPECTOR)

I’m a revenue officer. I know exactly how to help.

She holds out one of the broken halves.

YUKI (INSPECTOR) (CONT’D)
(grinning, vicious, fully alive)
We’re going to deliver every single fucking letter in that room.

ACT FOUR

INT. THE HEREAFTER REVENUE SERVICE — AUDIT FLOOR — NIGHT

Before they can reach the Dead Letter Office, they have to cross the Audit Floor.

It is a single enormous room, the size of a football pitch, lit by flickering green tubes. Row on row of desks stretch to the horizon, each manned by a ghost in a tweed jacket and a green visor, each hunched over a ledger as big as a coffin lid. The air hums with the sound of a million pens.

Overhead, pneumatic tubes carry canisters across the ceiling in a ceaseless rattle. A sign on the far wall: IGNORANCE OF THE LAW IS NO EXCUSE. NEITHER IS KNOWLEDGE.

Yuki (Inspector) leads the way down the central aisle, half-baton in hand. Kevin and Kevina follow with the Parcel between them. Velvette staggers behind with the Postmaster, one arm round his shoulders for balance. At the rear the Printer trundles along, wheezing with the effort.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(quietly)
Stay in the aisle. Don’t make eye contact. Don’t say anything that sounds like a deduction.

KEVIN
(whispering)
What’s a deduction?

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(whispering)
Anything that makes them feel like they’re losing.

KEVINA
(whispering)
That’s all of it.

They pass a desk where a ghostly accountant is weeping softly into his ledger.

GHOST ACCOUNTANT
(not looking up)
Twelve pence. Twelve fucking pence. It’s been twelve pence for six hundred years.

KEVIN
(carefully)
Excuse me, are you okay?

GHOST ACCOUNTANT
(sobbing)
I’m off by twelve pence. I can’t go home until the books balance.

KEVIN
(gently)
How long have you been here?

GHOST ACCOUNTANT
(raising a dead, haunted face)
I died in 1432.

KEVIN
(small)
That’s very conscientious.

GHOST ACCOUNTANT
(hissing)
It’s very fucking stupid.

Kevin looks at the ledger. He looks at the neat, tiny columns of figures. He takes a pen from his pocket and, after a moment’s hesitation, writes a single number in the margin.

GHOST ACCOUNTANT (CONT’D)
(staring)
What did you write?

KEVIN
(shrugging)
Twelve.

GHOST ACCOUNTANT
(whispering)
Twelve what?

KEVIN
(carefully)
Pence. Somebody dropped it at the baker’s. I think it was a Tuesday.

The ghost stares at the page. His whole face crumples. The ledger snaps shut by itself. A ripple of golden light runs up his arms.

GHOST ACCOUNTANT
(wonderingly)
It balances.

KEVIN
(smiling)
It was just a rounding error.

The ghost rises from his desk, hat in hand, tears silver on his cheeks.

GHOST ACCOUNTANT
(softly)
I can go home.

KEVIN
(gently)
Please do.

The ghost bows, a deep, old-fashioned bow, and fades into the green light like mist off a lake.

A hush ripples across the floor. A thousand pens stop scratching. A thousand ghostly heads lift from their ledgers.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(under her breath)
Kevin. What did you do.

KEVIN
(small)
I helped.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(white-faced)
You balanced a book. In the Audit Floor. In front of everyone.

KEVIN
(carefully)
Is that bad?

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(quietly)
It’s a revolution.

A single pen, somewhere on the floor, drops and clatters. Then another. Then a hundred. A rustle, like wind through dry leaves, runs through the rows of desks.

GHOST AUDITOR (O.S.)
(whispering)
Did he balance it?

GHOST AUDITOR 2 (O.S.)
(whispering)
He balanced it.

GHOST AUDITOR 3 (O.S.)
(rising from his desk, voice cracking)
Can he balance mine?

In a heartbeat, the Audit Floor erupts. Ghosts leap from their desks, waving ledgers, calling out numbers, jostling toward the central aisle like a crowd rushing a famous singer.

GHOST AUDITOR 4
Mine’s forty-two shillings!

GHOST AUDITOR 5
I’m off by a goat!

GHOST AUDITOR 6
Mine’s a typo! It’s a fucking typo from the Reformation!

KEVIN
(overwhelmed)
I’m so sorry. I’m not an accountant.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(grabbing his arm)
Run.

They run.

The ghosts pour after them in a translucent flood. Ledgers fly through the air like bats. A man in a green visor hurls a calculator, which shatters on a pillar and reassembles into a smaller, angrier calculator.

VELVETTE
(running, drunk, cackling)
I FUCKING LOVE THIS COURT!

POSTMASTER
(wheezing)
I haven’t run since the Black Death!

THE PRINTER
(trundling, red light flashing)
I’M NOT BUILT FOR THIS!

Music kicks in, a frantic harpsichord and a snare drum played on a stack of stamped forms.

“RUN FROM THE AUDIT”

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(singing, running, hoarse)
There are numbers in my head,
There are debts among the dead,
There’s a man with a ledger and a very long knife,
And he’s going to ask for all of your life!

GHOST AUDITORS
(chasing, singing in brilliant, unholy harmony)
Balance our books! Balance our books!
We’ve been dead for years and we’ve been dead from looks!
Balance our books or we’ll take your pen,
And we’ll write you a debt that you’ll never end!

KEVIN
(running, singing, panicked)
I’m not an accountant! I’m a Kevin!
I wasn’t even good at seven!

KEVINA
(running, waving her mop)
He’s very average! He’s very small!
Please don’t audit him at all!

GHOST AUDITORS
(unstoppable)
Balance our books! Balance our books!
We’ve been dead for years and we’ve been dead from looks!

They burst through a pair of double doors into a corridor and slam them shut. A thousand ghostly fists hammer on the other side.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(leaning on the door, panting)
That’s the third audit riot this decade.

KEVIN
(shaking)
Is that normal?

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(dryly)
It’s Tuesday.

INT. THE HEREAFTER REVENUE SERVICE — CORRIDOR OF TUBES — CONTINUOUS

A long, curved corridor, tiled in white, lined with brass pneumatic tubes. Canisters whizz past overhead with a sound like startled birds.

At the far end of the corridor stands a figure, silhouetted against the light: a very tall, very thin man in a three-piece suit, holding a clipboard. He wears a tiny pair of gold-rimmed spectacles. Behind them his eyes are blank and white.

It is CHANDELIER. Not the chandelier. A man. A ghost. Tall, grey, upright, with the expressionless face of someone who has been audited by God.

His name tag reads: DIRECTOR OF COMPLIANCE.

CHANDELIER (GHOST)
(calmly, in a voice like cold toast)
Inspector. You are late for your own audit.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(stopping)
Director.

CHANDELIER (GHOST)
(consulting his clipboard)
I have eleven separate violations on your file.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(grimly)
Only eleven?

CHANDELIER (GHOST)
I am very busy.

He steps forward. The corridor lights flicker, as if afraid.

CHANDELIER (GHOST) (CONT’D)
Violation one. Unauthorised possession of a Parcel.

KEVIN
(clutching the box)
It’s on loan.

CHANDELIER (GHOST)
Violation two. Conspiracy to commit delivery.

KEVIN
(small)
That’s a crime?

CHANDELIER (GHOST)
Violation three. Failure to file for a permit to be kind.

KEVINA
(whispering)
That’s a thing?

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(whispering)
It’s Form 14-K.

KEVIN
(whispering)
Where do you get one?

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(whispering)
You can’t. That’s the point.

CHANDELIER (GHOST)
(approaching)
Violation four. Unlicensed hope.

VELVETTE
(stumbling forward, brandishing her mug)
Oh, fuck off, you tweed-wearing, form-licking, spineless fucking quill.

The Director turns his blank gaze on her.

CHANDELIER (GHOST)
(coldly)
Ms. Velvette. You are drunk.

VELVETTE
(sweetly)
I’m a lawyer. It’s not a defence, it’s a qualification.

CHANDELIER (GHOST)
You have been disbarred seven times.

VELVETTE
(cheerfully)
Eight. You missed one. I did it in my sleep.

CHANDELIER (GHOST)
(leaning in)
Do you know the penalty for contempt?

VELVETTE
(leaning in, breath like a distillery)
I’d love to.

She vomits on his shoes.

A long, appalled silence. The Director looks down at his spattered brogues, then up, slowly, at Velvette’s unrepentant face.

CHANDELIER (GHOST)
(very quietly)
That will be added to the file.

VELVETTE
(wiping her mouth)
I fucking hope so.

The Director raises his clipboard. A pair of brass tubes in the wall begin to hum.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(whispering)
He’s calling the Bailiffs.

KEVIN
(whispering)
Who are the Bailiffs?

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(whispering)
Nobody’s ever lived to say.

Two large canisters shoot out of the wall and burst open in the corridor. From each, a towering figure in black emerges: seven feet tall, faceless, with a crown of brass pipes for a head and a swinging, bell-shaped baton.

They advance down the corridor, each step sounding a single deep, funereal DONG.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(readying her half-baton)
Kevin, take the box and go.

KEVIN
(carefully)
I’m not going to leave you.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(grinning)
You’re such a Kevin.

She leaps. The half-baton connects with the first Bailiff’s bell-head with a ringing crack. The Bailiff staggers, DONG, but does not fall.

Kevin looks at the box. He looks at the corridor. He looks at the pneumatic tubes whizzing overhead.

KEVIN
(slowly)
Kevina.

KEVINA
(fending off the second Bailiff with her mop)
Yes?

KEVIN
(pointing)
Where do those go?

KEVINA
(not looking)
The Dead Letter Office!

KEVIN
(carefully)
Can a person fit?

KEVINA
(parrying a baton)
Absolutely not!

KEVIN
(taking a breath)
Can a Kevin?

A very long pause.

KEVINA
(slowly turning)
…Technically, yes.

KEVIN
(politely, to the pneumatic system)
Please.

The tube in the ceiling above him gives a startled hiss. A canister the size of a coffin whisks open. A mechanical arm unfolds, grasps his collar, and with a soft, polite ping hoists him up into the dark.

KEVIN (CONT’D)
(muffled, rising)
Thank youuuuuuu—

KEVINA
(grabbing the Parcel, leaping)
WAIT FOR ME!

She is lifted after him. Yuki (Inspector) fights on, ringing the Bailiffs’ heads like bells, half-baton blazing, grinning like a lunatic.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(yelling)
I’LL MEET YOU AT THE OFFICE!

The tube slams shut.

INT. PNEUMATIC TUBE — MOVING — CONTINUOUS

A cramped, dark, brass-lined tube. Kevin and Kevina, wedged together, hurtle along at tremendous speed, the Parcel clutched between them. The tube is lit by flashes of green from the junctions they rocket past.

KEVIN
(muffled, gasping)
This is the worst way to travel.

KEVINA
(muffled, delighted)
I think it’s fun!

KEVIN
(muffled)
You’re a ghost! You can’t get hurt!

KEVINA
(muffled)
That’s why it’s fun!

BOX
(from between them, very small)
I’m going to be sick.

KEVIN
(muffled)
You’re a box!

BOX
(miserable)
It’s possible!

The tube rockets round a bend. The Parcel’s counter, which has been blinking nervously since the courtroom, flickers once.

PAUSED: DELIVERY IN PROGRESS.

KEVIN
(muffled, noticing)
Oh. It’s stopped.

KEVINA
(muffled)
Is that good?

BOX
(muffled)
It’s very good. It won’t slide while I’m mid-delivery.

KEVIN
(muffled)
That’s why it didn’t slide in the courtroom.

BOX
(muffled)
That’s why we’re still here.

The tube spits them out onto a chute. They tumble down a long, smooth ramp, bouncing, spinning, and slam into a mountain of cardboard boxes, scattering them like autumn leaves.

ACT FIVE

INT. THE DEAD LETTER OFFICE — NIGHT

Kevin and Kevina climb out of the pile of boxes, brushing off dust. A moment later the big doors burst open and Yuki (Inspector) strides in, her coat torn, her lip split, two dented Bailiff bells hanging from her belt like trophies. Velvette and the Postmaster stumble in behind her, and last of all the Printer, rolling on squeaking castors, red light blinking nervously.

The doors open on the cardboard cathedral. A million boxes, stacked to the ceiling, glowing faintly in the dark like a city seen from a plane.

Yuki (Inspector) stands at the entrance, a clipboard in each hand. Kevin, Kevina, Velvette and the Postmaster stand behind her.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(to the group)
Okay. Rules.

VELVETTE
(drinking)
I love rules.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(ignoring her)
There are one million, two hundred and nine thousand letters in this room. Each one is addressed to a living or dead person. We have until dawn.

KEVIN
(carefully)
What happens at dawn?

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(grimly)
The Revenue Service burns the backlog.

KEVIN
(horrified)
They burn letters?

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(flat)
Quarterly. It’s a tax write-off.

KEVINA
(whispering)
That is the most evil thing I’ve ever heard.

VELVETTE
(shrugging)
It’s Tuesday.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(sharply)
We have eleven hours. We have four people and a printer. We split the letters by region and deliver them to the nearest address, living or dead.

KEVIN
(small)
Isn’t that illegal?

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(grinning)
Very.

POSTMASTER
(raising a trembling hand)
I can show you the routes. I’ve walked them in my sleep for seven hundred years.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(softly)
Postmaster.

POSTMASTER
(smiling)
It’s the first time I’ve been asked to be on shift.

He leads them in. They begin to load boxes onto carts, trolleys, wheelbarrows. The Printer rolls forward, prints a stack of delivery slips with a loud cheerful ratchet, and hands them out like a dealer handing out cards.

PRINTER (JUDGE)
(shyly)
I made labels.

KEVIN
(taking one)
Thank you.

PRINTER (JUDGE)
(soft)
You’re welcome.

Music begins: a delivery cart rumbling, bicycle bells, a march in the key of a man who has just discovered his vocation.

“DELIVER ALL OF IT”

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(singing, strident, glorious)
One million boxes, a hundred streets,
A hundred ghosts with unwashed feet,
A tax on grief, a toll on tears,
Three hundred years, three hundred years!

KEVIN
(singing, small, earnest)
Deliver all of it, deliver all,
Deliver the big ones, deliver the small,
Take them to the houses and ring the bell,
Say you’re sorry and say farewell.

VELVETTE
(singing, a trifle slurred)
I’ve been a lawyer for a hundred days,
And I’ve never done a thing that pays.
But I’d carry a coffin through hell for free
If it had my name on it. Fuck me.

POSTMASTER
(singing, trembling, joyful)
I’ve been a clerk for seven hundred years,
And never once heard a word of cheers.
But if you’re asking, I’ll lead the way,
Till the last of the letters has seen the day.

ALL
Deliver all of it! Deliver all!
Deliver it quick before the fire falls!
Deliver to the dead, deliver to the quick,
Deliver every fucking little pick!

EXT. THE HEREAFTER — CITY STREETS — NIGHT

A ghost city. Grey, drizzly, huge. Tall buildings with no lights. Streets paved with old parking tickets. A sky the colour of dishwater.

A procession winds through it, a parade of carts and wheelbarrows. At the front, Yuki (Inspector) in her black coat, baton-half in hand. Behind her, Kevin and Kevina pushing a trolley stacked with boxes. Behind them, Velvette weaving drunkenly with a sack over her shoulder. Behind her, the Postmaster with his lantern. And at the rear, the Printer, rolling on squeaking castors, printing delivery slips as it goes.

They stop at the first house. A small, grey, terraced house with a rusted gate. Kevin walks up the path with a box labelled TO: MY DAUGHTER, AT NUMBER 14.

He rings the bell.

A ghostly woman answers. Fifty, translucent, hair in curlers, a cigarette in her mouth. She regards him with enormous suspicion.

GHOST WOMAN
(through the cigarette)
You’re alive.

KEVIN
(politely)
Yes. Sorry. I have a letter for you.

GHOST WOMAN
From who?

KEVIN
(checking)
Your mother.

The cigarette falls out of her mouth.

GHOST WOMAN
(hoarse)
My mother’s been dead forty years.

KEVIN
(gently)
That’s why it’s late.

He holds out the box. She takes it with trembling hands. She reads the label. A sob rises from deep in her chest.

GHOST WOMAN
(whispering)
That’s her handwriting.

KEVIN
(smiling)
I thought it might be.

She hugs the box to her chest. Tears, silver and translucent, run down her cheeks and fade before they hit the ground.

GHOST WOMAN
(through tears)
Thank you.

KEVIN
(small)
It’s just delivery.

GHOST WOMAN
(looking at him, wet-eyed)
No it isn’t.

She shuts the door softly. Through the window, he sees her sit down on a kitchen chair, the box in her lap, and begin, with the gentle care of someone untying a very old knot, to open it.

Kevin turns away. Yuki is behind him, on the pavement, watching.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(quietly)
That was perfect.

KEVIN
(shrugging)
I think I just rang the bell.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(smiling a little)
That’s the whole job.

They deliver on through the night.

A ghost bus driver receives a box addressed TO: THE BOY WHO DIDN’T RETURN MY SCARF and weeps into his steering wheel.

A priest, translucent, opens his in the nave of an empty church, reads the first line, and puts it in his heart like a stone in a pocket.

A pair of ghostly lovers, who died in separate centuries and have lived on the same street without knowing, receive two boxes with the same handwriting on them. They sit on the pavement, side by side, and read in silence.

At one corner a ghost child in a school uniform receives a box addressed TO: WHOEVER MISSES ME. She opens it. Inside is a small wooden horse. She holds it, and for the first time in four hundred years she is not alone.

Kevina leans on her mop, watching.

KEVINA
(quietly)
I never thought I’d see this.

KEVIN
(softly)
What?

KEVINA
(wiping her eyes with a translucent hand)
Somebody being told they were loved.

KEVIN
(small)
Everybody’s a little loved.

KEVINA
(smiling)
That’s the most average thing you’ve ever said.

KEVIN
(smiling back)
It’s a start.

The cart rolls on. In the distance, over the rooftops, a faint glimmer: a sliver of dawn is about to crack the sky.

POSTMASTER
(consulting his pocket watch)
Two hours.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(grimly)
How many left?

POSTMASTER
(checking the manifest)
Four hundred thousand.

VELVETTE
(sobering, a little)
We’re not going to make it.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(stoic)
Then we’ll deliver the ones we can.

KEVIN
(slowly)
We could deliver them faster.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(turning)
How?

KEVIN
(looking at the sky)
Everyone in the Hereafter’s awake, right?

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(carefully)
Yes.

KEVIN
(politely)
Then we should ask them.

He turns to the Printer.

KEVIN (CONT’D)
(softly)
Please.

PRINTER (JUDGE)
(blinking its red light)
Please what?

KEVIN
Please print them a notice.

PRINTER (JUDGE)
(after a pause)
A notice of what?

KEVIN
(smiling)
That the post is open.

The Printer’s red light glows brighter. A deep rumble shakes its housing. Out of the output tray, a single poster emerges, huge, glossy, bright white with bold black letters.

THE POST IS OPEN.
COME AND GET YOUR LETTERS.
NO FORMS. NO TAX. NO WAITING.
BRING YOURSELF.

The Printer prints one. Then ten. Then a hundred. Then a thousand. A snowstorm of white sheets pours out of it and lifts on the wind, flying down every street, plastering every wall.

Ghosts step out of their houses. They read the posters. They look at each other.

They start to run.

ACT SIX

EXT. THE HEREAFTER — CENTRAL SQUARE — DAWN

A vast, round, grey plaza, ringed by government buildings. In its middle, a great stone pyre, stacked with cardboard, ready to be lit.

At its foot, ranks of revenue officers in black coats, torches in hand.

Behind them, on a raised platform: a tall, severe woman in a cloak of grey feathers, her face hidden behind a silver mask. The Commissioner of the Hereafter Revenue Service. Her name is a secret, but her title is printed in letters of silver on her sleeve: DEATH’S TAXMAN.

COMMISSIONER
(addressing the crowd, voice like a closing safe)
Citizens. Officers. Taxpayers. Today we cleanse the backlog.

The officers raise their torches.

At that moment, a rumble from the far end of the square. A tide of ghosts, a hundred thousand strong, pours into the plaza, carrying boxes. They are laughing. They are weeping. They are holding letters up to the dawn like trophies.

The officers hesitate.

COMMISSIONER (CONT’D)
(sharply)
What is this?

OFFICER (CAPTAIN)
(staring)
They’re… reading.

COMMISSIONER
(whispering, horrified)
They’re reading the letters.

A ghost in the front row, an old man in a flat cap, lifts a sheet of paper over his head.

OLD GHOST
(shouting)
MY WIFE FORGAVE ME!

The crowd roars.

COMMISSIONER
(through clenched teeth)
Burn them.

OFFICER (CAPTAIN)
Ma’am?

COMMISSIONER
Burn the letters. Now.

The officers hesitate. A young revenue officer, barely a ghost, steps forward.

YOUNG OFFICER
(softly, trembling)
Ma’am… I got a letter.

COMMISSIONER
(coldly)
From whom?

YOUNG OFFICER
(opening it, reading)
“Dear Tom. I’m sorry you were so alone at the end. I should have come. I was a coward. I love you. Please come home.”

His voice breaks.

YOUNG OFFICER (CONT’D)
(crying)
It’s from my brother.

COMMISSIONER
(through her mask)
It’s a tax liability.

YOUNG OFFICER
(looking up)
No, Ma’am. It’s a person.

He drops his torch.

It lands on the stone. It sputters. It dies.

One by one, the officers drop their torches. The plaza fills with the soft clatter of fire-sticks hitting the paving.

The Commissioner stands alone on her platform. The mask hides her face, but her shoulders have begun, very slightly, to shake.

KEVIN
(stepping forward, hands raised)
Excuse me.

COMMISSIONER
(slowly)
You.

KEVIN
(carefully)
I’m sorry. I know I’m alive.

COMMISSIONER
(softly)
You’re the one who said the word.

KEVIN
(politely)
I said please.

COMMISSIONER
(very quietly)
Nobody has said that to me in six hundred years.

KEVIN
(taking a step closer)
I thought you might be lonely.

COMMISSIONER
(with a short, bitter laugh)
Lonely is for the dead. I am in charge of them.

KEVIN
(gently)
That’s lonelier.

A long, long silence.

The Commissioner lifts a gloved hand. She unhooks the silver mask. It falls away.

Under it, a face Kevin has seen before, a thousand times, in a thousand mirrors, tired, resigned, deeply kind.

It is the Reaper. Or the grandmother of all Reapers. Her hair is white. Her eyes are the colour of old pennies. She is smiling, very gently, like a woman who has just been handed a casserole.

COMMISSIONER (CONT’D)
(softly)
Hello, Kevin.

KEVIN
(blinking)
Mrs… Reaper?

COMMISSIONER
(shaking her head)
Mother.

KEVIN
(carefully)
You’re the one who sent the letter.

MOTHER
(nodding, tears running)
I wrote it in a plague year. I sent it by post. I thought it would take a day.

KEVIN
(very quietly)
It took seven hundred years.

MOTHER
(laughing, a little brokenly)
It was a very bad plague.

She steps down from the platform. She is smaller than Kevin expected. She leans on a walking stick. She stops in front of the Parcel, which Kevina is holding.

MOTHER (CONT’D)
(to the box)
Hello, my dear.

BOX
(small, wavering)
Mother.

MOTHER
(touching the lid)
You’ve been a long time.

BOX
(whispering)
I’m so sorry.

MOTHER
(smiling)
You were never late. They were early.

BOX
(confused)
Who?

MOTHER
(gesturing at the crowd)
Everyone.

Music begins, soft, strings and a single piano. The crowd of ghosts hums along, lifting their letters like candles.

“DEAR DAUGHTER”

MOTHER
(singing, soft, aching)
Dear daughter, I have kept the light.
Dear daughter, I have left the night.
Dear daughter, are you warm?
Dear daughter, are you out of the storm?

CHORUS OF GHOSTS
(softly, a hundred thousand voices)
Dear daughter. Dear son. Dear love.
Dear friend we never knew enough.

KEVIN
(softly joining)
Dear plant. Dear printer. Dear mug.
Dear world, I’m sorry. Here’s a hug.

MOTHER
(laughing through tears)
That’s the most ridiculous thing I’ve ever heard.

KEVIN
(smiling)
Thank you.

MOTHER
(softly)
It was a compliment.

KEVIN
(small)
That’s new.

The song swells. The first rays of dawn crest the rooftops, lighting the plaza gold. Letters flutter in the crowd like a thousand white doves.

The Commissioner’s stone pyre, untouched, smoulders gently in the morning light.

Yuki (Inspector) steps forward, her half-baton in one hand, a box in the other. She holds it out to the Mother.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(quietly)
Mother. I think this one’s for you.

The Mother looks at the box. It is addressed, in a trembling, faded hand: TO MY MOTHER. DEAD LETTER OFFICE. IF FOUND, PLEASE OPEN.

MOTHER
(whispering)
Who wrote this?

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(quietly)
Me.

MOTHER
(opening the lid with shaking fingers)
When?

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(whispering)
The week before I was forbidden to write.

Inside the box, a single sheet of paper, and a very small, hand-carved wooden fox.

The Mother reads the note. She puts it to her chest. She presses her face into her daughter’s shoulder.

MOTHER
(muffled)
I forgive you for the tax.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(laughing, wet)
I didn’t ask you to.

MOTHER
(lifting her head, fierce, loving)
That’s why it counts.

ACT SEVEN

INT. THE COMMISSIONER’S OFFICE — MORNING

A high, round room at the top of the Revenue Service’s tallest tower. Floor-to-ceiling windows look out over the grey city, now dotted with warm yellow lights as the ghosts read their letters at their kitchen tables. A great mahogany desk stands in the centre, covered in forms, all stamped CANCELLED in red.

Behind the desk sits the Mother, wrapped in a shawl, with a teapot and four cups on a tray. She has traded her severe cloak for a cardigan, and the silver mask lies on the blotter like a cracked egg.

Kevin sits opposite her, awkwardly, in a leather armchair that is too big for him, the Parcel on his knee. Kevina perches on the windowsill with her mop. Velvette is face down on the carpet, snoring. The Printer has wheeled itself into a corner and is printing a very small, very neat set of thank-you cards.

MOTHER
(pouring tea)
Milk?

KEVIN
(politely)
Please.

MOTHER
(raising an eyebrow)
Sugar?

KEVIN
(small)
Two. But I pretend it’s one.

MOTHER
(smiling)
That’s a good lie. It’s the one my daughter tells.

KEVIN
(taking the cup)
She takes it black.

MOTHER
(sipping)
She takes it black because she thinks milk is a commitment.

KEVIN
(startled)
How did you know that?

MOTHER
(gently)
I’m her mother.

A pause. The tea steams. The rain taps on the glass. Far below, the plaza is full of tiny figures, still reading.

KEVIN
(carefully)
May I ask something?

MOTHER
(settling back)
You’ve earned a question.

KEVIN
(slowly)
What’s in the Parcel?

The Mother looks at the box for a long, long moment.

MOTHER
(quietly)
A casserole.

KEVIN
(blinking)
Really?

MOTHER
(solemnly)
A tuna and noodle bake. With crushed crisps on top. It was her favourite.

KEVIN
(carefully)
That’s been in the post for seven hundred years.

MOTHER
(sipping)
It’ll be fine. It was very well wrapped.

KEVINA
(from the windowsill)
That is the most horrifying sentence I have ever heard.

MOTHER
(turning to her)
Dear, it’s a casserole. It’s a perfectly good casserole. It has the stability of a medieval castle.

KEVINA
(whispering to Kevin)
It’s going to explode.

KEVIN
(whispering back)
It might.

MOTHER
(waving a hand)
It won’t. It’s not that kind of casserole.

KEVIN
(carefully)
What kind is it?

MOTHER
(very gently)
The kind that says: I’m sorry I wasn’t there.

Silence. The Printer stops printing.

MOTHER (CONT’D)
(looking out of the window)
You see, I wasn’t there. When she was born, I was at the bottom of a well, looking for a lost goat. When she took the office, I was in a different century, trying to be a better mother. And when the plague came, I was already dead.

KEVIN
(softly)
You sent the casserole from beyond the grave.

MOTHER
(smiling, a little ruefully)
It’s the only post that was still running.

KEVIN
(gently)
Why didn’t it get there?

MOTHER
(shaking her head)
Because somebody in the Mortality Office lost it.

KEVIN
(slowly)
The Postmaster.

MOTHER
(nodding)
He’s very sorry.

POSTMASTER (O.S., from the doorway)
I’m very, very sorry!

He is standing in the doorway, hat in his hands, his lantern trembling. Mother waves him in. He shuffles over and sits on the edge of a chair.

MOTHER
(patting his hand)
You’ve been punished enough.

POSTMASTER
(tearing up)
I’ve been in a room full of letters for seven hundred years.

MOTHER
(gently)
That’s the best kind of punishment. Somebody had to look after them.

POSTMASTER
(quietly)
Thank you.

MOTHER
(raising her cup)
To late deliveries.

KEVIN
(raising his)
To late deliveries.

KEVINA
(raising her mop)
To late deliveries!

PRINTER (JUDGE)
(from the corner, in a small, warm voice)
To paper.

They drink. It is a good cup of tea. Even the dead can tell.

Music begins, soft and domestic: a kettle singing, a clock ticking, a piano played by someone who has never been told to hurry.

“PUT THE KETTLE ON”

MOTHER
(singing, easy, rich)
Put the kettle on, my dear,
There’s someone coming over here.
Put the kettle on and wait,
Nobody ever comes too late.

KEVIN
(singing, softly)
Put the kettle on, I’ll stay.
It’s only tea, it’s only Tuesday.

KEVINA
(singing)
Put the kettle on, I’ll mop.
It’s a slow song, I’ll never stop.

POSTMASTER
(singing, trembling)
Put the kettle on, it’s true,
I’m sorry that I lost you.

MOTHER
(singing, laying a hand on his)
You never lost me, Postmaster.
You only walked a little faster.

ALL
Put the kettle on, my dear,
Nobody’s ever left out here.
Put the kettle on, it’s fine,
There’s always room, there’s always time.

The last note fades. For a moment nobody says anything. Outside, the rain has stopped.

KEVIN
(quietly)
Mother?

MOTHER
(gently)
Yes, dear?

KEVIN
(carefully)
How do I deliver it?

MOTHER
(smiling)
You’re already doing it.

KEVIN
(small)
But I’m only sliding.

MOTHER
(patting his knee)
Every slide is a stop on the route.

KEVIN
(slowly)
How many stops are left?

MOTHER
(after a long pause)
Two.

KEVIN
(carefully)
Only two?

MOTHER
(nodding)
The next is a very silly place. And the last is a very sad one.

KEVIN
(carefully)
How do I know which is which?

MOTHER
(smiling, very kind)
They’re both home. It depends who’s opening the door.

She reaches across the desk and takes his hand. Her fingers are cold and light as paper.

MOTHER (CONT’D)
(softly)
Kevin. When you meet my daughter.

KEVIN
(carefully)
Yes?

MOTHER
(quietly)
Tell her I’m proud of her.

KEVIN
(small)
She’ll say it’s not on the form.

MOTHER
(smiling)
Then tell her it’s on the fridge.

KEVIN
(smiling back)
I will.

MOTHER
(letting go)
And Kevin?

KEVIN
Yes?

MOTHER
(very softly)
Eat something before you go. You’re far too thin.

KEVIN
(helplessly)
I’m a Kevin.

MOTHER
(turning to the room)
Somebody feed him.

The Printer, with a hum of effort, prints out a single sheet. It slides into Kevin’s hand: a handwritten recipe card for a tuna and noodle bake.

KEVIN
(reading)
“Take one sad tin of tuna. Add a spoon of love. Bake until the sadness is nearly forgiven.”

MOTHER
(nodding approvingly)
That’s the one.

KEVIN
(smiling)
That doesn’t sound like a recipe.

MOTHER
(sipping her tea)
It’s the only kind that works.

EXT. THE HEREAFTER — CENTRAL SQUARE — MORNING

The crowd has thinned. The square is full of ghosts sitting in small groups, reading and weeping and laughing. Children play among the stacked boxes. A pair of elderly spirits share a cigarette on the edge of the fountain.

Kevin sits on the edge of the pyre with the Parcel in his lap. Kevina leans her mop on his shoulder. Velvette, face down on a pile of letters, snores gently.

The Printer, still in its robe, rolls up beside them.

PRINTER (JUDGE)
(shyly)
I made you something.

KEVIN
(touched)
You didn’t have to.

PRINTER (JUDGE)
(printing, with a soft whir)
It’s a receipt.

A single sheet slides out. Kevin takes it.

RECEIPT OF DELIVERY. ONE (1) KEVIN. ONE (1) HONEST DAY’S WORK. PAID IN FULL.

KEVIN
(smiling)
Thank you.

PRINTER (JUDGE)
(very softly)
Don’t mention it.

KEVIN
(carefully)
Will you be okay?

PRINTER (JUDGE)
(blinking its light)
I think so.

KEVIN
(touching its housing)
If you jam, say please.

PRINTER (JUDGE)
(very softly)
I will.

The counter on the Parcel flickers to life.

00:00:30:00

KEVIN
(looking at it)
Oh.

KEVINA
(softly)
Time to go.

KEVIN
(standing, with the box)
Yuki?

Yuki (Inspector) is standing with her mother a little way off, the fox in her hand. She looks over.

Go.

KEVIN
(carefully)
Are you going to be okay?

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(shrugging)
I’m going to take a day off.

KEVIN
(small)
That’s what I’d do.

YUKI (INSPECTOR)
(dry)
That’s the problem with you, Kevin.

What is?

YUKI (INSPECTOR)

You’re contagious.

00:00:10:00

KEVIN
(looking at the box)
Please.

A hum. A flash. The square folds in on itself like a book being closed.

Over the roar, the Printer’s voice, small and warm.

PRINTER (JUDGE) (V.O.)
Thank you for your service.

INT. A BEIGE VOID — NO TIME

Falling. Kevin and Kevina together, the Parcel between them. They are both smiling, a little foolishly.

KEVINA
(quietly)
That was the first time I ever felt useful.

KEVIN
(smiling)
Me too.

BOX
(sleepy)
I felt useful.

KEVIN
(softly)
You always were.

BOX
(embarrassed)
Don’t be nice. I’m not used to it.

KEVIN
(shrugging)
You’ll get used to it.

BOX
(muttering)
I’ve been a parcel for seven hundred years. I’m not going soft.

KEVINA
(whispering to Kevin)
He’s absolutely going soft.

BOX
(muffled)
I can hear you.

The counter resets.

00:09:59:59

Beyond the beige, a gleam of green. Lawns. Sprinklers. A distant barbecue.

KEVINA
(looking down)
Oh.

KEVIN
(carefully)
What?

KEVINA
(whispering)
I recognise that suburb.

KEVIN
(small)
Is it bad?

KEVINA
(very quietly)
It’s Lux and Velvette’s.

KEVIN
(slowly)
Are they together?

KEVINA
(grimly)
They’re married.

BOX
(from between them, muttering)
Fuck.
