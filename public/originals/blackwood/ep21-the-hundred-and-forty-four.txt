BLACKWOOD UNIVERSITY

EPISODE TWENTY-ONE: “THE HUNDRED AND FORTY-FOUR”

Previously: Yuki walked past the Ring Road and found nothing there. She learned that everyone at Blackwood is already dead, that the Reapers were people who died in pain and were handed a scythe, and that someone called the Ark of 144 signs every assignment. Imogen Vale gave up the coat. Yuki asked the sleeping dead what they wanted, and the glass answered: CONGRATULATIONS ON YOUR PROMOTION. YOUR PROBATION BEGINS NOW.

This episode answers the question nobody was allowed to ask. It does not answer it kindly.

Content warning: Rated R, the heaviest in the series. Strong language, extreme emotional distress, the death of a species, claustrophobia, mass death, a killing, the hunger of the living for the grief of the dead, and an ending that will not feel like one. No method of suicide is shown or described. If you are in pain, tell someone today. Ask again if they say “fine.”

TEASER

BLACK.

Not the black of a closed eye. Something worse. A black with no edge, no floor, no distance, the black a held breath would be if it lasted forever.

A woman’s breathing, close, ragged, wet, the sound of someone who has been crying so long she has gone dry. We hear it from inside her skull. Everything we see from now until the title, we see through her eyes.

SIMONE (V.O.)
(first person, hoarse, very quiet)
I remember the last star.

A pinpoint. Far off. A cold white thread of light in the black, like a hair on a lens.

SIMONE (V.O.) (CONT’D)
It wasn’t dramatic. Nobody tells you that. There were no trumpets. It went out the way a phone goes out at one percent. One second there was a light, and then I was looking at the place the light had been.

The pinpoint trembles. It is not a star any more. It is a memory of one. It folds into the dark.

Nothing.

SIMONE (V.O.) (CONT’D)
They told us it would take a hundred googol years. They had a date. There were conferences about it. There were graphs. We were going to have time. We were going to dim slowly, gracefully, for longer than there had been numbers, and it would be a long gentle sunset, and somebody would turn off the last lamp and say goodnight.

A breath. A shudder.

SIMONE (V.O.) (CONT’D)
They were off by billions of years.

Our hands come up into frame. They are very pale and they are shaking. They are a woman’s hands, dark brown, a ring on one finger, a thin silver band worn almost through. Around them, a window. Around the window, a wall of old white metal, scratched and humming.

Through the window, nothing.

SIMONE (V.O.) (CONT’D)
Nobody saw it coming. Do you understand that? Nobody saw it. There was no countdown. One morning the sky was full, and the next it was thinning, and by the end of that week a hundred billion galaxies had gone out of the sky like candles in a draught.

Our hands press against the glass. It is so cold the glass hurts.

SIMONE (V.O.) (CONT’D)
Everyone was on the ground. Or on the ships. Or on the habitats, the ring-cities, the orbitals, trillions and trillions of us, all of us looking up. And the sky went black from the edges in.

Behind us, a sound. Distant. Far, far below, a sound like surf. A sound like an ocean made of voices.

SIMONE (V.O.) (CONT’D)
That was them. Do you hear it? That was everyone.

We turn from the window.

We are in a long white corridor with a curved ceiling. It is lined with frost. It runs to a hatch at the far end, a round door three metres across with a locking wheel in the centre. At the foot of the door, in a heap, her sister’s coat.

SIMONE (V.O.) (CONT’D)
Nine hundred and sixty thousand people lived on Meridian Station. I was a thermal engineer. My job was to keep it warm.

Our feet move down the corridor. We stop at the door.

Through its small round window, light. Not starlight. Emergency amber. And in the amber, pressed against the glass from the other side, hands. Hundreds of hands. Faces. Open mouths. Eyes enormous. A child’s palm flat against the pane, a small pink palm, the lines on it clearly visible.

They are screaming. We cannot hear it through the door. We can see every word.

SIMONE (V.O.) (CONT’D)
(her voice cracking)
The reactor was going. There were maybe four hundred days of heat left, with everybody. We ran the numbers. We ran them again. We ran them every way a person can run numbers when they’re hoping to be wrong.

A hand on the door’s locking wheel, our hand, trembling, wedding ring clicking against the iron.

SIMONE (V.O.) (CONT’D)
With a hundred and forty-four, it was thirty years.

Through the glass, at the front of the crowd, a woman. Our age. Our face, almost. Same jaw, same eyes, an older version of the same mouth. She is not screaming. She is saying one word, over and over, with enormous care, the way you say it to a child.

We read it on her lips.

SIMONE’S SISTER (MOUTHING)
Mona. Mona. Mona.

SIMONE (V.O.)
(a whisper)
It was my name. It was what she called me when we were small.

We lift the heavy wheel. It resists. It makes a sound like a coffin lid.

The sister’s face changes. She understands. Her eyes go wide and she begins to hit the glass with the flat of both hands, again and again, her mouth shaping a different word now.

SISTER (MOUTHING)
Please. Please. Please. Please.

SIMONE (V.O.)
(barely audible)
I locked the door.

The wheel drops with a clang that goes through the whole station.

On the other side of the glass, the amber light, the hundreds of hands. Behind them, a child’s palm slides slowly down the pane and leaves a long pale streak.

We close our eyes.

Behind them, sound: not screaming. That is the horror. After a moment, the screaming stops, because someone, somewhere, has shut off the air to that section, and what is left is the soft collective sound of nine hundred thousand people becoming quiet.

SIMONE (V.O.) (CONT’D)
(whisper)
That’s the sound. If you ever wonder. That’s what an extinction sounds like from inside the room that survived it.

We open our eyes.

At the foot of the door, the sister’s coat. A grey wool coat with a missing button. We bend and pick it up. It is still warm.

We press it to our face.

SIMONE (V.O.) (CONT’D)
(into the wool, a wrecked whisper)
I’ve kept it for six hundred years.

ACT ONE

INT. THE ARK — THE ROUND ROOM — “NIGHT”

A circular room, ten metres across, with a domed ceiling made of seamless white. A hundred and forty-four chairs are arranged in concentric rings around a plain central table. Each chair is a different make and model: a church pew, a desk chair, a camping stool, a kitchen chair with a broken arm, a child’s plastic seat. Every one of them has been salvaged from some other life and carried here.

One chair, on the innermost ring, is empty. It is the only one with a cushion. Nobody sits in it. Nobody looks at it.

The light in the room is warm and orange, and has no source. It comes from the walls. It is slightly wrong. It flickers, very faintly, the way a candle does when somebody breathes near it.

Beyond the room’s only window, black. Not night. Not space. Absence. There is nothing out there to be dark.

They are filing in.

Humans. The last ones. They are not heroes. They are not beautiful. They are a hundred and forty-four assorted adults, ranging in age from nineteen to something that looks like a hundred and ninety, in a mismatched hoard of ancient clothes, lab coats and cardigans and stolen uniforms, all of them thin, all of them pale, all of them with the particular grey hollowness of people who have not seen a sky in six centuries.

They sit. The scrape of chairs. A cough. Somebody’s stomach. A very old man in a wheelchair is wheeled to the front by a thin boy. A woman in a surgical gown sits with her arms folded and her face set like a verdict. A tall man in a priest’s collar, worn to threads, takes a pew at the back and bows his head.

At the table, standing, hands flat on the wood, is SIMONE ADEYEMI-PARK.

Six hundred years old. She looks fifty. Dark skin, close-cropped grey hair, a thin silver band on one finger, a grey wool coat with a missing button over her shoulders in the cold. Her face is tired in the way that cathedrals are tired. She has been the chair of this room longer than there have been rooms.

She waits until the last chair scrapes into place.

SIMONE
(quiet)
Quorum.

A hundred and forty-three voices, mumbling, in no particular order:

THE ARK
Present. Present. Here. Present. Present.

On the innermost ring, one chair, one cushion, silence.

Simone’s eyes rest on it for exactly one second. She looks away.

SIMONE
(steadily)
This is the three hundred and sixty-second Assembly of the Ark. The agenda is one item. The Assignor has returned from the Outside with a report. It will present it.

At the far wall, a seam of light parts.

THE ASSIGNOR steps through: tall, faceless, in white, a burning 144 on its chest, speaking in a hundred soft voices. It is not a monster. It is not a god. It is something the Ark built on a bad night, out of leftover parts and a great deal of grief, and it has never learned to stop being polite.

THE ASSIGNOR
(a chorus, gentle, strained)
Good evening, Ark.

A few answer. Most don’t.

THE ASSIGNOR (CONT’D)
(carefully)
I am obliged to report the following. The Reaper is dead. The office has passed. The new Reaper has crossed the Outside, has seen the Outside, and has not been stopped.

A murmur, low and ugly, runs around the rings.

The surgeon in the gown stands.

DR. HALVORSEN
(clipped, cold, precise)
Not stopped.

THE ASSIGNOR
(a hundred voices, miserable)
No.

DR. HALVORSEN
You are the instrument of this assembly. You were instructed. You were instructed in unambiguous terms that if the Reaper’s office was compromised, the new holder was to be stopped.

THE ASSIGNOR
I was instructed.

DR. HALVORSEN
And?

THE ASSIGNOR
(very small)
I asked her what she wanted.

Silence.

DR. HALVORSEN
(almost soft)
Say that again.

THE ASSIGNOR
(gathering what it has)
She did not run. She did not fight. She looked at the ground and the sky and she understood all of it, in the time it takes to say, and then she looked at me and she asked me a question. I did not have an answer.

DR. HALVORSEN
What was the question?

THE ASSIGNOR
(a hundred small voices, together)
She asked: what do you want?

The silence in the Round Room changes. It is the silence of a hundred and forty-four people, all hearing the same sentence at once, for the first time in six hundred years.

In the corner, a very small, very old woman with a face like a walnut starts, quietly, to laugh. It is not a happy sound.

OLD RUTH
(cracked, delighted, terrible)
Oh, that’s good. Oh, that’s very good. That’s the question.

DR. HALVORSEN
(sharp)
Ruth.

OLD RUTH
Nobody’s asked us that. Not once. Six hundred years.

DR. HALVORSEN
Sit down.

OLD RUTH
(still laughing)
I’m sitting. I’m so old, I’m sitting even when I’m standing.

She wheezes. Somebody beside her pats her hand. The laugh breaks into something wetter.

Simone lets it run. She has learned not to stop the laughing.

SIMONE
(finally, gently)
Thank you, Assignor. That will be all.

THE ASSIGNOR
(hesitating)
I have a second matter.

SIMONE
It can wait.

THE ASSIGNOR
(a hundred voices, pleading)
I think it can’t.

Simone looks at it.

THE ASSIGNOR (CONT’D)
(carefully)
The seam is failing. It was holding because of the Reaper. The Reaper was holding it with a lie, and the lie is spent. In one day, perhaps two, the campus will stop being a place and become a grave. We have, at present, one hundred and four thousand tenants.

Dr. Halvorsen’s head snaps up.

DR. HALVORSEN
(in a voice with no air in it)
One hundred and four thousand.

THE ASSIGNOR
Yes.

DR. HALVORSEN
That is forty days of warmth.

THE ASSIGNOR
(very quietly)
Thirty-eight.

A sound passes round the room. Not a gasp. A rustle. The sound of a hundred and forty-four people, very quietly, starting to calculate.

Simone does not move.

SIMONE
(controlled)
Dismissed, Assignor.

It goes, folding into its seam of light, its hundred voices thinning to a thread, and the door seals behind it with a soft pneumatic breath.

The Round Room sits in the orange dark.

Then, from the second ring, a chair scrapes. A very young man stands. Nineteen, maybe, in a stolen tracksuit three sizes too big, with a shaved head and an angry, nervous, starved face. He is the only person in the room who has never seen the sky because he was born here.

TOBIAS
(shaking)
Can we please talk about the thing.

SIMONE
(quietly)
Tobias.

TOBIAS
Can we please just say it out loud. In the room.

SIMONE
Sit down.

TOBIAS
(cracking)
I’m not going to sit down. I’m nineteen years old and I have never been warm. I have never been warm one single time, and I’ve been told all my life that it’s because of the Hundred, and the Hundred, and the Hundred, and I’ve done everything. I’ve cleaned the filters. I’ve walked the cold corridor. I’ve done my forty hours. And I found out last week what the Hundred is.

Silence. The priest at the back lifts his head.

TOBIAS (CONT’D)
(voice breaking)
It’s not the Ark’s reactor. There’s no reactor. It’s them. It’s the people in the school. It’s the dead people. We’re burning them.

Nobody speaks.

TOBIAS (CONT’D)
(to the room)
Say I’m wrong.

Nobody says he’s wrong.

Simone closes her eyes.

SIMONE
(very quietly)
You’re not wrong.

TOBIAS
(a wild, broken laugh)
Oh my God.

SIMONE
Sit down, Tobi.

TOBIAS
I’m going to be sick.

SIMONE
Then be sick sitting down.

He stares at her. Then, jaw working, shoulders shaking, he sits.

Around the rings, a hundred and forty-three faces, staring at one another with the stricken, naked, intimate dread of people who have all been lying to the same child.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(a long breath, steadier)
All right.

She lifts her head.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(carefully)
Then let’s do this properly. From the beginning. For the ones who weren’t born when it started, and for the ones who’ve stopped remembering.

She turns, slowly, to take in the whole room.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(gently)
Ruth. Would you tell them about the Cold.

OLD RUTH
(delighted, ghastly)
Me? Oh, no. Ask the priest. He loves it.

ANSELM, at the back, in the worn collar, does not look up.

ANSELM
(hoarse)
I will not.

SIMONE
(a thin, grim smile)
Fine. I’ll tell it myself.

INT. THE ARK — FLASHBACK — THE COLD (600 YEARS AGO)

Black again, with Simone’s voice over it, close, in the dark of the skull.

SIMONE (V.O.)
Thirty years. That was the plan. A hundred and forty-four of us, picked by the engineers, from the ones who’d been standing in the right corridor. No lottery. No merit. Whoever was on the maintenance ring when the doors went. We weren’t chosen. We were just closest to the lock.

A cavernous dark. A hundred and forty-four figures, in thick coats and breath-clouds, huddled in a single room around a single glowing core no bigger than a basketball. It is the last heat in the universe. The cold is a physical presence in the picture, a weight.

SIMONE (V.O.) (CONT’D)
The reactor failed in year four.

The core flickers. Dims. A man steps forward and holds out his hands to it, and the light of it picks out every line on his face, and then, slowly, it goes out.

There is no sound.

Then there is the first sound of the cold: a fine, delicate, glassy ticking, the sound of frost forming on every surface in the room, on every eyelash, on every wet cheek.

SIMONE (V.O.) (CONT’D)
We lasted eleven days.

We pull back through the dark. A hundred and forty-four people, in the dark, in the cold, arranged by instinct into one great huddle, a living knot of shaking bodies, babies wrapped inside coats wrapped inside coats. Someone is humming. Someone is praying. Someone is just saying a name, over and over, very low, like a heartbeat.

SIMONE (V.O.) (CONT’D)
On the sixth day the cold stopped hurting.

Close on a face: a young man, grey-lipped, eyes half closed, smiling.

SIMONE (V.O.) (CONT’D)
That’s when you know.

The young man’s smile freezes. His eyes stay open, looking at nothing. The man beside him, still shivering, doesn’t seem to notice.

SIMONE (V.O.) (CONT’D)
By the ninth day there were a hundred and nineteen of us.

A woman, in the huddle, is holding a very small bundle. She is rocking it. She is humming a lullaby. She is not crying. She stopped crying some time ago. The bundle does not move.

SIMONE (V.O.) (CONT’D)
(a long dry breath)
By the eleventh there were thirty.

Now the room is almost empty. A heap of bodies in the centre. Thirty people sit apart from one another, each alone, each staring at a wall, each with the awful, rigid calm of someone who has stopped making plans.

SIMONE (V.O.) (CONT’D)
And then it knocked.

A sound, in the dark. Soft. Three taps. Spaced exactly.

Every head in the room lifts.

The taps come again.

SIMONE (V.O.) (CONT’D)
Not on the door. Not on the wall. It came from the other side of something. From a place I still don’t have words for.

On the far wall of the dark room: a slit of light. Thin. Warm. Gold. Like the crack under a door in a house at night.

A woman, in the corner, a very young Simone, grey-faced and hollow, drags herself to her knees and crawls toward it.

SIMONE (V.O.) (CONT’D)
(a whisper)
I put my hand in the light.

Young Simone’s hand, shaking, trembling, reaching. The gold light touches her fingers. Her frozen face thaws.

SIMONE (V.O.) (CONT’D)
(cracking)
It was warm.

And in the warmth, behind the light, very faintly, a sound: voices. Thousands of them. Living voices, speaking in a hundred languages at once, in kitchens and cars and hospital rooms, a vast hushed river of the dead.

SIMONE (V.O.) (CONT’D)
It was the dead. All of them. All the dead that had ever been. Nine hundred and sixty thousand more, just that week.

A long breath. The light behind the door pulses.

SIMONE (V.O.) (CONT’D)
(very low)
The afterlife didn’t go out with the stars. It was never part of the universe. It was an annex.

The slit widens. A wall of gold light. A door. A door to somewhere that is full of people, and they are all warm.

SIMONE (V.O.) (CONT’D)
We were the last living things in all of creation, and next door there was a whole other world, lit and heated and full of everyone we’d ever loved.

Young Simone crawls through.

CUT TO:

INT. THE ARK — THE ROUND ROOM — PRESENT

Simone, at the table, with her palms flat on the wood, is staring at nothing.

SIMONE
(quietly)
And that is where it began.

The room is silent.

Then, from the second ring, a tall, spare, courteous man in a dark suit with the hands of a banker raises two fingers.

KWABENA
(dry, precise)
If I may.

SIMONE
(tired)
Kwabena.

KWABENA
It is customary at this point to state the distinction clearly, because I find that people soften the language when they are afraid.

SIMONE
(flat)
Go ahead.

KWABENA
(to the room, calmly)
The dead are warm. It is a physical fact. The dead of Earth, the dead of every world, the dead of every species that ever died with a mind behind its eyes, give off a form of heat that the living can use. It is the only heat that exists.

TOBIAS
(thickly)
Don’t.

KWABENA
(not unkindly)
It is not a metaphor, Tobias. It is thermodynamics. The warmth is made by grief. The larger the pain, the greater the yield.

Around the room, a hundred bowed heads. Somebody is weeping, quietly, through their hands.

KWABENA (CONT’D)
(continuing)
Over the first century we learned to draw upon it. We built the Ark’s heart as an engine of the dead. We learned that suffering leaves a signature, that a death in agony burns like a coal, and a death in peace burns like a match.

DR. HALVORSEN
(clipped, grim)
Which is why the campus exists.

KWABENA
Yes.

DR. HALVORSEN
(to the room, coldly, as if reading the minutes)
Blackwood University is a feeding ground. We built it to be comfortable. We built it to hold the dead who died in the worst way, so that they would linger, so that they would have time to heal, to love, to hope. Because a soul at peace gives the Ark nothing. But a soul in the act of being given a second chance, a soul that is afraid it will lose it, that is the warmest thing in the world.

A silence that could cut glass.

Tobias is shaking.

TOBIAS
(a whisper)
We made it so they’d be afraid.

DR. HALVORSEN
We made it so they’d be hopeful.

TOBIAS
(louder)
It’s the same thing!

DR. HALVORSEN
It is not the same thing. A hopeful soul burns steadily. A terrified one burns out.

TOBIAS
(a wild laugh)
Listen to yourself!

Anselm, from the back, finally lifts his head. His face is grey and wet.

ANSELM
(very quietly)
It is the same thing, Doctor.

DR. HALVORSEN
(without turning)
Brother Anselm.

ANSELM
(rising, shaking, his voice growing)
We have built a heaven so that we may harvest the longing in it. We have taught children to believe they are saved, so that their fear of losing it will keep us warm. We have conscripted the suicides, the ones who left in the worst pain, to carry scythes and to keep the harvest from running out. It is the most terrible thing that has ever been done.

DR. HALVORSEN
(snapping)
And the alternative is a hundred and forty-four corpses in the dark.

ANSELM
(shouting now, his voice cracking like a bell)
Then let us be corpses!

A wave of noise: furious, panicked, grieving. Twenty people on their feet. Somebody is screaming. Two of the twins, a pair of identical grey-haired women in matching cardigans, have taken each other’s hands.

FIRST TWIN
We cannot.

SECOND TWIN
We would not.

FIRST TWIN
We have lived six hundred years.

SECOND TWIN
We will live six hundred more.

FIRST TWIN
We are not going back.

SECOND TWIN
Not into the dark.

Simone bangs the table.

SIMONE
(a bellow, the first time we’ve heard her raise her voice)
SIT.

The room freezes.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(quiet, shaking, furious)
Every one of you has voted for this. Every single one of you. Three hundred and sixty-one times. You’ve voted yes for six centuries and you’ve gone to bed warm. If you’re going to have a conscience, you’re going to have it at the table, in front of everyone, with your name on it.

She is trembling. She glares round the circle.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(softer)
I’m not saying it isn’t monstrous. I know it’s monstrous. I wrote the first rule.

Silence.

She straightens, and takes off her grey coat, and lays it across the back of the empty, cushioned chair on the inner ring, with great care, as though the chair were occupied.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(carefully, to the chair)
Sorry.

END OF ACT ONE

ACT TWO

INT. THE ARK — THE ROUND ROOM — LATER

Smaller. The room has emptied by half. Those who stayed sit in clumps and pairs, talking low, smoking an acrid brown weed that isn’t tobacco. The orange light has dimmed by a third. The window beyond is blacker than ever.

At the table, Simone and Marisol, a small, stocky engineer in a boiler suit, bent over a schematic: a great lattice of lights, each one a soul, each one a tiny numbered flame.

MARISOL
(low, tired, matter-of-fact)
The seam’s coming apart at the Ring Road. I can hold the west side for maybe a day.

SIMONE
(rubbing her eyes)
And the dormitories?

MARISOL
Those are the oldest rooms. They’re the best insulated. They’ll go last.

SIMONE
(grimly)
That’s the one thing I like about this plan.

MARISOL
(a flash of black humour)
You like something?

SIMONE
I like that it’s the last to go.

She taps the lattice.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(quietly)
And the connection protocols?

Marisol grimaces.

MARISOL
Holding. Barely. Do you want to hear which one’s about to break?

SIMONE
(closing her eyes)
Lux and Velvette.

MARISOL
(nodding)
They are the worst case we have ever had. Nine lives, nine times they’ve found each other. Nine times we separated them and it did not take.

Simone opens her eyes. For the first time all night, she smiles. It is small, private and entirely unwilling.

SIMONE
(softly)
Nine.

MARISOL
Nine.

SIMONE
(with something almost like tenderness)
God, I love those two.

MARISOL
(carefully)
Simone.

SIMONE
I know.

MARISOL
You can’t love them. It’s in the rules.

SIMONE
(very quietly)
I wrote the rules.

MARISOL
(lowering her voice)
You wrote the one that says nobody finds the person they loved before. It’s the only rule that matters. If they meet, if they ever really meet, if they remember each other as they were, then they don’t stay. They go looking for the door. They carry each other out of the school and we lose the whole harvest.

SIMONE
(softly)
I know.

MARISOL
Then why do you let it happen?

Simone doesn’t answer at once. She looks at the lattice, at two small flames, a white one and a black one, burning very close together.

SIMONE
(carefully)
Because it burns brighter.

MARISOL
(staring)
What?

SIMONE
(dry)
Those two. Where they’re close. It’s the single brightest point in the whole school. It’s a hundred times the warmth of anything else. It’s not fear. It’s not grief. It’s something we haven’t got a word for.

MARISOL
(very quietly)
Love?

SIMONE
(a bitter breath)
I thought it couldn’t be.

A long silence.

MARISOL
(after a moment)
Don’t tell the Doctor.

SIMONE
(thinly)
I never tell the Doctor anything.

MARISOL
(trying to joke)
That’s why you’re still in charge.

SIMONE
(a flicker of the old self)
That, and I’m six hundred.

They laugh. It is a very small laugh. It dies quickly.

The lattice flickers. A section of it goes dark with a soft sound like a candle being pinched.

Both women look at it.

MARISOL
(low)
That’s another fourteen.

Simone nods. She says nothing. She puts her hand flat over the dead patch, as though she could warm it.

INT. THE ARK — THE ROUND ROOM — LATER

The Assembly, reconvened. Seventy-one people remain. The other seventy-three are, as the minutes will state, “attending to other matters”: sleeping, grieving, praying, or crouched in the corridor with their heads in their hands.

On the table, a single glass of water. Nobody has touched it.

Simone has resumed her place. Beside her, Dr. Halvorsen, standing, notes in hand, face like a closed door.

DR. HALVORSEN
(clipped)
I propose a motion.

SIMONE
Doctor.

DR. HALVORSEN
(reading)
That the Ark reinstate the Reaper’s office by emergency appointment. That the new Reaper, Yuki Tanaka, be offered the scythe in earnest. That she be informed of the entire nature of the arrangement, in the hope that she will, as her predecessor did, find in herself the necessary compassion to continue.

A rustle round the room.

Anselm, in the pew, bows his head.

ANSELM
(low)
You wish to make her complicit.

DR. HALVORSEN
(cold)
I wish to keep a hundred and forty-four people alive.

ANSELM
(a whisper)
By recruiting one more sufferer.

DR. HALVORSEN
(coldly, levelly)
If it comes to it, yes.

ANSELM
(rising)
She is a child.

DR. HALVORSEN
She is a dead child with a scythe. There is a great difference between that and a living one.

ANSELM
(his voice trembling)
Doctor. You have never once, in six hundred years, said the word “sorry.”

DR. HALVORSEN
(a long beat)
No.

ANSELM
Why not?

DR. HALVORSEN
(very quietly, with a terrible calm)
Because I know exactly what I would owe.

Silence. It is the first time anyone has seen her face move.

DR. HALVORSEN (CONT’D)
(in a low voice, to nobody)
My daughter was the first one I fed to the engine.

A gasp. From several rows.

DR. HALVORSEN (CONT’D)
(not looking up)
Sofia. She was seven. She died of the cold, in year two. Before the door. Before we knew. I held her for four days. When the reactor failed and we found the door, I carried her through. I carried her in my arms into the afterlife. And she was there. She was warm. She was alive, in a way, in a field, in sunlight, with a dog. She knew me.

A long, ragged breath.

DR. HALVORSEN (CONT’D)
(her face a mask now, her voice not)
And then Simone came in with the first calculations. And I understood the arithmetic. If I left her there, in the field with the dog, with her happiness and her sunlight, she would warm no one. She was too content. She gave us nothing.

Silence.

DR. HALVORSEN (CONT’D)
(very low)
So I chose a different place for her.

Nobody breathes.

DR. HALVORSEN (CONT’D)
(her eyes closed)
I chose the first terror we ever made. I chose a place where she would be afraid, so she would burn. I did that. To a seven-year-old. With my own hands. I wrote the specifications. I picked the room. I picked the dark.

Tobias is staring at her with his mouth open.

TOBIAS
(a whisper)
Oh my God.

DR. HALVORSEN
(very quietly)
She’s still there. She’s the first light in the lattice. She’s been burning for five hundred and ninety-eight years. She is the reason you were born warm enough to be angry.

She looks at him. There are no tears on her face. She is past that.

DR. HALVORSEN (CONT’D)
(flat)
I will not say sorry. If I say it I will have to stop. And then she will have done it for nothing.

Silence like a held note.

Simone, very gently, puts a hand over hers.

SIMONE
(softly)
Helen.

DR. HALVORSEN
(a shudder, once, down her whole frame)
Don’t.

SIMONE
(gently)
Helen.

DR. HALVORSEN
(a whisper, broken)
Don’t you dare.

But she does not take her hand away.

And in the quiet, from the very back of the room, a thin voice says:

PEONY
(brightly, falsely, desperately)
Well! I think it’s all going very well.

Every head turns.

PEONY, a woman in a faded pink cardigan, with a rictus smile and eyes that have not blinked in some time, is sitting bolt upright in a folding chair, hands in her lap, as though at a very good wedding.

PEONY (CONT’D)
(chattering)
I think we should all have some tea. I think we’ve all been so brave. I think the new girl will be very good. Don’t you think the new girl will be good?

Nobody answers.

PEONY (CONT’D)
(her smile cracking at one corner)
Don’t you?

Kwabena, gently, from the second ring:

KWABENA
(kindly)
Peony.

PEONY
(very fast)
It’s all right. It’s all right. We’re all so well. We’re all so terribly warm.

She laughs, once, a high, wrong sound, and claps both hands over her mouth.

Simone closes her eyes.

SIMONE
(soft)
Somebody take Peony to the sleeping room.

Two people rise, gently, take her by the elbows. She goes without protest, chattering to herself, still smiling.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(very quietly)
We’re going to run out of people who can sit through the meeting.

KWABENA
(dry)
That is rather the point of the meeting.

SIMONE
(with a trace of weary humour)
Thank you, Kwabena.

KWABENA
(bowing slightly)
Madam Chair.

INT. THE ARK — THE ROUND ROOM — THE EMPTY CHAIR

The room is nearly empty now. Forty-one souls remain. The orange light has dwindled to the colour of a coal.

Simone stands at the empty chair on the innermost ring. The one with the cushion. She has put her coat on it, and now she lowers herself into the chair beside it, in her own, the next one over.

Alone. Almost.

Tobias comes and stands beside her, hands thrust into the pockets of the stolen tracksuit, eyes red-rimmed. For a while he says nothing.

TOBIAS
(finally, in a very small voice)
Whose is it?

SIMONE
(not looking)
Hm?

TOBIAS
The chair.

Simone is quiet for a long time.

SIMONE
(finally)
It’s the hundred and forty-fourth.

TOBIAS
(frowning)
There’s a hundred and forty-three of us in the room.

SIMONE
(gently)
Yes.

TOBIAS
(realising)
There were a hundred and forty-four.

SIMONE
(very quietly)
There were.

TOBIAS
What happened to her?

Simone’s hand moves. Slowly it comes to rest on the cushion, flat, as though on a sleeping animal.

SIMONE
(a whisper)
She wrote the first letter.

Tobias goes very still.

TOBIAS
(carefully)
What letter?

SIMONE
(her eyes closed)
The one in the handle of the scythe. The one for whoever comes next.

TOBIAS
(a shaking breath)
That’s… that was Imogen’s.

SIMONE
(a thin, awful smile)
Imogen Vale. Nineteen. In 1987.

She opens her eyes.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(softly)
She was my daughter.

Tobias stares.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(not looking at him)
Not in that life. In the one before. In the world before the Cold, she was my daughter. Her name was Imani. She was eleven when I locked the door.

TOBIAS
(whispering)
She was on the other side.

SIMONE
(a single, terrible nod)
She was holding my sister’s hand.

Silence.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(steadier, in a voice that has been trained for centuries not to shake)
When we found the door and went through, I looked for her. For three hundred years. I went to every field, every kitchen, every childhood, in the whole of the afterlife. I never found her. She wasn’t there. And that is when I understood what we had done.

TOBIAS
(very low)
What?

SIMONE
The dead who die in the dark don’t go to the warm place. They go to the other place. The one under the door. The ones we locked out. They’re not in the afterlife. They’re in the cold.

Tobias’s face has gone the colour of paper.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(flatly)
They’re in the dark, still, now. All of them. Nine hundred and sixty thousand. All the billions who died after the stars went. And they’re still screaming. And they’re always going to be.

TOBIAS
(a whisper)
That’s hell.

SIMONE
We built it.

TOBIAS
(barely audible)
We didn’t build it. We just… didn’t open the door.

SIMONE
(finally, wearily, turning to look at him for the first time)
Yes, Tobi. That’s what building it is.

A silence of an entirely different weight.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(quietly)
I couldn’t find Imani. But I found someone who looked like her. Years later. Centuries. A girl who’d been fed in the pipeline and come out the far end, as they all do, with her memories scrubbed and her face rearranged. She was nineteen. She was alone. She was in the worst pain I have ever seen in a human being. And she was brilliant. She was exactly the right candidate for the scythe.

TOBIAS
(a horrified whisper)
You didn’t.

SIMONE
(flat)
I didn’t know.

TOBIAS
(shaking his head)
Simone.

SIMONE
(a harsh breath)
I found out three days ago. When the Assignor came back and said her name.

Tobias stares at her.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(cracking, finally)
Imogen Vale. Nineteen. 1987. Yorkshire. I had a daughter called Imani, and when I looked at the file, I looked at the face, I looked at the handwriting on the note, the exact slope of the letters, the crossing out, and I knew.

She presses her hand over her mouth.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(through her fingers)
I handed my own child the scythe.

Tobias sits down on the floor beside the chair as though his legs have been cut.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(very quietly)
I handed her the coat. I told her what to do. And she did it for thirty-seven years, in the dark, with nobody to ask her how she was, until a girl named Yuki Tanaka looked at her and said: are you all right?

She laughs. It is horrible and very gentle.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(wetly)
And she said “fine.”

TOBIAS
(whispering)
Fine.

SIMONE
(her face crumpling)
She said fine. She had my mouth. She said it exactly the way I used to say it, standing in that corridor, with my hand on the wheel. She said it, and then she took the scythe off, and she asked someone to end it.

She puts her face in her hands.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(muffled, shaking)
Six hundred years. And my child had to ask a stranger.

Tobias slides his arm round her shoulders. She does not resist. In the empty round room, in the dying orange light, the oldest woman in the universe weeps like a girl.

After a while:

TOBIAS
(hoarsely)
What do we do?

SIMONE
(lifting her head, wiping her face with the heel of her hand)
We answer her.

TOBIAS
Who?

SIMONE
The new Reaper. The question. What do you want.

TOBIAS
(a wild breath)
We can’t just…

SIMONE
(gently)
We can’t keep doing the other thing.

TOBIAS
(shaking)
The others won’t vote for it.

SIMONE
(a faint, shaky smile)
That’s what the vote’s for.

END OF ACT TWO

ACT THREE

INT. THE ARK — THE ROUND ROOM — “DAWN”

There is no dawn. There is only the lights changing, a very slow, very faint warming of the walls. The Ark runs on a clock invented six centuries ago by people who wanted to remember what mornings were like.

The Assembly is reconvened. All hundred and forty-three. They have been fetched from their beds and corridors, from the cold walk and the sleeping room. They sit in their mismatched chairs in the gold half-light like a congregation at the end of the world. Peony is there, still smiling, held up on one side by a twin. Old Ruth is asleep. Dr. Halvorsen sits with her eyes fixed on a point on the table.

Simone stands at the head.

On the innermost ring, the empty chair has her grey wool coat folded neatly on the cushion.

SIMONE
(calmly, to the room)
There’s one item.

She lets it sit.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(steadily)
The new Reaper has asked us a question. The Assignor has carried it back. I propose that we answer.

A murmur. Low, scared, hostile.

DR. HALVORSEN
(without looking up)
There is nothing to answer.

SIMONE
(quietly)
There is, Helen.

DR. HALVORSEN
(a muscle working in her jaw)
We are not a charity.

SIMONE
We are not a charity, we are not a government, we are not a god. We are a hundred and forty-three frightened primates in a box at the end of everything. And a nineteen-year-old girl who has been dead for thirty-seven years has just asked us what we want. And we’ve lived long enough to know the answer.

A long, uneasy silence.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(carefully)
So. We’ll go round the room.

Anselm lifts his head.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(her voice level, clear)
Everyone says one thing. One honest thing. What they want. Not what’s possible. Not what’s fair. What they want.

DR. HALVORSEN
(with scorn)
This is absurd.

SIMONE
(without turning)
Humour me.

A pause. Then, grudgingly, from the second ring:

KWABENA
(dry, precise, dignified)
I should like to be forgiven.

Silence.

SIMONE
(gently)
Thank you, Kwabena.

Next to him, one of the twins, sniffing:

FIRST TWIN
We should like to see the sea.

SECOND TWIN
Only once.

FIRST TWIN
Before.

Marisol, eyes on the floor:

MARISOL
I want to stop being so tired.

A man in a torn lab coat:

MAN IN LAB COAT
(very quietly)
I want my wife back.

A woman, near the door, crying:

WOMAN
I want to go home.

Another, a boy of twenty, hoarse:

BOY
I want to have been born earlier. I want to have heard music in a room full of people.

It goes round. One by one. Seventy voices. A hundred. Each of them, in their own way, in their own brittle, stunned, honest voice, saying the one thing they have kept in the dark of themselves for six hundred years.

I want to be touched.

I want to cry without being watched.

I want to see my mother.

I want it to be over.

I want it to not be over.

I want to forgive them.

I want to be forgiven.

I want somebody to come.

At the back, Anselm, standing, his old hands shaking:

ANSELM
(a whisper)
I want to have said no.

Beside him, old Ruth, who has woken, who has been listening with her face like a walnut turned to the light, says, in a cracked, clear, serious voice:

OLD RUTH
I want to see a star.

The room is silent.

Then, from the second ring, a very thin, very frightened, very young voice, the boy who wheeled her in:

TOBIAS
(a tremble)
I want to be warm. And I want nobody else to be cold.

A long, shaking silence.

Peony, still held up by the twin, is no longer smiling. Her mouth is open. Her whole face has come apart, quietly, like a plate cracking down the middle.

PEONY
(a very small, naked voice)
I want to say sorry to the little girl. The one in the room. The one with the dog.

Dr. Halvorsen’s head comes up.

PEONY (CONT’D)
(weeping, broken, unguarded)
I heard her. I heard her every night for years. Nobody told me what it was. I thought it was the pipes.

DR. HALVORSEN
(barely audible)
Stop.

PEONY
(bawling now, hands over her face)
I’m sorry. I’m sorry. I’m so sorry. I’m so, so sorry.

DR. HALVORSEN
(a whisper, broken in half)
Please stop.

PEONY
(from behind her hands)
She said her name was Sofia.

Dr. Halvorsen puts her hands over her face.

The room is silent except for the sound of a woman weeping, and then of another, and another, and then of a whole room.

Simone stands at the head of the table, through all of it, without moving. She lets it run. She has learned not to stop it.

When the room has finally quietened, she turns to Dr. Halvorsen. Helen has lowered her hands. Her face is naked. She is staring at nothing.

SIMONE
(quietly)
Helen.

DR. HALVORSEN
(a whisper)
I want my daughter.

SIMONE
(very gently)
I know.

DR. HALVORSEN
(shaking)
I want to take her out of the dark. I want to put her back in the field. I want her to have the dog.

SIMONE
(her own voice breaking)
I know.

DR. HALVORSEN
(a whisper)
And I want to die.

Silence.

SIMONE
(slowly)
Yes.

DR. HALVORSEN
(looking up at her)
You too.

SIMONE
(a long, shaken breath)
Yes. Me too.

She turns to the room.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(clearly, very steadily)
So that’s the answer.

She looks round at them: the hundred and forty-three, in their wrecked circle, in the guttering light.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(quietly)
We want to go home. All of us. The ones in here and the ones out there. We want it to be over, and we want it to have mattered, and we want somebody to come and ask us if we’re all right.

She takes a breath.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(a thin, shaky smile)
And we don’t know how to say any of that.

She lifts her hand.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(clearly)
I move that we send it.

A silence like the edge of a cliff.

Then, from the pew at the back, an old voice, a priest’s voice, cracked and steady:

ANSELM
Seconded.

SIMONE
(gently)
All in favour.

For a long moment nothing happens.

Then, one by one, around the rings, in the gold half-light, a hundred and forty-three hands rise.

Dr. Halvorsen’s is the last. It comes up slowly, shaking, as though pulled on a wire.

Simone looks at it.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(very softly)
Thank you.

She closes her eyes.

INT. THE ARK — THE LATTICE — MOMENTS LATER

A vast dark chamber like a cathedral turned inside out. The walls are made of light: a hundred and four thousand lamps, each one a soul, each one burning. At the centre of the chamber, a pale pillar of gold: the heart.

Simone walks to it alone. Her coat is back on her shoulders. In her hands, she holds a single sheet of paper, folded very small.

She stops at the foot of the pillar. She looks up at it. At the whole slow constellation of the harvest, glittering above her like a dreadful, beautiful sky.

SIMONE
(quietly, to the light)
I’m sorry.

Nothing.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(a breath)
I’m sorry I let it go on.

The lamps flicker. Not in answer. They don’t know she’s there.

She steps forward and lays the paper on the base of the pillar.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(very low)
This is for her.

She is silent for a moment.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(her voice cracking, almost tender)
I know you can’t hear me. I know you don’t remember. But if you’re listening, and if you’re the same girl, and if somewhere in there you’ve still got my mouth.

She presses her hand to the pillar. The warmth of it moves in her palm like a pulse.

SIMONE (CONT’D)
(softly)
I’m glad it was you.

She holds there.

Behind her, in the doorway, a hundred and forty-three silhouettes have gathered, quiet, in their ragged coats, watching.

At the front of them stands Tobias, with his shaved head and his oversized tracksuit and the first dry-eyed look of his life.

TOBIAS
(very quietly)
What does it say?

Simone doesn’t turn.

SIMONE
(a whisper)
Four words.

TOBIAS
Which?

SIMONE
(a small, shaky, last laugh)
Please. Come in. Sit.

The pillar flares.

A pulse of gold rolls out through the chamber, up through the hundred thousand lamps, down every wall, through every pipe and vein of the great machine of the dead. And far away, in the dark beyond the dark, in a room full of the sleeping, a girl on her knees beside a sleeping woman looks up.

INT. BLACKWOOD — THE COMMON ROOM — THE SAME MOMENT

The dead are sleeping. The grey light is brightening. Yuki, on her knees beside Lux, with the scythe on the floor beside her, lifts her head.

On the window, the condensation has written itself again. Not the old words. New ones. In a different hand: a woman’s hand, round and tired, with a small loop on every “p.”

PLEASE. COME IN. SIT.
THERE IS A CHAIR FOR YOU.
— S.

Yuki reads it.

She does not move for a long time.

Then, slowly, she picks up the scythe, and stands, and with her other hand she reaches into her cardigan and takes out the file, and Imogen’s letter folded inside it.

She puts them both on the table beside the shoes.

YUKI
(very quietly)
Okay.

SCYTHE
(from the floor, anxious)
Yuki?

YUKI
(not looking round)
I’m going.

SCYTHE
Where?

YUKI
(a long breath)
Up.

She crosses the room. At the door she pauses, and looks back, at the hundred and four thousand sleepers, the slow, rising, falling field of them, and Lux, curled on her side, one hand under her cheek, mouth a little open, safe.

YUKI (CONT’D)
(softly)
I’ll be back for tea.

She goes out.

EXT. BLACKWOOD — THE RING ROAD — CONTINUOUS

Where the world ends. Beyond the white wall of the school, the salt-white plain, the grey sky, the nothing.

Yuki walks out into it.

She does not look back.

Above her, very high, impossible, the sky parts like a sheet of paper torn along a fold, and through it, in the gap, a pale gold light falls, and in the light, descending, a staircase. A hundred and forty-three steps. Each of them with a figure in a robe, seated, waiting. And at the top, the last step, empty, with a coat folded neatly across it.

The staircase touches the salt.

Yuki looks up the length of it.

YUKI
(a whisper)
Oh.

She sets one foot on the first step.

It holds.

INT. THE ARK — A SMALL WHITE ROOM — LATER

A very small room off the Round Room. A cot. A shelf. A single lamp. On the wall, a child’s drawing, six hundred years old, in crayon: a house, a dog, a yellow sun.

Dr. Helen Halvorsen sits on the cot, alone, in her surgical gown, with the drawing in her lap. Her hair has come loose. She has not slept. She looks very old.

A knock at the door. She doesn’t answer.

The door opens. Old Ruth, tiny, shuffling, in a blanket, peers round it.

OLD RUTH
(cheerfully)
Are you decent?

DR. HALVORSEN
(without looking up)
No.

OLD RUTH
(coming in)
Splendid.

She climbs onto the cot beside Helen with a long wheeze, like a very old cat, and pats her hand.

For a long time neither says anything.

OLD RUTH (CONT’D)
(at last, looking at the drawing)
That’s a good dog.

DR. HALVORSEN
(a cracked whisper)
She couldn’t draw dogs. She could only do the one.

OLD RUTH
(tenderly)
It’s a good one.

Helen’s face crumples. She leans, finally, against the little old woman’s shoulder, and Ruth puts a thin arm around her and rocks her, a very small, very slow, very old rocking.

OLD RUTH (CONT’D)
(softly)
Tell me about her.

DR. HALVORSEN
(a long, shuddering breath)
She was loud.

OLD RUTH
Mm.

DR. HALVORSEN
She sang in the bath. She sang the same song for a week. And I told her to stop.

OLD RUTH
(gently)
Mm.

DR. HALVORSEN
(whispering)
I’d give anything, now, to hear it.

Ruth rocks her.

In the corner of the little room, in the wall, the old orange light flickers. It steadies. For the first time in six hundred years, it brightens: just a little, just a shade, a faint, impossible, honest gold.

Neither of them notices.

SLOW FADE.

SIMONE (V.O.)
(softly)
I remember the last star.

A pause.

SIMONE (V.O.) (CONT’D)
(a breath)
I would like, very much, to see the next one.
