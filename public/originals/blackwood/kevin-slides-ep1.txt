TEASER

INT. MORTALITY SERVICES — MONDAY — 8:59 A.M.

The office is exactly as it has always been: fluorescent, damp, and one coffee short of a collapse.

The PRINTER coughs out a sheet nobody asked for. The sofa covered in dead people has been rotated ninety degrees, which the dead people have noticed and resent. A plant on Kevin’s desk has been apologised to so often it has started to look smug.

KEVIN sits at that desk. Thirty-one. Eleven pockets. A mug that says WORLD’S OKAYEST EMPLOYEE, which he bought for himself and has never once mentioned.

He is filing a form that is already filed. It is the third time this morning. He doesn’t notice. Nobody ever notices Kevin noticing.

ACT ONE

INT. A BEIGE VOID — NO TIME

White, then beige, then the particular grey of a day that has not yet decided to be a day. Kevin falls through it without making a sound, clutching the box.

He falls for a long time. Long enough to think. He thinks about the plant. He thinks about the tray. He thinks, with sudden, appalling clarity, that he has never told Yuki that her thermos is the only thing in the office that makes the place feel like a home.

The box is counting.

(eyeing Kevin)
Is that a Kevin?

VELVETTE
It’s a fucking Kevin.

SASHA
(sighing)
Another one. We’re running out of rooms.

KEVIN
(climbing out, politely)
Hello. I’m so sorry to impose.

SASHA
(not unkindly)
Don’t be sorry. Be quiet. There’s a bounty hunter in room six.

KEVIN
(whispering)
Is that bad?

SASHA
She’s a very good one.

GRIM REAPER
(from the passenger seat, reading the map)
What’s the room rate?

SASHA
One soul per night.

GRIM REAPER
(flatly)
That’s extortion.

SASHA
(shrugging)
It’s a motel.

INT. THE LAST STOP — LOBBY — NIGHT

A small room with orange carpet, a fish tank full of dead fish, and a front desk with a bell that says PLEASE DING FOR SERVICE OR DEATH.

A man sits behind it. He is a Kevin. His name tag says NIGHT CLERK. He is reading a dog-eared paperback with a skull on the cover.

He looks up.

NIGHT CLERK KEVIN
(deadpan)
Checking in?

KEVIN
(politely)
Yes, please. Two rooms. If that’s okay.

NIGHT CLERK KEVIN
(eyeing the box)
That’s a Parcel.

KEVIN
(protectively)
Yes.

NIGHT CLERK KEVIN
(sighing, closing his book)
You’re going to want the quiet room.

KEVIN
(carefully)
What’s the loud room?

NIGHT CLERK KEVIN
(handing over a key)
Room six.

KEVIN
(small)
Is the bounty hunter in there?

NIGHT CLERK KEVIN
(not looking up)
That’s her name on the door.

Kevin looks at the key. Taped to it is a small card.

ROOM 6 — “THE BEAST.”

KEVIN
(very small)
Oh no.

INT. THE LAST STOP — ROOM SIX — NIGHT

The loud room. A grubby motel room that smells of cigarettes, gun oil and perfume. Blackout curtains, a neon sign outside blinking BLOOD in pink.

On the bed, sitting cross-legged in a leather jacket, is a woman in her thirties with a cigarette between two fingers and an enormous revolver across her knees.

It is SUGAR RUSH. Not the cheerful bounty hunter from the Horsemen’s world. A grimmer, older version. Scar across one cheek. A pair of gummy-bear earrings that don’t seem funny any more.

She watches Kevin enter, box in his arms.

SUGAR RUSH
(flat)
Kevin.

KEVIN
(small)
Hi.

SUGAR RUSH
(not moving)
Close the door.

He closes it.

SUGAR RUSH (CONT’D)
Sit.

He sits on the edge of a chair.

SUGAR RUSH (CONT’D)
(looking at the box)
That’s a nice parcel.

KEVIN
(politely)
Thank you.

SUGAR RUSH
It’s worth two hundred million.

KEVIN
(quietly)
I heard.

SUGAR RUSH
(turning the revolver in her hand)
Do you know why people want it?

KEVIN
(honestly)
No.

SUGAR RUSH
Because it’s a way out.

KEVIN
(slowly)
Out of what?

SUGAR RUSH
(smiling, scarred and tired)
Out of being the person they were stuck as.

KEVIN
(small)
Everybody wants to leave.

SUGAR RUSH
(nodding)
Everyone’s a Kevin, in the end.

She lifts the revolver. She does not point it at him. She sets it on the bedside table, very gently, as if putting a child to sleep.

SUGAR RUSH (CONT’D)
(softly)
I’m going to let you walk out of this room.

KEVIN
(blinking)
Why?

SUGAR RUSH
Because you said please to a delivery.

KEVIN
(carefully)
Is that rare?

SUGAR RUSH
(dry)
It’s the rarest thing I’ve ever heard.

She stubs out her cigarette.

SUGAR RUSH (CONT’D)
Don’t make me regret it.

KEVIN
(standing)
I’ll try not to.

SUGAR RUSH
(not looking at him)
Everybody says that. It never helps.

00:03:12:44

INT. THE LAST STOP — QUIET ROOM — NIGHT

A tiny room with one bed, one lamp and one very poorly framed painting of a duck. Kevin sits on the bed, box in his lap. Outside, the neon sign blinks BLOOD in a slow, patient rhythm.

KEVIN
(to the box)
Why does everybody want to open you?

BOX
(softly)
Because they think I’m a door.

KEVIN
(carefully)
Are you?

BOX
(after a long silence)
I’m a letter.

KEVIN
(slowly)
A letter?

BOX
A letter addressed to someone who never opened it.

KEVIN
Who?

BOX
(very quietly)
I don’t remember.

KEVIN
(gently)
Do you remember who sent you?

BOX
(slowly)
I think… I was sent by someone who loved them very much.

KEVIN
(touching the cardboard)
That’s a nice thing to be.

BOX
(softly, almost shy)
It’s the only thing I’ve ever been.

There’s a knock on the door.

KEVIN
(whispering)
Who is it?

VOICE (O.S.)
It’s me.

He opens the door. The GRIM REAPER stands in the corridor, baseball cap in her hands, face tired and open in a way he has never seen on her.

GRIM REAPER
(quietly)
Can I sit down?

KEVIN
(stepping aside)
Of course.

She sits on the end of the bed. She stares at the painting of the duck.

GRIM REAPER
(after a long time)
Do you know what the worst thing about being Death is?

KEVIN
(carefully)
The paperwork?

GRIM REAPER
(shaking her head, smiling faintly)
Nobody ever sends you a letter.

KEVIN
(quietly)
Oh.

GRIM REAPER
(looking at the box)
Not one. In four thousand years. Not a birthday card. Not a postcard. Not a thank-you note from anybody I ever reaped.

KEVIN
(carefully)
That doesn’t sound very fair.

GRIM REAPER
(shrugging)
It’s the job.

KEVIN
(very gently)
Is that why you want the Parcel?

GRIM REAPER
(shaking her head)
No. I want to know who sent it.

KEVIN
(small)
Why?

GRIM REAPER
(looking at him at last)
Because it’s got my name on it.

KEVIN
(carefully)
Where?

GRIM REAPER
(quietly)
On the underside. Look.

Kevin turns the box over. On the underside, in faded, feminine handwriting, an address:

TO: THE GRIM REAPER
MORTALITY SERVICES, MAIN OFFICE, 1347.

FROM: MOTHER.

KEVIN
(staring)
It’s from your mother.

GRIM REAPER
(a long, ragged exhale)
I know.

KEVIN
(very softly)
Why didn’t you open it?

Because it’s been in the post since 1347.

That’s the longest delay in the history of the post.

GRIM REAPER
(dryly)
It was a plague year.

Silence. Outside, the neon sign blinks BLOOD, BLOOD, BLOOD.

KEVIN
(carefully)
What do you think is inside?

GRIM REAPER
(quietly)
I think it’s a casserole.

KEVIN
(horrified)
It’s been in the post for seven hundred years.

GRIM REAPER
(deadpan)
It will be very good.

Kevin starts laughing. He can’t help it. It comes out of him in a small, helpless wheeze. The Reaper looks at him, a little affronted, and then, against all odds, she starts to laugh too.

They laugh until they cry, in a motel room with a duck painting and a box that has been waiting half a millennium to be opened.

When it passes, the Reaper wipes her eyes with the back of one bony hand.

GRIM REAPER (CONT’D)
(softly)
She hated it when I was late.

KEVIN
(gently)
It isn’t late if somebody delivers it.

GRIM REAPER
(looking at him)
That’s the first nice thing a Kevin has ever said to me.

KEVIN
(smiling)
I’m very average. I get a lot of practice.

The box hums, softly, under his hands.

BOX
(very quietly)
Thank you.

KEVIN
(touching it)
You’re welcome.
