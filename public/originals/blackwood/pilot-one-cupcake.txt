EXT. PUBLIC PARK – AFTERNOON – ELEVEN YEARS AGO 
Rain. Not a storm. The thin, mean kind that soaks you slowly and never apologizes. 
A cheap municipal playground. Wood chips gone black with wet. A tube slide, a rusted swing set, a sandbox nobody has used since a raccoon died in it. Parents stand under umbrellas at the edge of the lot, calling their kids in. 
Under the plastic slide sits MADISON, nine. Auburn hair. A cheap pink ribbon tied in a lopsided bow. Knees pulled up to her chin. A raincoat that is one size too small because nobody noticed she grew. 
On her lap: a crushed plastic deli container. Inside, one homemade cupcake. The frosting has slid off to one side like it tried to escape. 
Nobody has taken a bite. 
By the swings, three GIRLS trade beaded bracelets, holding their wrists up to compare. Pink beads. Glitter beads. Letter beads that spell names. 
Madison looks down at her own wrist. A plain piece of white string. A knot she tied herself. 
She takes a breath. Crawls out from under the slide. Holds the container up with both hands like an offering at an altar. 
MADISON: Do you want a cupcake? I made it. Mostly. The oven helped. The girls look at the frosting. The frosting looks back. 
GIRL 1: It looks gross. 
MADISON: It tastes better than it looks. 
GIRL 2: My mom says don't take food from weird kids. 
GIRL 3: (not unkindly, which is worse) Is that your real ribbon?

EXT. PUBLIC PARK – STREET – LATER 
A black van with no plates rolls to the curb without headlights. The engine is silent. The tires are silent. The rain doesn't land on it. 
Three FIGURES in matte-black hazard suits step out. Faceless visors. One carries a case that hums. On their shoulders, a small stitched logo: a gray headstone with a keyhole in it. 
TOMBSTONE. 
They don't go to the slide. They don't look at the sleeping child. They kneel in the wood chips where the light was brightest and scrape. Glittering white residue, fine as powdered sugar, comes up off the ground in their trowels and glows in the vials.
