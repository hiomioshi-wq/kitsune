Ambrose Vell arrived on the seventh of November, as promised, in a gray coat and good boots, carrying a leather case and a theodolite in a wooden box. He came down the hill from the coach stop with the careful walk of a man who did not trust wet cobbles, and he stopped at the end of the pier to look at the bay.

Iona watched him from the office window. He was younger than she had expected, perhaps forty, with the tired, tidy look of a man who had spent his life being reasonable in rooms that were not. He looked at the water for a long time. Then he looked at the Almanac through the window, and then at Iona, and he inclined his head as though they had already been introduced and had agreed to disagree.

"Miss Fenn," he said, when she opened the door. "I'm sorry for your loss. The Office sent a note when we heard about Mrs. Quill. She was a correspondent of my predecessor's, though they never met."

"You've read her file."

"I've read the file," Vell agreed. "It's thin. That's rather the point of my visit."

He came in and put his case on the table and his coat on the back of the chair, and he did not look at the Almanac again until he had made himself a cup of tea, which he did with the air of a man performing a courtesy for a room he did not expect to like. Then he sat down opposite the great book and opened his case and took out a folder of tables.

"May I?" he asked.

"Will it matter if I say no?"

"Not legally," said Vell. "But I'd rather ask."

She let him look. He turned the pages slowly, with clean fingers. He read the entries for October as though he were reading a novel he had been told was overrated. Once he stopped and went back and read a page again. Once he made a small noise that might have been interest or might have been indigestion.

"This is very detailed," he said. "And very irregular. The Office has records of Cradle Bay going back to 1782, when the surveying service first mapped the coast. The tides here have never matched the lunar tables. Not once in two hundred and forty years."

"Perhaps the tables are wrong for this bay."

"Perhaps," said Vell, in a tone that meant *no.* He turned the Almanac around so the pages faced her. "Look at this. The fifteenth of October. You have a high tide at ten past four in the afternoon and another at a quarter to eleven at night. The lunar table says one high tide, at half past five. You are two hours early, and then you have an extra tide."

"The bay has an extra tide," said Iona.

"The bay cannot have an extra tide. The moon has one pull. The water has one response. That is not a matter of local custom. That is physics."

"The water here does what the book says."

"I'm sure it seems that way." Vell opened his folder. "I'm going to set up my instruments on the pier. I'd like to take readings for a week, and I'd like you to write nothing in the Almanac for that week. Not one word. If the bay's tides are as you say, they will continue without your book, and the Office will revise its assessment. If they don't, we'll know that what we have here is a very old and very beautiful habit, and we can discuss how to bring it into line with the coast."

Iona wanted to refuse. But she was nineteen and had been the Tidewriter for thirty-seven days, and she did not have Marra's coat or Marra's certainty. So she said, "One week."

Vell nodded. He did not gloat. He went out onto the pier in the rain and set up his theodolite and his tide gauge and a small brass instrument that he called a resonating float, which sounded to Iona like a thing invented by a man who had never been seasick. He wrote numbers in a notebook with a pencil that he sharpened twice a day.

For six days, Iona wrote nothing.

The first day was fine. The sea kept to the last entries she had made, moving with the old confidence of a story that had already been told. The second day was fine. On the third day, the heron who fished the shallows came and stood outside the office window and looked at her for a long time, and then flew away.

On the fourth day, the tea in her cup trembled at an hour when there should have been no tide.

It was small at first. The water in the harbor lifted a finger's width and set down again, as though testing a step. Then it lifted again, higher, and the dories strained at their ropes. The old men left their card game. Sedge came down the pier with her coat over her nightgown, which she never did, and asked Iona what was happening.

"I don't know," said Iona.

"Then ask."

"I can't. I promised him a week."

Sedge looked at Vell, who was out on the pier in the dark in his good boots, writing in his notebook by the light of a lantern. "He is a very tidy man," Sedge said, "to be making such a mess of the water."

On the fifth day the sea came in at the wrong hour and stayed too long. It filled the fish market to the third row of tiles. It lifted a crate of bottles and set it down, unbroken, in the middle of the harbor master's office. It stranded a ferry from Moonwake Bay on the sandbar for six hours, and the passengers sat on the deck and ate their sandwiches and watched the sun go down and then up again. The ferryman, who kept a ledger of every crossing and was not a man to complain, wrote one line in it and showed it to Iona when they finally floated free.

*Crossing delayed by argument.*

That night, Iona went to find Vell. He was on the pier, hood up, writing. The theodolite pointed at the water like a cannon. His notebook was full of columns. His pencil had worn to a stub.

"Look at it," she said.

He looked. The sea was doing something that was not in any table: it was coming in in little pulses, like a heartbeat, each pulse a little higher than the last. The water around the pier posts had begun to turn, very slowly, in a circle.

"It's trying to read your instruments," Iona said. "That's what Marra would have said. It's looking for something to follow. It always follows the thing that is paying attention."

"That is a metaphor," said Vell.

"Is it?"

He did not answer. He watched the water for a long time. Then he closed his notebook.

"Miss Fenn," he said. "What would you write, if I let you write tonight?"

"I'd write a tide that goes out before the next pulse."

"Then write it."

The words surprised them both. Vell looked as though he had bitten into something with an unexpected filling. He put his pencil in his pocket and held the lantern while Iona wrote, there on the pier, in a little notebook she kept for drafts: *Eleven o'clock: the water goes out, all the way, and stays out until the morning. It is not angry. It is being asked.*

She read it aloud over the water.

The pulses stopped. The sea held its breath, and then it went out, all the way, the way she had written, and stayed out, and the dark harbor floor lay open under the stars.

Vell let out a breath. "That's not possible," he said, without conviction.

"No," Iona agreed. "But it's what happened."

They stood together in the cold for a while. The lantern hissed. Somewhere out in the dark a gull complained.

"I have to file a report," Vell said at last. "I have to say something. The Act doesn't have a category for a bay that does what it's told by a book."

"Then write a new category."

He laughed, one short surprised note, and then looked embarrassed by it. "The Office doesn't much like new categories."

"The Office doesn't live here."

Vell was quiet. Then he said, "Show me the margin."

She showed him. He read Marra's notes by lantern light, all the way back to October, and some of them twice. He read the line about the heron knowing before the book does, and the line about the fourth of June, and the line about asking properly. When he was done he closed the Almanac gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child.

"My predecessor," he said, "wrote a note in her own file, before she retired. She said there were places on this coast where the water had been *made local* by long attention, and that the Office ought to leave them alone. The note was not adopted. It was not even minuted." He tapped his folder. "I have carried it around for six years and never once had cause to remember it."

"And now?"

"Now I have cause." He looked at the sea. "I can give you a provisional report. Traditional practice, locally administered, subject to review at the year's end. It will keep the Office off your pier until December. After that, I can't promise."

"December is when the new Almanac is written."

"I know," said Vell. "That's why I said it."

He packed his instruments the next morning. Before he left, he did something unexpected: he gave Iona his pencil.

"For the margins," he said. "It's a good pencil. It doesn't lie."

She watched him go up the hill in his gray coat and his good boots, with the theodolite on his shoulder like a rifle he had decided not to use. At the top of the hill he turned and raised a hand, and then he was gone.

That evening Iona opened the Almanac to the last page, the page for the thirty-first of December, which Marra had left blank every year until the writing was done. There was a note pinned to it that Iona had never seen, in Marra's hand, older than the others.

*The last tide is the asking. Write it with the town.*

Iona read it four times. Then she went to find Sedge, and the priest, and the fishmonger, and the old men who played cards, and the ferryman from Moonwake Bay, who was still waiting for his passengers to finish their sandwiches.

She had six weeks to teach them how to read.
