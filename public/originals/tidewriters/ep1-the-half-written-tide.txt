In Cradle Bay the sea does not follow the moon. It follows the book.

The book lives at the end of the harbor master's pier, in a room with one window and a stove that is never lit, on a desk built from the ribs of a wrecked schooner. It is bound in tarred canvas and its pages are the size of a door. It is called the Almanac, though it is not an almanac in the way that farmers keep almanacs, full of moon phases and saints' days and guesses about the frost. The Almanac is a schedule. Every year, on the last night of December, the Tidewriter of Cradle Bay sits down with a pot of ink made from storm-water and soot and writes down what the sea will do for the next three hundred and sixty-five days.

The sea reads it. Then the sea does it.

The first entry is always the same. HIGH AT SIX AND FORTY MINUTES. The last entry is always the same. LOW AT THE TURNING. Between them, the writing is the Tidewriter's own, and no two years are alike.

Marra Quill had been the Tidewriter for fifty-one years. She was eighty-one on the day this story starts, and she had written the Almanac for every one of those years but the first three, when she was an apprentice and her teacher wrote it and let her hold the pen for the tides that fell on Sundays. She wore a oilskin coat indoors. She could tell you the height of the water at any hour of any day by the ache in her left knee. She had never once been wrong about the sea, though she had sometimes been wrong about people, which she said were different things and only one of them mattered in the long run.

Her apprentice was called Iona Fenn. Iona was nineteen, from the upland side of the bay, where the family grew flax and did not go down to the water if it could be helped. She had been sent to the Tidewriter's office the way some girls are sent to a convent: because she was good at figures, poor at conversation, and unlikely to be missed. She had been there two years. She could copy a tide table faster than anyone alive. She could calculate the height of a spring tide to the width of a hair. What she could not do, no matter how patiently Marra tried to teach her, was stand at the end of the pier and feel the sea decide.

"That is because you are asking your arithmetic instead of asking the water," Marra said.

"How do I ask the water?"

"You don't," said Marra. "You stand there until the water asks you."

Iona thought this was the kind of thing old people said to avoid explaining things.

On the first morning of October, Iona came down the hill with the post and found the office door open. That was not unusual. Marra came and went as she pleased and left the door open for the gulls. What was unusual was the silence. The stove ticked. The gulls did not.

Marra was at the desk, slumped forward over the Almanac, with her pen still in her hand. She had died some time in the night, the doctor said later, and he said it kindly, as though dying in the night were an achievement. There was no pain in her face. There was only the small frown of concentration she wore while writing, the one Iona had teased her about, the one that made her look like a woman working out a difficult sum in her head.

The pen had stopped on the first of October. Marra had written, in her narrow upright hand:

HIGH AT SIX AND FORTY-THREE. WIND OFF THE

That was all. The rest of the line was white.

Iona stood in the doorway for a long time. She had the post under one arm and the smell of rain in her nose and the cold, clear knowledge that the year had three months left in it, and that the woman who had been keeping it had stopped.

She did the sensible things. She sent for the doctor and the priest and the harbor master. She covered the stove. She made tea for four people and drank none of it. When the harbor master, a broad woman named Sedge who had been at school with Iona's mother, asked who was to finish the Almanac, Iona said, "I am."

Sedge looked at her the way she looked at a boat with a patched sail. "Can you?"

"I have the tables," said Iona. "I have the last fifty years. I have the method."

Sedge was quiet. Outside, the bay made the sound it always made, which was not a pleasant sound and not an unpleasant one, but a sound like a large animal breathing in its sleep. "The method," Sedge said at last, "is not the same as the woman."

Iona finished the tea and went back to the desk.

She worked until dark, copying the pattern of the previous October, adjusting for the year's drift the way Marra had taught her. The entries were not creative. Highs and lows, ebb and flood, the long slow shoulder of the neap. She wrote with Marra's second-best pen because she could not bring herself to use the first. By midnight she had covered the first week of October. By dawn she had done the first fortnight. She was tired and her hand ached and the sea, outside the window, was doing exactly what the book said it would do.

At six and forty-three minutes, the water reached the third step of the pier. Just as written.

Iona felt a fierce, guilty pride. She slept for two hours on the floor and woke with the taste of ink in her mouth.

The sea came in wrong at noon.

She heard it before she saw it: a sound like a crowd, a hiss and a rush and then a slap of water against stone that was much too high up the harbor wall. She ran out onto the pier. The bay was rising past the fish market, past the coil of rope that marked the safe line, past the place where the old men played cards in the afternoons. Gulls went up in a white panic. A dory broke its mooring and spun out into the channel. The water climbed the steps of the harbor master's office and stopped one step below the door, trembling, as though it were deciding whether to knock.

Then, as suddenly as it had come, it went out.

It went out further than it had any right to. The harbor floor showed: green weed, black stones, a rusted anchor, the bones of a pram. And along the wet line where the water had stopped, in a stripe of kelp and shell and dark sand, something was written.

Iona knew tide-script. Every apprentice learned it. It was not a language exactly; it was a way of laying things down so that the water would carry the shape of them. You saw it sometimes on beaches after a storm, or in the grain of a driftwood board. The old Tidewriters used it to leave messages for one another. The shape in the kelp was six letters long.

WHO IS WRITING?

Iona read it three times before she believed it. Then she sat down on the wet stone of the pier and put her head in her hands.

The town gathered behind her. Nobody spoke for a while. Sedge came and stood at the edge with her arms folded, looking at the letters the way she looked at a bill she did not intend to pay.

"Well," Sedge said at last. "It's asking."

"It's never asked before," said the fishmonger.

"It's never had a reason to before," said Sedge.

Iona got up. Her hands were shaking. She went back into the office and opened the Almanac to the page for the second of October and looked at what she had written there, and then at the entry for the first, where Marra's pen had stopped.

HIGH AT SIX AND FORTY-THREE. WIND OFF THE

She turned the page. In the margin, in Marra's hand, there was a note she had not noticed before. It was small and half-hidden by the binding, and it had been there so long the ink had gone brown.

*The sea does not want to be right. It wants to be asked.*

Iona sat with that for a long time. Then she went and found the tide-tables, and the last fifty years of Almanacs, and she began to read them the way she had never read them before: not for the numbers, but for the notes in the margins.

Marra had left hundreds of them. Some were practical. *Eelgrass wants a slower flood here.* Some were cryptic. *The heron knows before the book does.* Some were almost tender. *Leave the fourth of June high. The children swim.*

And in nearly every year, beside the great autumn tide, the same line, in slightly different words:

*Ask it. Ask it properly.*

Iona had always assumed this was a note to herself, a reminder of some technical step. Now she looked at the sea, which had gone quiet and was waiting, and understood that it was a note to whoever came next.

She took a fresh sheet of paper and wrote out the tide for the second of October. Then, below the numbers, in the margin, she wrote:

*I am writing. I am sorry it is not her. I will do my best, and where I am not sure, I will say so.*

She did not know if that was allowed. Nobody had told her the margins were for anything but notes.

She carried the page down to the water at the hour of the low. The tide was out. The harbor floor smelled of weed and old iron. She stood at the edge, feeling foolish, and read the numbers aloud. Her voice sounded thin in the wide gray morning.

Then she folded the page and put it in a bottle and threw it as far as she could out into the bay.

The bottle bobbed. The water took it, turned it over once, and drew it under without a sound.

For a moment nothing happened. Then the sea began to come in. Not the way it had come at noon, in a rush, but slowly, the way it was supposed to, the way it had come for fifty-one years under Marra's hand. It reached the third step. It stopped. It held there, trembling, and then it went out again, and the wet line it left behind was clean and unlettered.

Iona let out a breath she had not known she was holding.

She wrote the rest of October that afternoon. In the margins she wrote the truth: where she was sure, and where she was guessing, and once, on the fifteenth, *I don't know why the water wants this, but I think it does.* The sea kept to the book. It was not perfect. On the nineteenth it came in a hand's width higher than she had written, and she found a note in the margin of the old Almanac that said *the sea rounds up in October,* and she wrote that down for next year.

The town began to breathe again. Sedge stopped standing at the end of the pier with her arms folded. The fishmonger brought Iona a mackerel, which she did not know how to cook and gave to the priest. The old men came back to their card game on the fish market steps, a little higher up than before.

On the last day of October, a letter arrived. It was addressed to *The Tidewriter, Cradle Bay,* in a hand that was not from the bay, and it had been posted from the capital. Iona opened it at the desk with the gulls arguing outside the window.

It was short. It said that the Office of Coasts had received reports of an anomalous tidal regime in Cradle Bay, and that an inspector would arrive on the seventh of November to conduct an assessment. It said that the Tidewriter was requested to make the Almanac available for inspection. It said that the Office was empowered, under the Coastal Uniformity Act, to impose a standard tidal schedule where local practice was found to be irregular.

It was signed, with a firm and entirely unremarkable hand:

*Ambrose Vell, Inspector of Coasts.*

Iona read it twice. Then she looked out the window at the bay, which was doing what the book said it would do, and had been doing it, more or less, since before her grandmother was born.

"Anomalous," she said, to the gulls.

The gulls did not offer an opinion.

She put the letter under the corner of the Almanac, where Marra used to keep the tide-tables, and went back to the page for the first of November. She wrote the numbers carefully. In the margin, she wrote:

*The Office of Coasts is coming. I do not know what to tell them. I do not think they will believe the book.*

Then she sat back and waited, as Marra had taught her, for the water to answer.

It did not answer that day, or the next. But on the third of November, when she walked down to the pier at dawn, there was a new line in the wet sand at the foot of the steps, written in the tide-script she had come to know as well as her own name.

It said: *TELL THEM THE TRUTH.*

And underneath, smaller, in a hand that was almost like Marra's:

*AND BRING A COAT. HE WILL BE TIRESOME.*
