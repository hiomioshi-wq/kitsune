They met in the harbor master's office on the first of December, because it was the biggest room in the bay that did not smell of fish.

The cards were put away. There were twenty-three people, which was most of Cradle Bay in winter and all of Cradle Bay that mattered. Sedge stood at the head of the table with her arms folded. The priest sat at the corner with his hands folded, looking as though he had been asked to bless a boat and had reservations about the boat. The fishmonger had brought a plate of something. The old men had brought their opinions. The ferryman from Moonwake Bay had brought his ledger, because he said it was the only book he trusted and he wanted it nearby in case anything needed proving.

Iona stood at the foot of the table with the Almanac open on the desk in front of her.

"Six weeks ago the sea asked who was writing," she said. "You all saw it. In October it came in wrong because I guessed, and it went out wrong because I stopped. The Office of Coasts sent an inspector, and he was a decent man, and he left me his pencil and a provisional report that runs out at the end of the month." She looked around the room. "We can't keep relying on a single person being right. Marra could do it because Marra was extraordinary. I am not extraordinary. I am good at sums and I am learning the rest."

Sedge shifted. "What are you saying?"

"I'm saying the last tide is the asking. That's what she wrote. Not the last number. The asking. I don't think one tidewriter writes it anymore. I think the town has to."

The room was quiet. The stove ticked. The fishmonger put down his plate.

"Read the old ones," Iona said. She turned the great book around and showed them the margins. "Look. Her notes aren't just about water. *Leave the fourth of June high. The children swim.* *The heron knows before the book does.* She was writing a tide that the bay could live in. Not the sea's tide. The bay's."

"So what do we do?" said one of the old men.

"We learn to read it," said Iona. "And then, at the turning of the year, we read the last tide aloud, all of us, and we say what we are asking for. Not just the water. The year."

Sedge looked at her for a long time. "You want the town to become the Tidewriter."

"I want the town to be asked," said Iona.

They argued until the fish got cold. Some of them liked the idea. Some of them did not want a mystery they could not control, even if the mystery had been good to them for two hundred years. The priest said the sea should never be spoken to except in a storm. The ferryman said he had seen stranger things at Moonwake Bay and had written them all down. The fishmonger, who had been quiet, said that his grandmother had told him that the first Tidewriter had not been a magician at all, but a woman who had asked the sea a favor in a bad winter, and that the favor had been renewed every year since by the town being grateful.

This was the first Iona had heard of it as a favor. She went back to the Almanac that night and read the very first pages, the ones bound closest to the spine, which were so old they had no entries left, only a note in a hand so faded it looked like a watermark.

*In the year of the long frost, Fenn of the bay asked the water to stay. The water asked what it would get. Fenn said: a book, and a reading, and a town that remembers. The water said: a year at a time. They have kept it.*

Iona read it twice. Then she began to laugh, and then she put her head down on the desk and cried a little, which she had not done since October, and which she had needed to do.

The Almanac was not a cage. It was a promise. It had always been a promise. The town had been renewing it every year without knowing, by living on the bay and remembering and being grateful, and the sea had been keeping it every year without being thanked. The first Tidewriter had not made the sea obey her. She had asked it to stay because the bay would starve otherwise, and the sea had said yes, for a year at a time, and the years had added up to two hundred and forty.

The last tide of the year was not the closing of a door. It was the signing of a lease.

On the last night of December, the town came down to the harbor with lanterns. It was cold and clear and the stars were out over the water. Vell had come back three days earlier, because the year's end was the year's end, and he stood at the edge of the crowd with his theodolite in its box and his hands in his pockets. Sedge stood at the head of the pier with her arms folded, but she had brought a candle for the priest's lantern, which he took as a sign.

Iona stood at the foot of the steps with the Almanac open to the last page. The page was blank except for the line Marra had written every year in the margin: *Low at the turning.*

She read out what she had written underneath.

"At the turning of the year, at the last low tide, the water comes in one last time. Not to flood. Not to claim. To ask. The town will be here. The town will answer. The sea may stay for another year, or go, or come back. We will not stop it. We will remember it."

Then she said, "That's what I've written. But the asking is yours. I'll read the numbers. You say the rest."

She read the numbers. The old men read the hours. The fishmonger read the heights. Sedge read the wind, which was off the land and cold. The priest read the prayer, which was not in the Almanac but which Iona had decided was allowed. The ferryman read a line from his ledger: *All crossings remembered. All passengers safe.* The children read their names, because they had asked to.

Then they all said, together, the way they had practiced in the town hall for three weeks, some of them stumbling, some of them laughing, some of them with their eyes shut:

"We are here. We remember. Stay, or go, but come back."

The sea was low and quiet. For a long moment nothing happened. The lanterns hissed. The stars hung.

Then the water began to come in.

It came in slowly, quietly, without the boom and rush the town was used to. It climbed the steps and paused at the first one, the way a visitor pauses at a door. It did not stop at the third step, as the Almanac said. It came up to the fifth, and the sixth, and then it lay along the pier in a long soft line, and it stayed there while the whole town watched, and nobody was afraid.

When it went out, it left a high-water mark of shells along the edge of the harbor square, a line so neat and so high that the fishmonger said it would take him a week to sweep. And in the wet sand at the foot of the pier, in the old tide-script, there were four words.

*SAME TIME NEXT YEAR.*

Vell was the first to move. He walked out onto the pier with his theodolite still in its box. He looked at the words in the sand for a long time. Then he took out his notebook and wrote four lines and closed it and put it away. Iona, standing near him, saw what he had written.

*Not anomalous. Local. Asked. Recommend continuation.*

"Provisional report?" she said.

"Final report," said Vell. "It will take the Office three years to disagree with me. By then I intend to be retired, and you will have a new category. Possibly two."

Sedge came down the pier with her candle. She looked at the words in the sand, and then at the shells, and then at Iona.

"The harbor master's ledger," Sedge said, "has said for a hundred and eighty years that the bay is irregular. I have kept that ledger since I was thirty. I have never once written in it that the irregularity was the point."

"There's still time," said Iona.

Sedge snorted. "Write it yourself. My hand's cold."

The town went home in the dark with their lanterns, and the water stayed out, and the shells lay along the square like a line of small white promises. Iona went back to the office and opened the Almanac to the first page of the new year, which was blank and waiting.

She wrote the first entry the next morning, the way it had always been written: *High at six and forty minutes.*

Then she sat back and looked at the margin.

In the old Almanac, Marra's notes had filled it. Now the margin was empty. Iona took out Vell's pencil and wrote, in the same place where Marra had written *The sea does not want to be right. It wants to be asked.*:

*We are here. We remember. Stay, or go, but come back.*

She did not know if the sea would answer. She did not know if the Office would let them keep their irregularity, or if the town would keep its promise once the winter was over and the summer people came and the fish were plentiful and the story began to sound like a story. She only knew what Marra had known and what the first Fenn had known: that a promise is not a cage, and a bay is not a place, and the water outside the window was not a thing to be measured but a thing to be asked.

That afternoon the sea came in at six and forty minutes.

And in the wet sand at the foot of the pier, very small, there was a new line: *THANK YOU FOR ASKING.*
